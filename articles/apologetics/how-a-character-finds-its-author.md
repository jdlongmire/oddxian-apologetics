## The Category Mistake Behind “There Is No Evidence for God”

“There is no evidence for God.” The statement sounds like an empirical conclusion, a sober assessment of the data. But before accepting it, we must ask a more fundamental question: What kind of evidence should an observer inside a created reality expect to find for a Creator who transcends that reality?

Imagine a conscious character inside a video game investigating his world. He studies matter, discovers mathematical regularities, and builds increasingly sophisticated instruments. Eventually, he searches every accessible region of his universe for the programmer. He finds objects, forces, fields, other characters, and stable laws, but no programmer. He concludes, “There is no evidence for a programmer. Everywhere I look, I find only things inside the game.”

The mistake is clear. The programmer was never proposed as another object rendered inside the game.

The same point becomes clearer with a book. Suppose Sherlock Holmes searches Baker Street, questions Watson, and investigates all of London for Arthur Conan Doyle. He finds no Doyle. Has Holmes shown that Doyle does not exist? Of course not. Doyle is not another character hiding somewhere in Victorian London. Holmes, London, and the story itself stand in a different explanatory relationship to their author.

The analogy does not establish that our universe is literally a game, a book, or even an artifact. It establishes a category distinction. If God is proposed as the transcendent Creator of physical reality, demanding that He appear as another physical object within that reality misunderstands the claim. God is not proposed as a missing constituent of the universe. He is proposed as the ground of the universe and everything within it.

Nor does this settle every form of atheistic objection. A thoughtful atheist may agree that God would not be a physical object and still judge the indirect evidence inadequate. That is a substantive position. But it is importantly different from saying there is no evidence at all.

This essay therefore makes a limited but consequential argument. If creation can bear inferential evidence of its transcendent ground, if claimed interventions can function as intentional signs, and if revelation and incarnation can be investigated historically, then “there is no evidence” cannot stand as a blanket dismissal. The remaining questions concern whether the evidence is genuine, what it supports, and whether it is sufficient.

## How Access Could Occur

If the character cannot leave his world, how could he know anything about its designer?

He could infer something about the designer from the system itself. The designer could intervene. The designer could communicate. The designer could make himself personally accessible within the system.

That gives us a progression:

**Creation → Intervention → Revelation → Incarnation**

From the observer’s side:

**Inference → Sign → Communication → Encounter**

This is where Romans 1 becomes remarkably relevant. Paul writes:

> “For what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them. For his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made.” (Romans 1:19–20, ESV)

The structure is explicit. The attributes are invisible. The things through which they are perceived are visible. The proposed referent transcends creation while the evidence appears within creation. The creature does not escape the world to inspect God. According to Paul, something about God is perceived through what He has made.

## The Architecture We Encounter

Triadic Reality Theory develops a larger account of reality in terms of three mutually implicated dimensions: logic, information, and action. What follows is a compressed application of that architecture, not its full derivation.

Reality exhibits stable rational structure. Mathematical relationships hold. Regularities persist sufficiently for experimentation, prediction, engineering, and rational inquiry. Science depends upon a real correspondence between rational minds and an intelligible world. Call this **L: Logic**.

Reality also contains distinguishable and consequential states. Initial conditions matter. Boundary conditions matter. Relationships between states matter. In living systems, transcription, translation, regulation, signaling, and replication display extraordinary information-processing architecture. Call this **I: Information**.

Reality is also actual and dynamic. States change. Causes produce effects. Particles interact, stars ignite, chemistry proceeds, cells metabolize, organisms behave, and human beings reason and choose. Call this **𝒜: Action**.

In compressed form:

\[
\chi \equiv \mathcal{A}(I \mid L)
\]

Actuality unfolds through action upon informationally differentiated states under logical constraint. This is not presented here as a complete physical theory or as a proof of God. It is a compact ontological description of the reality we encounter: logically ordered, informationally differentiated, and dynamically actual.

Then persons appear within this reality. We recognize logical relations, interpret information semantically, form intentions, assign meaning, and act deliberately. These facts are not self-interpreting, but they are evidentially relevant. They belong among the things a worldview must explain.

The naturalist may propose that logic, information, causal actuality, consciousness, semantic content, and intentionality are brute features, emergent properties, or consequences of some deeper impersonal structure. Those are possible proposals, but calling them “natural” does not discharge their explanatory burden. The Christian proposes instead that rational, informational, action-capable reality proceeds ultimately from rational agency.

The inference is abductive. We compare explanatory frameworks and ask under which one these features are more expected.

## Then John Says: Logos

John begins:

> “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” (John 1:1, ESV)

He continues:

> “All things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made.” (John 1:3, ESV)

John identifies the source of creation as the **Logos**. Romans says that something concerning the Creator is perceptible through creation. John identifies creation’s source as rational and personal. When we then encounter a universe characterized by rational constraint, informational differentiation, causal actuality, and persons capable of knowing all three, the correspondence deserves investigation.

This correspondence alone does not move in one step from an intelligible universe to every claim of Christianity. It does, however, show that Christianity possesses explanatory categories directly relevant to the kind of universe we encounter. Within the Christian account, the rational intelligibility of reality has rational ground. Semantic content does not arise from an ultimate absence of meaning. Causal actuality does not emerge from an ultimate absence of agency.

Genesis adds another part of the Christian explanation:

> “Then God said, ‘Let us make man in our image, after our likeness.’” (Genesis 1:26, ESV)

The correspondence between mind and world is therefore not surprising within the biblical framework. The world originates from rational agency, and human beings are derivative rational agents made in the image of that same source.

## Where Explanation Ends

Every worldview eventually reaches explanatory bedrock. The important question is not whether explanation terminates, but where it terminates and whether the proposed terminus can carry the required explanatory weight.

A brute fact is something for which no further explanation is available or required. But we should distinguish two claims:

1. We do not currently possess a deeper explanation.
2. No deeper explanation exists.

The first is ordinary intellectual humility. The second is a metaphysical judgment. It is not established merely because scientific inquiry has reached its present boundary.

Christianity does not simply place one more unexplained object at the end of a causal chain. Classical Christian theism identifies God as necessary rather than contingent, *Ipsum Esse Subsistens*, Subsistent Being Itself. God does not receive existence from another. He is proposed as the source upon which contingent being depends.

Naming God this way is not by itself a demonstration. The coherence and necessity of that account require philosophical argument. Yet the proposal is importantly different from saying that the universe, its laws, and its initial conditions simply happen to exist without rational ground. The comparison is between rival explanatory termini: necessary, rational, personal Being on one side, and some ultimately impersonal fact, law, structure, or state on the other.

Science cannot decide that metaphysical question merely by describing the internal regularities of nature. Its history does, however, teach caution about premature closure. Many apparent stopping points have yielded to deeper explanation. That history does not guarantee that every question has a scientifically accessible answer, but neither does a current boundary establish that ultimate explanation is impossible.

## Description Is Not Ultimate Explanation

Methodological naturalism is enormously productive for studying ordinary processes within nature. It asks investigators to seek regular causal relations within the system. Trouble begins when that working restriction is converted into an ontological conclusion without additional argument.

Suppose our game investigator adopts the rule that only causes represented within game physics may be considered. He discovers increasingly detailed internal explanations and then announces that nothing exists outside the game. His method has demonstrated no such thing. It excluded external agency from consideration before the investigation began.

Likewise, a scientific account of how one physical state follows another does not, merely by becoming more complete, establish that physical reality explains its own existence, intelligibility, or actuality. Description of the workmanship and explanation of the Worker are different questions.

This does not prove metaphysical naturalism false. Naturalists offer further arguments concerning causal closure, parsimony, divine hiddenness, evil, and the reliability of religious testimony. Those arguments deserve direct engagement. The narrower point is that methodological success inside nature cannot alone establish that nature is all that exists.

## The Designer Could Act and Speak

Now suppose the programmer intervenes. Something occurs within the game that does not arise from its ordinary internal causal history. This gives us an analogy for miracle.

An unexplained anomaly alone proves little. It may expose only incomplete knowledge of the system. Something more significant occurs when an extraordinary event carries recognizable intentional content through its connection with a particular person, claim, prediction, or purpose. Then it can function as a sign. Biblical signs are not merely strange events. They signify.

The programmer could also communicate information into the game. Christianity calls this revelation. The character cannot climb outside the system to interrogate the programmer, but the programmer can communicate inward. The direction is:

**Creator → creation → creature**

That direction is already present in Romans 1: “God has shown it to them.”

## The Author Enters the Story

Christianity then makes its most extraordinary claim:

> “And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us.” (John 1:14, ESV)

The analogy must remain bounded. Christianity does not teach that reality is literally software, and Christ is not an avatar. The analogy concerns the direction of access. A character cannot cross the ontological boundary toward his author. The author can cross toward the character. Christianity claims that the Logos through whom creation exists entered created history.

Romans 1 gives us the Author inferred through His workmanship. John 1 gives us the Author entering His story.

## And Then Come the Signs

The Gospel accounts do not present Jesus as making claims in isolation. John explicitly connects His identity with signs:

> “These are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God.” (John 20:31, ESV)

Jesus heals, gives sight to the blind, exercises authority over nature, raises the dead, and claims authority to forgive sins. Christianity then makes its central historical claim: He is crucified, dies, is buried, and rises from the dead.

The Resurrection is not presented as a random anomaly. Its meaning is inseparable from the identity and claims of Jesus Christ. The sign signifies.

At this point the evidential question becomes historical. What sources do we possess? How early are they? What did the witnesses claim? Which explanation best accounts for the relevant facts? Christians and skeptics answer those questions differently, but they are plainly questions about evidence.

## The Author Was There All Along

John writes:

> “He was in the world, and the world was made through him, yet the world did not know him.” (John 1:10, ESV)

Read that alongside Romans 1. Creation testified concerning its Creator. The Creator acted within history and communicated through revelation. Then the Logos through whom the world was made entered that world, and still the world did not know Him.

The movement is from God toward us:

**Creation → Intervention → Revelation → Incarnation**

Our corresponding access is:

**Inference → Sign → Communication → Encounter**

So how could a character find his Author? He cannot climb outside his universe by his own power. He can examine the world in which he finds himself. He can ask why it is rationally ordered, why informational differentiation and causal actuality exist, and why his own mind corresponds so remarkably well with the reality it investigates. He can examine claimed interventions, evaluate revelation, and, if the Author enters the story, investigate that claim historically.

Christianity claims that all of these modes of access are present.

The serious question is therefore not, “Why can’t I find God somewhere inside the universe?” That searches for the Author among the characters. The better question is, “If the Creator has made Himself known through creation, acted within history, communicated through revelation, and entered creation in Jesus Christ, what evidence should those acts have left, and do we have it?”

One can challenge the inference from creation. One can propose an impersonal ground for logic, information, and causality. One can reject biblical revelation, dispute miracle accounts, and propose alternative explanations for the Resurrection. Those are substantive arguments because they evaluate proposed evidence.

But the blanket declaration “there is no evidence for God” can no longer do the work. At most, the critic may argue that the evidence is inadequate, ambiguous, or better explained by another worldview. That is precisely the debate Christianity invites.

Study creation rigorously. Learn its regularities. Map its information. Understand its causal structure. Follow the evidence wherever it leads. But do not confuse an increasingly sophisticated description of the workmanship with an ultimate explanation for the Worker.

And do not spend your entire life searching the story for its Author while ignoring Christianity’s extraordinary claim:

**The Author has already entered it.**
