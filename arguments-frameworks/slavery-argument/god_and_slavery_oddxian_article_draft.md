# God and Slavery: What God's Liberation of Israel Adds to the Question

**James D. Longmire | oddXian.com | Publication candidate, not yet approved**

The most difficult objection to biblical slavery is not that the Bible mentions the practice. It is that the law given to Israel expressly allows people to be bought, held, and inherited. Leviticus 25:44–46 says precisely that about foreign slaves. Any response that converts those provisions into ordinary employment arrangements has already lost contact with the text.

But a second set of facts must also be heard. God intervenes to deliver Israel from bondage (Exodus 3:7–8). Before giving the Ten Commandments, He identifies Himself as the one who brought them out of the house of slavery (Exodus 20:2). He commands Israel to remember its own enslavement when releasing Hebrew servants, and to provide generously for those released (Deuteronomy 15:12–15).

God's liberating action is foundational to Israel's identity. Freedom is also protected through particular laws: a Hebrew servant's term of service can end (Exodus 21:2); certain serious injuries require emancipation (Exodus 21:26–27); and the prophet Jeremiah condemns the re-enslavement of Hebrew servants who had been released (Jeremiah 34:8–17).

These protections are unequal. Foreign slaves do not receive the same general release guarantee in Leviticus 25. The text leaves a genuine moral tension.

The theological distinction I propose is between legal authorization and moral endorsement. Jesus gives an explicit example of a permitted practice that did not reflect the creation ideal when He discusses divorce and hardness of heart (Matthew 19:8). Applying that principle to slavery is an inference, and it does not tell us why God authorized permanent foreign ownership in the first place.

The broader moral norm is equally explicit. All human beings bear God's image (Genesis 1:26–27). Israel is commanded to love neighbor and resident foreigner (Leviticus 19:18, 34). Jesus says love of God and neighbor undergirds the Law and Prophets (Matthew 22:37–40). Paul says love does no wrong to a neighbor (Romans 13:10).

The New Testament does not provide a universal civil decree abolishing slavery. It does, however, place slave and free within the same Christian fellowship and calls Onesimus a beloved brother (Philemon 16; Galatians 3:28). Those teachings matter without pretending they contain a command they do not contain.

So the biblical record includes legal accommodation, specific emancipation, divine liberation, and a moral obligation of love. The cumulative case supports an abolition-oriented Christian ethic. It also leaves an honest question: why did God permit inheritable foreign slavery at all?

The answer cannot be manufactured by ignoring the difficult text. We must consider the whole of Scripture, including God's demonstrated disposition toward people in bondage, and state clearly where interpretation begins.
