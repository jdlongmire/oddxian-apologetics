# God and Slavery: Divine Accommodation, Moral Law, and the Biblical Pattern of Liberation

**James D. (JD) Longmire**  
ORCID: 0009-0009-1383-7698  
oddXian Apologetics | Working research white paper | 20 November 2026  
**Status:** Research draft for critical review; not a completed independent peer review.

## Abstract

The Mosaic law permits forms of human ownership, including permanent, inheritable enslavement of foreigners (Leviticus 25:44–46). This poses a serious theological challenge to claims about God's goodness and the moral coherence of Scripture. This paper distinguishes legal authorization, regulation, and moral endorsement, then evaluates the biblical evidence for God's disposition toward earthly slavery. Its central contribution is to place God's liberating action in the Exodus and the Torah's specified routes to freedom alongside the laws that authorize enslavement. It argues that the commands to love God and neighbor provide an interpretive moral standard, while the Exodus supplies affirmative narrative evidence that liberation is a good. The proposed explanation of permission as accommodation to fallen social conditions is a theological inference, supported analogically by Jesus' treatment of divorce (Matthew 19:8), rather than an explicit biblical explanation of Leviticus 25. The paper acknowledges the unequal treatment of Hebrew and foreign slaves, the limited reach of release provisions, and the absence of a categorical biblical command abolishing all slavery. A cumulative abolition-oriented reading is defensible within Christian canonical theology, but it is neither an explicit Mosaic statute nor a deduction free of interpretive premises.

**Keywords:** biblical slavery; Exodus; liberation; accommodation; common grace; neighbor-love; Leviticus 25; emancipation; Christian ethics; biblical interpretation.

## 1. The actual question

The difficult question is specific: Why would a holy God authorize Israelites to acquire and transmit ownership of other human beings, especially foreigners, while setting Israel apart from surrounding societies in other respects? Merely observing that ancient law restricted abuse cannot answer this question. Exodus 21:20–21, 26–27 regulates injury to enslaved persons; Leviticus 25:44–46 permits acquisition, inheritance, and ongoing service. A law that moderates a relationship can simultaneously authorize its continuation. The objection therefore deserves direct treatment.

This study asks whether that authorization warrants the further conclusion that God regards human ownership as a morally exemplary institution. The answer depends on distinctions that must be kept explicit.

**Permission** permits a practice in a specified legal setting. **Authorization** gives legal warrant to engage in it. **Regulation** constrains how it operates. **Moral endorsement** commends it as consistent with a moral ideal. **Abolition** legally terminates it. These categories can overlap, but one cannot infer all the others from any single category. The Torah clearly authorizes some forms of slavery. It also prescribes some routes to freedom. It does not categorically abolish all slavery. Whether its authorization implies divine moral endorsement is the theological question under examination.

## 2. Creation and the governing moral norm

Genesis 1:26–27 presents humanity, male and female, as created in God's image. This provides a foundational claim of shared human dignity. Genesis 2 portrays human relationship and communion before the Fall, though it does not directly legislate against later institutions of servitude. These texts therefore support a moral premise without themselves settling the slavery-law question.

The Shema commands comprehensive love of God (Deuteronomy 6:4–5). Leviticus 19:18 commands love of neighbor, and Leviticus 19:34 extends love-as-self language to the resident foreigner. Jesus identifies love of God and neighbor as the two commandments on which the Law and Prophets depend (Matthew 22:37–40). Paul says love fulfills the law and does no wrong to a neighbor (Romans 13:8–10).

Here **meta-law** is an analytical term for that governing moral norm, not a biblical technical term. The proposed inference is that a human relationship granting one party coercive ownership and the power to deny another's freedom requires moral justification under the love command. Kindness within ownership cannot by itself establish that the ownership is justified. Conversely, the love command does not independently specify a detailed ancient civil code. The inference from neighbor-love to universal abolition needs argument, including consideration of economic dependence, coercion, and the slave's interests.

## 3. Fall, patience, and accommodation

The biblical narrative presents fallen humanity as prone to violence, domination, and broken relationships (Genesis 3–4). Divine patience toward sinners is a major theme (Psalm 130:3–4; Romans 2:4; 2 Peter 3:9). In this paper **common grace** names God's continued preservation and restraint within a fallen social order. That label is theological synthesis.

Jesus explicitly explains that Moses permitted divorce because of hardness of heart, despite a prior creation pattern (Matthew 19:3–8). This supplies a demonstrable example of permission that falls short of an original moral ideal. It does **not** say that the Mosaic slavery provisions were given for the same reason. Applying the divorce precedent to slavery is an analogy requiring independent justification.

The prior corpus paper frames accommodation under the Noahic covenant as structurally necessary. This draft adopts the more modest claim that patience and restraint are consistent with the canonical narrative. The stronger proposition that God had only two logically possible options, immediate universal judgment or accommodation, has not been established as a deductive theorem. God could have imposed additional specific prohibitions without immediately destroying humanity. The question why He did not prohibit permanent foreign slavery remains.

Thus accommodation is a proposed theological explanation, not a textual escape hatch. It cannot be used to erase the authorization contained in Leviticus 25.

## 4. God's demonstrated disposition: the Exodus

The most important additional evidence is narrative action. In Exodus 3:7–8 God identifies Israel's affliction and announces His intention to deliver His people. Exodus 6:6–7 connects redemption from Egyptian bondage with covenant belonging. Exodus 20:2 opens the Decalogue by identifying God as the one who brought Israel out of the house of slavery. The event becomes a recurring basis for Israel's moral memory.

This matters because the Exodus is not merely an incidental rescue story. God's deliverance establishes the identity of the covenant community before the Sinai laws are articulated. The covenant law is received by a liberated people, who are repeatedly required to remember their own prior bondage (Deuteronomy 5:15; 15:15; 24:18).

The resulting inference is deliberately bounded. **God's liberation of Israel positively demonstrates His concern for a people suffering enslavement and oppression.** It does not by itself establish that every form of servitude in every setting is prohibited, or that God undertakes the emancipation of all enslaved peoples in the Exodus narrative. Indeed, Israel's privileged status is part of the difficulty: the same covenant law that recalls their liberation differentiates their release protections from those of foreigners.

Nonetheless, an account of God's disposition that considers only the slavery permissions and omits the centrality of liberation is incomplete.

## 5. Freedom within the accommodated legal order

The Torah provides concrete, unequal routes to freedom:

**Term-limited Hebrew service.** Exodus 21:2 mandates release in the seventh year for a Hebrew male slave. Deuteronomy 15:12–15 speaks of Hebrew men and women and requires released servants to receive substantial provision, expressly grounded in Israel's redemption from Egypt. Leviticus 25:39–43 treats impoverished Israelite kin as hired workers until Jubilee, with restoration to clan and property. These passages have distinct legal settings and cannot simply be collapsed into one uniform schedule.

**Injury-based emancipation.** Exodus 21:26–27 requires freedom when a master destroys a male or female slave's eye or knocks out a tooth. The passage is not explicitly restricted to Hebrew slaves. This establishes a legal consequence for specified injuries, not a general right to depart whenever one chooses. Exodus 21:20–21 also contains a troubling rule concerning the punishment of a master who beats a slave, including a distinction based on whether the victim survives a day or two. The protection and the permission must both remain in view.

**Escaped slaves.** Deuteronomy 23:15–16 prohibits handing an escaped slave back to a master and allows the escapee to dwell where desired. The wording is broad, but its historical scope, especially whether it principally concerns fugitives from foreign jurisdictions, is debated. It should not be represented without qualification as universally nullifying Israelite slave ownership.

**Prophetic enforcement.** Jeremiah 34:8–17 condemns the reversal of an agreement to free Hebrew slaves. Isaiah 58:6 depicts the loosening of oppression as proper worship. These passages reinforce liberation as a positive biblical moral concern, although Jeremiah's immediate legal case concerns Hebrew persons.

**The decisive asymmetry.** Leviticus 25:44–46 authorizes the purchase of slaves from surrounding nations and resident foreign families, calls them property, and permits inheritance as permanent possession. Unlike Israelite kin, these persons receive no general seventh-year or Jubilee release guarantee in that text. Any claim that all biblical slavery was simply temporary debt employment is untenable. Likewise, the presence of some emancipation mechanisms does not establish that all enslaved persons had an effective route to freedom.

The precise thesis is therefore: **even within a law that authorizes slavery, freedom is expressly protected and commanded in particular circumstances.** That is meaningful evidence, with real limits.

## 6. The New Testament and the question of abolition

Jesus' proclamation of liberty in Luke 4:18–19 draws on Isaiah's prophetic language. Its messianic and spiritual dimensions should not be reduced to a modern civil emancipation statute. The liberation of sinners from sin (John 8:34–36) and release from human ownership are distinct realities, even when Christian theology connects them analogically.

Paul addresses actual enslaved people and masters in the existing Roman social order. Ephesians 6:5–9 instructs slaves to obey earthly masters while also instructing masters to stop threatening and reminding both that they share a heavenly Master. This is morally consequential regulation, but it does not explicitly command manumission. In Philemon 8–21 Paul appeals concerning Onesimus, asking Philemon to receive him as a beloved brother. Whether Paul intended or expected formal emancipation is debated; the letter contains no unambiguous universal abolition command. First Corinthians 7:21 has a disputed interpretive and translation history; the ESV reads it as encouraging a slave to avail himself of freedom when possible. Galatians 3:28 teaches unity in Christ across slave/free status without directly replacing Roman civil law.

First Timothy 1:10 condemns enslavers or slave traders, but that condemnation cannot simply be assumed to cover every form of ownership authorized in Leviticus 25 without lexical and contextual argument.

These texts support the thesis that Christian equality, brotherhood, and freedom create ethical pressure on the institution. They also explain why critics insist that the New Testament stops short of explicit abolition. Both observations are textually relevant.

## 7. Objections and responses

### Objection A: Leviticus 25 authorizes property in human beings

**Granted.** The language is legally affirmative and hereditary. A defense that calls the passage merely a humane employment contract misrepresents the text. The proposed response is that legal authorization does not logically entail commendation as a timeless ideal. The opponent can reasonably ask why this authorization was given at all. Accommodation is a possible canonical explanation, but no verse expressly applies Matthew 19:8 to Leviticus 25. This residual question remains unresolved.

### Objection B: God set Israel apart in other matters; why not here?

This is a stronger objection than simply saying the Bible mentions slavery. It challenges the selectivity of divine law. The Exodus, love commands, and emancipation provisions show that freedom and human dignity are morally significant within the same legal corpus. They do not supply an explicit explanation of why a complete ban was withheld. Claims of practical necessity, economic feasibility, or pedagogical gradualism require additional evidence and should be presented as hypotheses.

### Objection C: A kindly master can own another person

Correct. Beneficent treatment and freedom are distinct. The centurion's concern for his servant in Matthew 8:5–13 evidences care and faith; Jesus' healing and praise of faith do not constitute an explicit endorsement of slaveholding. Equally, His silence in that encounter does not establish a direct condemnation of the centurion's status as a master.

### Objection D: The apostles accommodated slavery

Correct as a descriptive matter. Household instructions and Philemon address people inside an existing institution. The canonical argument for abolition requires moral synthesis across texts and is not equivalent to quoting an apostolic statute that abolishes slavery.

### Objection E: Christians historically used Scripture to justify slavery

The historical use of biblical slavery texts is real and cannot be dismissed merely by pointing to later Christian abolitionism. Nicholas V's fifteenth-century bulls are relevant to the history of religious justifications of enslavement, but their specific language, jurisdiction, reception, and relationship to Catholic claims of authority require verification from the original documents before detailed historical claims are made. The theological validity of an interpretation cannot be established solely by its historical adoption.

## 8. The cumulative argument and its limits

The proposed argument has five stages:

1. The creation narrative identifies all humanity as bearers of God's image.
2. The commands to love God, neighbor, and sojourner provide a governing moral norm.
3. God's liberation of Israel is central to His self-disclosure and covenantal identity.
4. The law provides specified emancipation rights and commands remembrance of bondage, while also authorizing permanent foreign slavery.
5. The New Testament extends spiritual and ecclesial equality and recognizes opportunities for freedom without enacting a universal civil prohibition.

Together these premises support a **canonical, abolition-oriented moral reading**: permanent coercive ownership is difficult to reconcile with the full demands of love of neighbor and shared human dignity. This is an ethical and theological inference. The Bible's explicit legal material is more complex than a claim that God simply prohibited slavery from the outset.

The most important unanswered question remains: **Why did God authorize inheritable foreign slavery in Leviticus 25:44–46 rather than prohibit it?** The accommodation hypothesis offers an intelligible account of how permission and a higher moral norm might coexist, but it does not independently demonstrate why that specific permission was necessary. A responsible apologetic should concede this limitation.

## 9. Conclusion

God's disposition toward earthly slavery must be assessed through His actions as well as His laws. He delivered Israel from bondage, made redemption foundational to covenant identity, required remembrance of enslavement in the treatment of others, and established particular mechanisms for release. Those facts are substantial and cannot be ignored in interpreting the Mosaic permissions. Yet the same Scripture authorizes the permanent, inheritable ownership of foreigners. Both belong to the evidence.

The distinction between accommodation and endorsement prevents a simplistic inference from legal permission to moral idealization. It does not resolve every moral difficulty. The strongest Christian case is cumulative: creation dignity, covenant liberation, love as governing moral obligation, concrete release protections, prophetic critique of oppression, and the equal standing of believers in Christ. This case supports an abolition-oriented moral conclusion while remaining candid about the gap between that conclusion and the explicit ancient legal texts.
