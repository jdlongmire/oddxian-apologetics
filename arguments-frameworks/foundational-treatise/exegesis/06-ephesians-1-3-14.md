## 1. Text and translation

Verses 3-14 are one sentence in the Greek text, running without a full stop from the opening blessing to the third occurrence of the refrain "to the praise of his glory" (the NET note ad 1:4 states the fact plainly: "Eph 1:3-14 are one long sentence in Greek that must be broken up in English translation"). It opens as a berakah, a blessing of God on the Jewish pattern: "Blessed be (eulogetos) the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed (ho eulogesas) us with every spiritual blessing (en pase eulogia pneumatike) in the heavenly places in Christ." The sentence then unfolds in three movements, each closing with the praise refrain: the Father electing and predestining (1:3-6, refrain at v. 6, "to the praise of the glory of his grace"); the Son redeeming and gathering all things (1:7-12, refrain at v. 12); the Spirit sealing and guaranteeing (1:13-14, refrain at v. 14). The sphere phrase "in Christ / in him / in the Beloved" saturates every movement (vv. 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13); no blessing in the sentence is located anywhere else.

Verse 11, the study's center of weight, reads in transliteration: "en ho kai eklerothemen, prooristhentes kata prothesin tou ta panta energountos kata ten boulen tou thelematos autou." Literally: "in whom also we were allotted (eklerothemen), having been predestined (prooristhentes) according to the purpose (kata prothesin) of him who works (tou... energountos) all things (ta panta) according to the counsel (kata ten boulen) of his will (tou thelematos autou)."

Translation comparison for verse 11 (the kleroo verb / energeo / bouleh):

| Version | Eklerothemen | Energeountos phrase | Bouleh |
|---|---|---|---|
| KJV | we have obtained an inheritance | him who worketh all things | after the counsel of his own will |
| ESV | we have obtained an inheritance | him who works all things | according to the counsel of his will |
| NASB | we also have obtained an inheritance | Him who works all things | in accordance with the plan of His will |
| NIV | we were also chosen (footnote: "Or were made heirs") | him who works out everything in conformity with the purpose of his will (bouleh rendered "purpose," prothesis rendered "plan") | purpose |
| NET | we too have been claimed as God's own possession | him who accomplishes all things | according to the counsel of his will |
| CSB | we have also received an inheritance | the one who works out everything in agreement with the purpose of his will (prothesis rendered "plan") | purpose |

Three translation facts matter. First, the versions split on the kleroo verb (section 3.8): the majority tradition (KJV, ESV, NASB, CSB) takes the inheritance as ours; NET takes us as God's; NIV prints "chosen" and footnotes the alternative. Second, no version weakens the energountos clause: energeo is rendered "works," "works out," or "accomplishes," and ta panta is "all things" or "everything" in every version in the table. Third, bouleh divides the table: KJV, ESV, and NET keep "counsel"; NASB renders it "plan"; NIV and CSB render prothesis as "plan" and bouleh as "purpose," so that the two English nouns trade Greek partners between the version families. The doctrinal content is unaffected, but a treatise quoting one version's wording should know which Greek noun stands behind its "plan" and which behind its "purpose."

## 2. Context

The berakah stands at the head of the letter, before any exhortation, and its function is ground-laying: everything Ephesians will ask of its readers is built on what God has done in this sentence. Three contextual controls bear on the study's questions.

First, the sentence's own temporal architecture. The choosing is located "before the foundation of the world" (1:4); the purpose is one God "set forth in Christ" for "the administration of the fullness of the times" (1:9-10); the hearers enter the blessing in time, at the hearing of the gospel (1:13). Eternity, plan, and history are distinguished and related inside the sentence itself, which is why the competing readings in section 5 are really arguments about which of these joints bears the weight.

Second, the "we" and "you" of 1:11-13. Verse 12 describes the "we" as "the ones who hoped in Christ beforehand" (tous proelpikotas en to Christo, proelpizo being a New Testament hapax); verse 13 turns: "in whom you also (kai humeis), when you heard the word of truth... and believed, were sealed." Two analyses are live. (a) The "we" are those who believed first (in the letter's horizon, Jewish believers and the apostolic circle), and the "you" are the Gentile readers now included; the letter's own second chapter, with its "you who once were far off" (2:13) and its making of the two into one, reads naturally as the expansion of this movement. (b) The "we" is Christians inclusively throughout and the kai of 1:13 simply gathers the readers into the same story (so the Expositor's Greek Testament, arguing that the kai in 1:11 does not qualify the subjects and opposing the restriction of the "we" to Jewish Christians). This study does not need to decide between them, and does not: as section 4, finding 3 shows, the scope claim of 1:11 rests on the energountos clause, which describes God and not the boundary of either pronoun group. What the we/you movement does establish, on either analysis, is the order of experience: the blessing is announced as already secured (aorists throughout 1:3-12) before the hearers' believing is mentioned (1:13), and the seal follows the believing. The sentence's sequence is decree, announcement, faith, seal; it nowhere runs the other way.

Third, the destination question (section 6) hangs over the letter, not over this sentence: nothing in 1:3-14 names a city or depends on one.

## 3. Grammar and word studies

### 3.1 Exelexato (Strong's G1586, eklegomai), 1:4

Thayer: eklego in the New Testament is "always middle, eklegomai, to pick or choose out for oneself"; of God, it is said of "those whom he has judged fit to receive his favors and separated from the rest of mankind to be peculiarly his own and to be attended continually by his gracious oversight," with Christians so described at Mark 13:20 and 1 Corinthians 1:27-28, and, Thayer's own gloss on this verse, "tina en Christo, so that the ground of the choice lies in Christ and his merits, followed by the accusative with an infinitive denoting the end, Ephesians 1:4." The form in 1:4 is exelexato, aorist middle, third singular, God the subject, hemas ("us") the accusative object, en auto ("in him") marking the sphere and ground. Two grammatical facts carry weight in section 5: the middle voice (he chose out for himself; the choosing is reflexive in interest, God's own act for his own possession), and the infinitive of purpose einai hemas hagious kai amomous, "that we should be holy and unblemished before him": holiness is the stated end of the choosing. The choosing is dated pro kataboles kosmou, "before the foundation of the world" (katabole, G2602, from kataballo, a laying down, hence a founding; the phrase is the New Testament's standard marker for what precedes the created order's history, as at John 17:24 and 1 Peter 1:20, where the Son's being loved and foreknown stand in the same position).

### 3.2 Proorizo (Strong's G4309), 1:5 and 1:11

From pro and horizo, to bound or mark out: Strong's, "to limit in advance, i.e. (figuratively) predetermine." The verb occurs six times in the New Testament: Acts 4:28 (whatever God's hand and bouleh "predestined to occur," of the cross); Romans 8:29, 30 (unit 05, section 3.4); 1 Corinthians 2:7 (the wisdom God "predestined before the ages"); Ephesians 1:5 and 1:11. In 1:5 the form is an aorist participle, proorisas, nominative masculine singular, agreeing with the Father who is the subject of exelexato, with hemas as its object and eis huiothesian, "for adoption as sons," as its goal, the whole phrase governed by kata ten eudokian tou thelematos autou, "according to the good pleasure of his will." In 1:11 the form is passive, prooristhentes, nominative masculine plural, agreeing with the "we" inside eklerothemen. The object profile matters for section 5: where Paul uses proorizo with a personal object (Romans 8; Ephesians 1:5, 11), persons are predestined to a blessing (conformity, adoption, inheritance); the verb's one impersonal object is the event of the cross (Acts 4:28), where it stands in tandem with bouleh exactly as in 1:11. Nowhere in the passage is anyone predestined to unbelief or ruin; the text does not raise the subject, and this study registers that silence as a limit on what Part II may cite Ephesians 1 for, not as a datum in either direction.

### 3.3 Prothesis (Strong's G4286), 1:11, and proetheto, 1:9

Unit 05, section 3.3 established the noun's profile: from protithemi, "to set before," hence a purpose or plan set forth; for divine purpose at Romans 8:28; 9:11 ("the purpose of God according to election"); Ephesians 1:11; 3:11 ("the eternal purpose," kata prothesin ton aionon); 2 Timothy 1:9. Unit 06 adds the cognate verb inside this sentence: 1:9, the mystery of his will made known "according to his good pleasure (kata ten eudokian autou) which he purposed (hen proetheto) in him." The NET note ad loc. takes the referent of "in him" to be Christ rather than the Father (against the KJV's "in himself"), on the ground that protithemi "implies a plan that is carried out in history (cf. Rom 1:13; 3:25)," Romans 3:25 being the setting-forth of Christ himself. The prothesis of 1:11 is therefore not a second, distinct purpose: it is the same purpose set forth in Christ in 1:9, now named as the standard (kata prothesin) according to which the heirs were predestined. The sentence's purpose vocabulary is one fabric, not a pile of synonyms; section 3.6 draws the consequence.

### 3.4 Eudokia (Strong's G2107), 1:5 and 1:9

Strong's: "satisfaction, i.e. (subjectively) delight, or (objectively) kindness, wish, purpose." The word appears twice in the sentence, both times as the named ground of a divine act: the predestination to adoption is kata ten eudokian tou thelematos autou (1:5), and the revealing of the mystery is kata ten eudokian autou (1:9). Whatever else the conditional reading in section 5.2 must do, it must reckon with the fact that the sentence twice assigns the ground of God's saving acts to his own pleasure, and assigns it to nothing else. Eudokia is not bare arbitrariness: it is pleasure, delight taken in the act; but it is a pleasure located in God, not a response elicited by a quality in the object, which is the only point this study needs from it.

### 3.5 Bouleh (Strong's G1012) and thelema (Strong's G2307), 1:11

Thayer on bouleh: "counsel, purpose... especially of the purpose of God respecting the salvation of men through Christ: Luke 7:30; Acts 2:23; Acts 4:28; (Hebrews 6:17); pasan ten boulen tou theou, all the contents of the divine plan, Acts 20:27; he bouleh tou thelematos autou, the counsel of his will, Ephesians 1:11." The word is the vocabulary of deliberated resolve, counsel taken and settled, and its Pauline and Lukan company is exact: Acts 2:23 joins it to foreknowledge ("the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God"), Acts 4:28 joins it to proorizo, and Hebrews 6:17 speaks of "the immutability of his counsel" (to ametatheton tes boules autou). Thelema (Strong's: "a determination (properly, the thing), i.e. (actively) choice (specially, purpose, decree; abstractly, volition) or (passively) inclination") is the sentence's most repeated noun of volition: the mystery is "of his will" (1:9), the predestination is according to the pleasure "of his will" (1:5), and the working is according to the counsel "of his will" (1:11). The genitive chain in 1:11, kata ten boulen tou thelematos autou, makes will the owner of the counsel: God's working is not will acting on impulse, nor counsel acting without will; the counsel is the will's own deliberated form.

### 3.6 The accumulation in 1:11: prothesis, bouleh, thelema

Verse 11 stacks three determination nouns around one act: the heirs were predestined kata prothesin (according to purpose), by the God who works kata ten boulen tou thelematos (according to the counsel of his will); and 1:5 and 1:9 have already added eudokia twice. The accumulation is emphatic, not taxonomic. Each noun contributes a shade: prothesis, the plan as set forth (and, per 1:9, set forth in Christ); bouleh, the plan as deliberated and settled; thelema, the plan as willed by God himself; eudokia, the plan as his pleasure. Part II must not mint three decrees or a sequence of divine deliberations out of the rhetoric: the sentence gives one determination described under several aspects, all of them God's, none of them conditioned on the creature in the text. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM: the one-determination judgment is a reading of the accumulation's rhetoric, though the aspects distinguished are the words' lexical senses]

### 3.7 Energeo (Strong's G1754) and the phrase ta panta energountos

Thayer distinguishes two uses: (1) intransitive, "to be operative, be at work, put forth power" (Matthew 14:2; Mark 6:14; Ephesians 2:2; Galatians 2:8); (2) transitive, "to effect," with an accusative object, citing 1 Corinthians 12:11 and, expressly, Ephesians 1:11. The form here is energountos, present active participle, genitive masculine singular, with the article (tou ta panta energountos): "of the one who works all things," a standing description of God, in the present tense of characteristic activity, not a report of one act. The object is ta panta, with the article: not "some things" and not bare panta, but the all-things as a definite totality. The phrase is a settled Pauline description, not an ad hoc flourish of this sentence: 1 Corinthians 12:6 has "the same God who works all things in all" (ho energon ta panta en pasin), and Philippians 2:13 has "God is the one at work in you" (ho energon en humin); Ephesians itself returns to the word family for the power exerted in raising Christ (1:19-20) and the power at work in believers (3:20). For the corroborant question this is the grammatical heart of the study: in 1:11 the agent of the universal working is not embedded, supplied, or disputed; the participle's subject is God by the structure of the genitive chain, the verb is transitive on Thayer's classification, and the object is total by its article.

### 3.8 Kleroo (Strong's G2820), eklerothemen, 1:11

The verb is a New Testament hapax (the compound proskleroo occurs once, Acts 17:4). Its root is kleros, a lot, and then the portion assigned by lot. The debate is over the direction of the possession in the passive eklerothemen, and it is a real debate, which this study reports rather than settles by silence.

- **"We were made a heritage": believers are allotted as God's own portion.** This is BDAG's classification as reported by the NET note: the verb in the Old Testament field meant "to appoint a portion by lot" (the more frequent cognate kleronomeo meant "obtain a portion, inherit"); in the passive here, "we were appointed [as a portion] by lot" (BDAG 548 s.v. kleroo 1). Robertson's Word Pictures takes the same side: "We were made a heritage. First aorist passive of kleroo, an old word, to assign by lot (kleros), to make a kleros or heritage. So in LXX and papyri." The English Revised Version rendered "we were made a heritage," the Douay-Rheims "we also are called by lot," and the NET prints "we too have been claimed as God's own possession," adding the note that God is not named in the Greek but the context is clear, with Deuteronomy 32:8-9 behind it: there God apportions the nations, and the LORD's own portion (kleronomia in the LXX field) is his people Jacob. The lexical edge of this reading is the NET note's distinction: kleroo is not kleronomeo; to render eklerothemen "we obtained an inheritance" treats the hapax as though it were the common inheritance verb.
- **"We have obtained an inheritance": the inheritance is allotted to us.** This is the majority translation tradition (KJV, ESV, NASB, CSB, YLT), and the NIV's footnote ("Or were made heirs") shows its committee holding both doors open. In its favor: the sentence's inheritance vocabulary runs toward the believers as well (1:14, the Spirit as "down payment of our inheritance," arrabon tes kleronomias hemon; and 1:18 can be read either way), and Hoehner's discussion, cited by the NET note (Ephesians, 226-27), is the standard modern treatment of the option. The Expositor's Greek Testament, while rendering "were made a heritage," concedes the depth of the dispute and traces how lot-language and inheritance-language interpenetrate in the Old Testament's land allotments, where the portion assigned by lot is precisely the inalienable family inheritance.

Verdict: the "made a heritage" reading is the lexically stronger account of kleroo in the passive and is adopted in this study (with BDAG as quoted by NET, Robertson, and the ERV), at MEDIUM-HIGH confidence; it also coheres with the sentence's God-ward refrain and with 1:14's "redemption of God's own possession" (tes peripoieseos). But the study records, with equal explicitness, what the verdict does not touch: the clauses that carry this unit's assignment, prooristhentes kata prothesin and the energountos clause, are identical on either rendering of eklerothemen. The inheritance's direction is a genuine exegetical question for Part II's treatment of possession and inheritance; it is not a variable in the corroborant test, and no party to the debate may recruit this study's scope findings as if they depended on it.

## 4. Findings

1. **The sentence grounds its saving acts exclusively on God's side.** Choosing (1:4), predestining (1:5, 11), purposing (1:9, 11), and working (1:11) are all God's acts, and the grounds named are his pleasure (eudokia, 1:5, 9), his purpose (prothesis, 1:11), the counsel of his will (bouleh tou thelematos, 1:11). No human quality, act, or foreseen response is named anywhere in the sentence as a ground or condition. [Explicit text, HIGH]
2. **The acts are pretemporal and their objects are persons marked out for blessings.** The choosing is pro kataboles kosmou (1:4); the predestining is of hemas, "us," to adoption (1:5) and to the inheritance (1:11); the pro- compounds (proorisas, proetheto, prooristhentes, and 1:10's pleroma horizon) put the determination before the history it governs. [Explicit text, HIGH]
3. **The corroborant test: unit 05's use of Ephesians 1:11 is sustained, and strengthened.** Unit 05 used this verse for the agency component of RP because Romans 8:28's syntax leaves the agent of the working distributed among construals (unit 05, section 3.2). At 1:11 there is no corresponding ambiguity: God is the participle's subject by construction, energeo is transitive, and ta panta is its object. Whatever else is disputed in this verse (section 3.8), the proposition "God works all things according to the counsel of his will" is stated in so many words. [Explicit text, HIGH]
4. **The scope of ta panta in 1:11 is all things without exception, within the created totality the sentence itself defines.** The discourse evidence is internal and specific. (a) The object is articular, ta panta, the totality as one definite whole (section 3.7). (b) The nearest antecedent occurrence is 1:10: the purpose is "to sum up all things in Christ" (anakephalaiosasthai ta panta en to Christo), and verse 10 glosses its own ta panta with an exhaustive bipartition, "things in heaven and things on earth" (ta epi tois ouranois kai ta epi tes ges); 1:11's ta panta follows within the same sentence and takes the same referent unless the text signals a narrowing, and it signals none. (c) The clause's rhetorical function is to ground the heirs' predestination in the character of the God who made it: a working restricted to the redeemed subset would restate the conclusion instead of grounding it. The rival scope options fail on this evidence: "all things without distinction" (all kinds of things, or all kinds of people) describes a truth about the inheritance's distribution across Jew and Gentile in this letter, but it is not what the noun phrase denotes in a clause whose antecedent gloss is heaven and earth; and a contextually limited sense (all things pertaining to the summing up, or to salvation history) is the one restriction verse 10's merism expressly excludes, since the summing up itself is of the heaven-and-earth totality. What "without exception" means here must be stated exactly: the domain of God's working admits no remainder outside the counsel of his will; the verse speaks of domain, not of manner (finding 5). [Necessary inference from the discourse structure, HIGH for the domain verdict; the inference is from 1:10's gloss and 1:11's anaphora, both textual]
5. **What 1:11 cannot carry, stated as limits on this study's own corroborant.** (a) It does not specify the mode of the working: how God's working relates to secondary causes, creaturely intention, or evil as evil is not addressed by the participle, and the treatise's asymmetrical-intention account (unit 03) and its Part V theodicy may not cite 1:11 as though it settled them. (b) It states no per-event benevolent telos: there is no eis agathon here; the good-for-the-called of Romans 8:28 is not repeated, and no universal beneficial outcome for all persons is asserted. (c) It states no reprobation: the non-elect are simply absent from the sentence (section 3.2). (d) Its universal is a description of God ("him who works all things"), not a phenomenological claim about how events appear or a promise about how they feel; Part II quotes it as a premise about God or not at all. [Explicit text, HIGH for each negative point: these are absences in a sentence whose positive contents are fully inventoried in findings 1-4]
6. **The kleroo verdict does not load on the corroborant.** Eklerothemen is best read "we were made God's own portion" (section 3.8), and the majority rendering "we have obtained an inheritance" remains a defensible account of the verse's theology given 1:14 and 1:18; but the prothesis and energountos clauses are invariant across the debate. [Lexical verdict, MEDIUM-HIGH; invariance point, HIGH]
7. **The sentence's register is corporate and Christ-located; its election is nevertheless of persons, not of an abstraction.** The berakah speaks in plurals, locates every blessing en Christo, and names no individual in isolation; at the same time its verbs take persons as objects (exelexato hemas; proorisas hemas) before the foundation of the world, when no group existed in history to be incorporated into. The adjudication in section 5 states the position in full; the finding here is the shape any faithful Part II treatment must preserve: corporate in form, personal in object, Christological in location. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH]

## 5. Competing readings

1. **The corporate-election reading: election is primarily of the group in Christ; individuals are elect by incorporation.** Strongest form, in its own literature's words. Abasciano's definition (as collected in the Society of Evangelical Arminians' corporate election excerpts): "corporate election refers to the choice of a group, which entails the choice of its individual members by virtue of their membership in the group. Thus, individuals are not elected as individuals directly, but secondarily as members of the elect group." Klein's thesis (The New Chosen People, 1990, and his article "Is Corporate Election Merely Virtual Election?"): "we can indeed speak of God's choice of the church in Christ without also implying that God specifically chose the individuals who will comprise that church," and "Ephesians 1:4 affirms precisely the church's election in Christ without any hint of God's choice of individuals to populate that body." Its commentary form (Snodgrass, Ephesians, NIVAC, as quoted by Klein): "election is primarily a corporate term. Nothing in Ephesians 1 focuses on individuals; rather, the text focuses collectively on those who are in Christ... Individuals are not elected and then put in Christ. They are in Christ and therefore elect." Barth's form of the reading concentrates the whole doctrine in Jesus Christ as both the electing God and the elected man, with the community elect in him and individuals elect as members of the community; its exegetical pillars are the en Christo saturation of this sentence, the uniformly plural pronouns, the Old Testament pattern of a people chosen in its head (Israel in Jacob), and 1:13's incorporation of the "you" at the moment of hearing and believing. *Adjudication.* Accepted: the register. This sentence is corporate in form and Christ-located in every clause, and any account of election that begins from isolated individuals and adds Christ afterward has reversed Paul's order; Part II adopts the corporate form and the en Christo location as the text's own. Declined: the strong negative claim, that the passage contains no divine choice of individuals. Three grammatical facts resist it. (i) Exelexato is middle voice with a personal object: God chose us out, for himself, and Thayer's parallels for God's eklegomai are of persons "separated from the rest of mankind to be peculiarly his own," a separation predicated of the chosen, not of an open class. (ii) The predestining acts are aorists dated before the foundation of the world, with persons as objects and blessings as goals (1:5); an eternal act cannot have as its object a group with no members and no determination of membership, for the group at that date is not a vague intention but either a determinate people in the purpose of God or nothing at all. The corporate reading must silently supply determinacy of membership from the side of future faith, which relocates it into reading 2 and its difficulties. (iii) The we/you movement of 1:12-13 describes the arrival of the blessing in the hearers' experience (the seal follows believing), not the constitution of the elect set; the seal in 1:13-14 is the arrabon of an inheritance already being spoken of as "ours" (hemon, 1:14). What the grammar cannot do, and this study does not pretend it does, is display the mechanism by which individuals come to be in Christ; for that, Part II has the wider canon, including unit 05's chain, in which faith is conspicuously not a link. Verdict: corporate in form, individual by necessary entailment; Barth's Christological concentration is the passage's own framing and is retained, while his further dogmatic steps concerning the extent of election and reprobation in Christ go beyond this sentence and belong to Part X's register, not to this unit's findings. [Assessment: partially accepted, HIGH confidence in the grammatical points (i) and (iii); MEDIUM-HIGH on (ii), which joins the text's dating to a premise about eternal acts and their objects]
2. **The conditional-foreknowledge reading: God chose and predestined on the basis of foreseen faith.** Strongest form: before the foundation of the world God, foreknowing who would freely believe, chose those believers (as a class, or as individuals) in Christ and predestined them, not to faith, but to the blessings faith receives: adoption (1:5), inheritance (1:11), holiness (1:4); prothesis in 1:11 is the plan of salvation and the manner of its working, and "works all things" describes God's sovereign administration of the plan, not a determination of who believes. This reading guards, sincerely, both human responsibility and the text's silence about reprobation. *Adjudication.* Declined as an account of this sentence's grounds, on the sentence's own grammar. (i) No condition is expressed: across twelve verses dense with causal and grounding phrases (kathos, kata with eudokia twice, kata prothesin, kata ten boulen), every ground assigned is on God's side (finding 1); a foreseen-faith condition must be imported into the one passage in the letter that most elaborately lists its grounds. (ii) The infinitive of 1:4 inverts the proposed order: he chose us einai hagious, to be holy; holiness, the sentence's summary of the life faith produces, is the choosing's purpose, not its observed prerequisite, and faith itself is nowhere named as the choosing's basis. (iii) The middle voice of exelexato (for himself) and the dating pro kataboles kosmou locate the determination in God's self-directed purpose before any foreseen act could be its ground in the text's presentation; foreknowledge as a word is absent from the passage (contrast Romans 8:29, unit 05, where proegno carries its own debate and cannot be imported here). What this reading rightly protects, the study keeps and records for Parts II and V: the passage does not teach that God predestines any person to unbelief, and 1:13's believing is real, sequential, and necessary in the order of experience. A reading may not, however, purchase those truths by inserting into 1:4-5 a condition the sentence omits while naming its grounds four times over. [Assessment: declined, HIGH]
3. **The individual unconditional reading the treatise presupposes: God, before the foundation of the world, chose particular persons in Christ and predestined them to adoption and inheritance, according to his own purpose and pleasure, without condition on their part.** Accepted, with two disciplines that the text itself imposes and that Part II must observe when it cites this unit. First, the reading must keep the passage's own form: the election it finds here is of persons in Christ, announced in corporate plurals, inside a blessing of God; it is not the election of bare individuals abstracted from Christ, and the individualizing is presented in this study as entailment from the verbs' personal objects and the acts' pretemporal dating (section 5.1), not as though Paul had written a roster. Second, the reading must not overclaim the sentence's universals: the only universal quantifier in the passage is ta panta in the energountos clause, which is about God's working, not about election's extent (finding 5). Within those disciplines, the reading is the one the grammar supports: personal objects, divine grounds, pretemporal aorists, and a purpose whose content is the persons' adoption and inheritance. [Assessment: accepted as disciplined, HIGH for the grounds and dating; MEDIUM-HIGH for the individual entailment, as tagged in finding 7]

## 6. Textual notes

The decree vocabulary of the sentence is textually stable: in the variant lists consulted for chapter 1, no variant affects exelexato (1:4), proorisas (1:5), proetheto (1:9), prooristhentes, prothesin, or the energountos clause (1:11). The variants that do exist in and around the passage are these.

**1:1, en Epheso ("in Ephesus").** The words are omitted by P46, the original hands of Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, 6, 424c, and 1739, and are printed in brackets by NA28 (the consulted summaries differ slightly in detail: the NET note names 6 and Marcion and does not name 424c); Marcion's canon knew the letter under the title "to the Laodiceans" (Tertullian's report), and the omission underlies the encyclical hypothesis, that the letter circulated to several churches with the destination supplied locally. Bearing on this study: limited but real. If the destination is general, the "you" of 1:13 is the Gentile readership as such, which sits comfortably with the first-hopers analysis of the we/you movement (section 2) without deciding it; nothing in 1:3-14 names or depends on Ephesus, and no finding in section 4 rests on the address. [Textual, MEDIUM-HIGH: the attestation is as listed in the consulted variant summaries; the destination's originality remains genuinely disputed among critics]

**1:11, eklethemen for eklerothemen.** A few uncials read eklethemen, "we were called," for eklerothemen (so the Expositor's Greek Testament, noting the reading was favored by Griesbach, Lachmann, and Ruckert). The study judges it a scribal assimilation toward the common calling vocabulary (and possibly toward Romans 8:30's chain), not the original: it replaces the rarer lot-word with the familiar call-word, the usual direction of travel, and the critical text reads eklerothemen. The variant's existence does, however, explain part of the kleroo debate's history, since a text reading "called" invites no inheritance question at all. [Textual, MEDIUM]

**Minor items.** At 1:7, chrestotetos ("kindness") is read for charitos ("grace") by A, 365, and the Bohairic; the sense is barely affected and nothing in this study rests on the verse's wording. At 1:14, variation between hos and ho for the Spirit as the down payment is recorded in the tradition; the relative's gender does not touch the inheritance argument. The much-discussed variant in the 1:15 area lies outside this unit's text and is not treated here.
