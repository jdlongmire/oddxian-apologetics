## 1. Text and translation

Genesis 6:5 sets the cause: the LORD saw that human wickedness was great, and that every intention of the thoughts of man's heart was "only evil continually." Verse 6: "And the LORD regretted (wayyinnachem, Niphal of nacham) that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him (wayyit'atsev, Hithpael of atsav) to his heart (el-libbo)." Verse 7 puts the regret in God's own speech as the stated ground of the flood judgment: "for I regret (nichamti) that I have made them."

Translation comparison for verse 6 (first verb / second verb):

| Version | Nacham | Atsav |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | it repented the LORD | it grieved him at his heart |
| ESV | the LORD regretted | it grieved him to his heart |
| NASB | was sorry | was grieved in His heart |
| NIV | the LORD regretted | his heart was deeply troubled |
| NET | the Lord regretted | he was highly offended |
| BSB / LEB | regretted | grieved in his heart |

The modern versions converge on "regretted" for nacham and a grief rendering for atsav, with NET the outlier on the second verb (section 5.3). The KJV's "repented" is the rendering most likely to mislead a modern reader, for the reason given in section 3.1.

## 2. Context

The passage is the flood's prologue and God's stated rationale for it. Two contextual facts control the reading. First, the regret is a response to a moral state of affairs (6:5), not to new information about outcomes: nothing in the narrative presents God as surprised by data; the evaluation is of wickedness. Second, the same root pair has just appeared in 5:29, where Lamech names Noah in the hope that "this one will comfort us (yenachamenu, Piel of nacham) from our work and from the painful toil (itsavon, from atsav) of our hands." In 6:6 both roots return with God as subject. Section 4.4 treats that echo.

## 3. Word studies

### 3.1 Nacham (Strong's H5162; 108 occurrences)

BDB's Niphal senses: (1) be sorry, moved to pity, have compassion (Jeremiah 15:6; Psalm 90:13; Judges 2:18); (2) be sorry, rue, repent, suffer grief, including divine relenting from announced judgment (Exodus 32:14; Amos 7:3, 6; Jonah 3:10; the conditional policy of Jeremiah 18:7-10); (3) comfort oneself, be comforted (Genesis 37:35; Psalm 119:52). In the Piel the same root comforts another (Genesis 5:29; Isaiah 40:1). The NET note on 6:6, following Chisholm's semantic survey, assigns this occurrence to the first family: emotional pain and regret concerning a past action, as the parallel grief clause requires.

Two controls on the word's use for God:
- **Nacham is not the repentance-from-sin verb.** That verb is shuv, to turn or return. God is never the subject of shuv from wrongdoing, and nacham of God never carries confession of error. The KJV's "repented" imports a connotation the Hebrew word does not require.
- **Scripture itself qualifies divine nacham.** Numbers 23:19: "God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind (yinnachem)." First Samuel 15 contains both poles in one chapter: "I regret (nichamti) that I have made Saul king" (15:11; echoed 15:35), and "the Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret (lehinnachem), for he is not a man, that he should have regret" (15:29). The denial is explicitly comparative: not *as a man* relents. What is denied is man-like reversal: lying, caprice, and regret born of ignorance or failure. What is affirmed in the same chapter is real divine regret over a real appointment.

### 3.2 Atsav (Strong's H6087)

Root senses (BDB via Blue Letter Bible): Qal, to hurt or pain; Niphal, to be pained or grieved (Genesis 45:5, "do not be grieved"; 2 Samuel 19:3, the king grieving; Nehemiah 8:10-11, "do not grieve"); Piel, to vex (Isaiah 63:10, "they vexed his Holy Spirit"); Hiphil, to cause pain (Psalm 78:40, "how often they rebelled against him in the wilderness and grieved him in the desert"); Hithpael, to feel grief oneself, the form in Genesis 6:6, intensified by el-libbo, "to his heart." The cognate nouns give itsavon, the painful toil of Genesis 3:17 and 5:29, and etsev, pain (Genesis 3:16).

The NET note distinguishes three contextual senses for atsav: physical injury; emotional pain; and offense or insult rising toward anger, and assigns Genesis 6:6 to the third because judgment follows in verse 7. This study accepts the offense as an overtone and keeps emotional pain as the primary sense, on three grounds: the Hithpael is reflexive (felt grief, not a judicial posture); the phrase el-libbo locates the pain in the heart; and the word's closest narrative uses (Genesis 45:5; 2 Samuel 19) are grief, with anger supplied by context rather than by the verb. [Lexical, MEDIUM-HIGH]

## 4. Findings

1. **The regret is real and is moral in object.** Nacham in 6:6-7, in God's narration and then in God's own speech, expresses sorrow and regret over what humanity had become, grounded in the moral verdict of 6:5. The verse is not a narrator's loose figure that the canon elsewhere corrects; 1 Samuel 15 repeats the construction for Saul's appointment. [Explicit text, HIGH]
2. **The grief is real.** Atsav in the Hithpael, "to his heart," predicates felt pain of God, in a pattern Scripture repeats (Psalm 78:40; Isaiah 63:10). A reading that evacuates the affection entirely must explain away a settled biblical pattern, not one verse. [Explicit text, HIGH]
3. **Neither verb asserts ignorance, error, or reversal of character.** Nacham's range includes relenting toward the penitent and pity toward sufferers, neither of which implies prior mistake; and the canon's own qualification (Numbers 23:19; 1 Samuel 15:29) denies man-like relenting, lying, and caprice, while affirming regret in the same breath. The text fixes two poles: genuine divine affection in time, and no instability of character or counsel. The metaphysical reconciliation of the poles belongs to Part V; this study's task is that both poles are textual, not imported. [Canonical synthesis, HIGH for the two poles as textual data; the reconciliation itself is deferred, not claimed here]
4. **The 5:29 / 6:6 root echo is deliberate at the level of the text.** Lamech hopes for nacham-comfort from itsavon-toil through Noah; instead, human wickedness brings nacham-regret and atsav-grief to God, and the comfort that comes arrives through Noah by way of judgment and remnant. The same two roots, reversed in subject and direction. [Literary observation, HIGH for the root repetition; theological synthesis, MEDIUM for the reading of it]
5. **Regret and decree are not set against each other in this text.** Verse 7 makes the regret itself the stated ground of an act of judgment that the wider canon presents as purposeful (compare WP 6.18's theological result, and Genesis 50:20 in unit 03). Genesis 6:6 does not teach exhaustive predestination by itself, and the treatise does not ask it to. [Scope judgment, HIGH]

## 5. Competing readings

1. **Open theism: God learns, is surprised, and revises his plans.** The text supplies no ignorance premise: the regret responds to wickedness evaluated, in a narrative where God has already announced the trajectory of judgment (6:3, the 120 years; 6:7, 13). The canonical qualifications of section 3.1 deny precisely the man-like change this reading requires, and 1 Samuel 15 holds both poles in one chapter, which the open reading must split apart. Declined.
2. **Pure metaphor: the verbs describe effects in creatures only, with no affection in God.** Declined, per finding 2: the Hithpael with el-libbo, and the Psalm 78 / Isaiah 63 pattern, resist evacuation. The classical doctrine of impassibility, properly stated, does not require this evacuation; it denies involuntary, destabilizing passion, not genuine affection. The fuller treatment belongs to Part V with the broader corpus (Augustine, Aquinas on divine will and mercy) in view.
3. **NET's "highly offended" for atsav.** Partially accepted (offense as overtone), not adopted as primary, per section 3.2. Recorded here so the matrix shows the disagreement honestly.

## 6. Textual notes

The Masoretic text of 6:5-7 is stable for both verbs; no significant variant in the consulted apparatus affects them. The ancient versions are interpretive rather than variant evidence here: the Septuagint renders verse 6 with cognitive verbs (enethymethe, "considered"; dienoethe, "reflected") and verse 7's nichamti with ethymothen, "I was angered," a well-attested softening that reshapes the emotion before it can raise the theological difficulty, and later Jewish paraphrase (Targum Onkelos) moves the same direction. The study reads the LXX rendering as early evidence of the interpretive pressure this verse exerts, not as a better text. [Textual, MEDIUM-HIGH: LXX wording verified; the characterization of motive is the standard scholarly one and is an inference]
