## 1. Text and translation

The Greek text (critical text and Textus Receptus agree at this verse in the displays consulted) reads, in transliteration: "Ho de theos tes eirenes, ho anagagon ek nekron ton poimena ton probaton ton megan en haimati diathekes aioniou, ton kyrion hemon Iesoun." Literally: "Now the God of peace, the one who brought up from the dead the shepherd of the sheep, the great one, by the blood of an eternal covenant, our Lord Jesus." The sentence does not end at the verse boundary: verse 21 supplies its only finite verb, the optative katartisai ("may he equip"), so that verse 20 is one long participial description of the God to whom the prayer of verse 21 is addressed. Every clause of verse 20 is therefore a ground of benediction, not an assertion argued for its own sake; the author piles up titles of God and Christ the way a benediction does, and the exegesis must respect that compression. Three translation questions divide the versions: the rendering of anagagon ek nekron ("brought again / brought up / brought back from the dead"); the rendering of aionios ("eternal" or "everlasting"); and the placement of the en haimati phrase, which the Greek holds back until after "the great one," while most versions pull it forward against the verb.

Translation comparison for verse 20:

| Version | Anagagon ek nekron | En haimati placement | Aionios |
|---|---|---|---|
| KJV | that brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus | through the blood (phrase last) | everlasting covenant |
| ESV | who brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus | by the blood (phrase last) | eternal covenant |
| NASB | who brought up from the dead the great Shepherd of the sheep ..., that is, Jesus our Lord | through the blood (in Greek position) | eternal covenant |
| NIV | who ... brought back from the dead our Lord Jesus | through the blood (fronted, before the verb) | eternal covenant |
| NET | who ... brought back from the dead the great shepherd of the sheep, our Lord Jesus Christ | by the blood (fronted, before the verb) | eternal covenant |
| CSB | who brought up from the dead our Lord Jesus | through the blood (phrase last) | everlasting covenant |

Three facts stand out. First, all six versions construe the en haimati phrase with the resurrection clause in sense, whichever position they print it in; none attaches it to "great" (the one commentary proposal that does is treated in section 3.5). Second, "again" (KJV, ESV) and "back" (NIV, NET) and "up" (NASB, CSB) are three different handlings of the same two facts: anago means to lead or bring up (section 3.4), and there is no separate Greek word for "again" in the verse; the versions supply it from the ana- prefix and from the sense (a bringing up out of death is necessarily a bringing back). Third, the versions divide on aionios exactly as English usage divides: "eternal" (ESV, NASB, NIV, NET) against "everlasting" (KJV, CSB), a difference that carries no doctrinal weight in itself, since both render the same adjective whose sense is fixed in section 3.2. The NASB alone preserves the Greek order in which the object of the raising is not named until the verse's last words, "our Lord Jesus," with "that is" supplied to carry the delayed identification.

## 2. Context

Verses 20-21 are the epistle's only formal benediction, and they stand where a benediction stands: after the final exhortations of 13:1-19 and the author's request for prayer (13:18-19: "pray for us... that I may be restored to you very soon"), and before the postscript of 13:22-25 (the appeal to bear the word of exhortation, the news of Timothy, the greetings). The benediction is a single sentence. Verse 20 names the God who is asked to act, by a participial clause that recapitulates the whole letter; verse 21 states the petition and closes in doxology: "equip you (katartisai) in everything good in order to do his will (eis to poiesai to thelema autou), working in us (poion en hemin) what is pleasing before him (to euareston enopion autou) through Jesus Christ, to whom be glory forever. Amen."

Three contextual controls follow. First, the benediction's grounds are the epistle's own argument in miniature. The God addressed is identified by the resurrection of Jesus (the epistle's presupposed foundation, here stated outright and climactically: God raised him); Jesus is named by a title Hebrews has not used before, shepherd; and the instrument named is the blood of the covenant, the epistle's central cultic category (9:20; 10:29; 12:24), restated in the immediately preceding paragraph, where Jesus "suffered outside the camp in order to sanctify the people by his own blood" (13:12, NET). Nothing in verse 20 is new coinage; every element is a term the letter has already loaded.

Second, the verb of verse 21 must be reported accurately, because a careless reader will assume the energeo family and a verbal link to Ephesians 1:11 ("him who works all things," unit 06) or Romans 8:28 (unit 05). There is none. The "working in us" of 13:21 is poion, the present participle of poieo ("to make, to do"), and the doing of God's will is likewise poiesai, an infinitive of the same verb. The verb energeo does not occur in Hebrews at all; the epistle's only word from that root is the adjective energes, "effective," describing the word of God at 4:12. Part II may still set 13:21 beside Ephesians 1:11 conceptually (in both, God effects what pleases him in his people through Christ), but it must label that a theological synthesis, not a verbal echo, and it must note that Hebrews' chosen verb is the ordinary verb of making and doing, applied to God producing in believers the very obedience the benediction asks him to equip them for.

Third, the petition's shape matters for how verse 20 is used. The benediction asks God to equip the readers for doing his will on the ground of what God has already done in raising the shepherd. The eternal covenant of verse 20 is thus introduced as the ground of present sanctification, not as a locus for speculation about eternity; that pastoral placement does not settle what the phrase denotes, but it fixes the order of the author's interests, and Part II should keep it.

## 3. Grammar and word studies

### 3.1 Diatheke (Strong's G1242), and the word's profile in Hebrews

Thayer distinguishes two senses: (1) "a disposition, arrangement of any sort, which one wishes to be valid," specifically "the last disposition which one makes of his earthly possessions after his death, a testament or will"; (2) "a compact, covenant," the sense the word bears "very often in the Scriptures" for the Hebrew berith, denoting the relation God entered into with Noah, with the patriarchs, and with Israel (Thayer's printed references for the sense begin at Genesis 6:18; 9:9; Leviticus 26:42 in the LXX). Abbott-Smith's entry runs parallel: sense 1, "as usually in classical Greek, a disposition, testament, will" (Galatians 3:15; Hebrews 9:16, 17); sense 2, "as in the LXX (for berith), a convention, arrangement, covenant," used "always between God and man," with a note quoted from Moulton and Milligan that the dia- compound may have been preferred to syntheke (a treaty between parties meeting as near-equals) precisely because it suits a covenant God disposes. Abbott-Smith's citation list for sense 2 includes Hebrews 13:20 expressly.

The distribution is the epistle's own argument in numbers. Diatheke occurs 33 times in the New Testament, 17 of them in Hebrews (concordance count as displayed in the consulted sources). Within Hebrews the word's field is fixed by the letter's great contrast: the first covenant made obsolete (8:6-13, quoting Jeremiah 31), the new covenant of which Jesus is mediator and surety (7:22; 9:15; 12:24), its ratification in blood (9:18-22), and the one local wordplay where the testament sense is activated, 9:16-17, where the argument about a death's necessity briefly exploits sense 1 before returning to sense 2 at 9:18-20. At 13:20 no such wordplay is in motion: the diction is blood, shepherd, resurrection, and Abbott-Smith files the verse under the covenant sense without qualification. [Explicit text / lexical, HIGH]

### 3.2 Aionios (Strong's G166)

From aion (G165), an age. Strong's: "perpetual (also used of past time, or past and future as well): eternal, for ever, everlasting." Thayer's outline of usage (as reproduced at Blue Letter Bible): "1. without beginning and end, that which always has been and always will be; 2. without beginning; 3. without end, never to cease, everlasting." The question for 13:20 is which of these the adjective contributes at this slot, and Abbott-Smith's entry answers it directly in the course of its sense division: aionios is (a) "of that which is without either beginning or end" (Romans 16:26, of God; Hebrews 9:14, of the Spirit); (b) "of that which is without beginning" (Romans 16:25; 2 Timothy 1:9; Titus 1:2); (c) "of that which is without end," and the examples given under (c) include, in so many words, "diatheke, Hebrews 13:20," alongside lytrosis ("eternal redemption," Hebrews 9:12) and kleronomia ("eternal inheritance," Hebrews 9:15). The consulted lexica therefore classify this occurrence on the endless side: the covenant of 13:20 is eternal in the sense that it will never cease, never be revoked, never be superseded. That Hebrews can use the same adjective on the beginningless side when its subject requires it (9:14, "the eternal Spirit") only sharpens the point: the classification of 13:20 under "without end" is a judgment about this covenant, not a limitation of the word. Any Part II claim that the covenant of 13:20 is beginningless must therefore be carried by theological synthesis from other texts, not by the adjective at this occurrence. [Lexical verdict, HIGH for the lexica's classification; the discipline it imposes on Part II is this study's stated inference]

### 3.3 The phrase "diathekes aioniou" and its Old Testament field

The genitive phrase is Hebrews' only "eternal covenant," and the New Testament's only one; but it is not coined here. It is the Septuagint's standing equivalent for the Hebrew berith olam, "everlasting covenant": attested in the consulted display at Exodus 31:16, where the Sabbath is a sign "throughout their generations, a diatheke aionios," and standing behind the Old Testament's whole everlasting-covenant register (Genesis 9:16; 17:7; Isaiah 55:3; 2 Samuel 23:5, David's "everlasting covenant, ordered in all things, and sure," quoted in the Owen sermon material consulted for section 5). The register's most concentrated member is Ezekiel 37:26, where the two phrases of Hebrews 13:20 meet in one verse two verses after the shepherd promise: "David my servant shall be king over them, and they shall all have one shepherd" (37:24), then "I will make a covenant of peace with them; it shall be an everlasting covenant with them" (37:26, berith shalom; berith olam yihyeh otam in the Hebrew display consulted). God of peace, one shepherd, everlasting covenant: the whole lexical cluster of Hebrews 13:20 stands together in Ezekiel 37:24-26, and at the level of phrase-stock the resonance is real. What kind of resonance is adjudicated in section 3.6: the words travel as the LXX's common covenant idiom, not as a quotation, and Hebrews 13:20 nowhere signals that Ezekiel is being cited.

### 3.4 Anagagon (anago, Strong's G321) ek nekron

Anagagon is the aorist active participle, nominative masculine singular, of anago, "to lead or bring up." Abbott-Smith: "to lead or bring up... of raising the dead (classical usage), ek nekron, Romans 10:7, Hebrews 13:20." The participle is aorist: the raising is presented as one completed act of God, the benediction's foundational fact. Two precisions follow. First, the verb is the exodus verb family: anago is what God does when he brings Israel up out of Egypt in the LXX's idiom, and its compound anabibazo is the verb of Isaiah 63:11 (section 3.6); the choice of a "bringing up" verb, rather than the standard resurrection verbs egeiro or anistemi (both absent here), lets the resurrection be heard as a second exodus, a leading up out of the realm of the dead. Second, ek nekron is "from among the dead ones," plural: Jesus is brought up out of the company of the dead, which is why several versions print "from the dead" with "the dead" as a place-like collective. [Explicit text / lexical, HIGH]

### 3.5 En haimati: attachment and sense

The phrase en haimati diathekes aioniou stands in the Greek after ton megan ("the great one") and before the delayed naming of the object, ton kyrion hemon Iesoun. Two attachments have been defended.

(i) **With the resurrection participle**: the God who brought up the shepherd did so by, or in, the blood of the eternal covenant. This is the construal of all six versions compared in section 1 and of the mainstream of commentary. Abbott-Smith's entry on en supports the sense: en with the dative serves "of the agent, instrument or means," with the expressly Semitic allied usage instanced from sacrificial contexts, "en haimati, Hebrews 9:22" ("without shedding of blood there is no forgiveness" turns on the same construction), and "en haimati, Hebrews 9:25" under the sense of accompanying circumstance. On this attachment the verse says something theologically exact: the resurrection was not merely after the blood but by means of it; the covenant blood is the instrument in virtue of which God raised the shepherd (the adjudication of what that means belongs to section 5 and finding 7).

(ii) **With the shepherd title**: Barnes's note on the verse proposes that in the Greek the phrase "is not connected... with his being raised from the dead" but with the shepherd's greatness: he was "made or constituted the great Shepherd of the sheep... by that blood," asking, "what can be the sense of 'raising Christ from the dead by the blood of the covenant?'" The proposal is syntactically available (the phrase does follow megan) and it is not frivolous: something in the verse must say wherein the shepherd's greatness consists. But it asks the en phrase to do constitutive work across the intervening article structure (ton poimena ton probaton ton megan is one articular unit, "the shepherd of the sheep, the great one"), it leaves anagagon with no instrument where the sentence's only candidate instrument stands unused, and the puzzle Barnes raises is answerable on attachment (i) without strain, as section 5 shows Owen answering it: if the blood made full satisfaction and confirmed the covenant's promises, the resurrection follows in God's purpose as the covenant's own first consequence, and "by the blood" is the natural way to say it. This study adopts attachment (i). [Necessary inference, MEDIUM-HIGH: the grammar permits both, the versions and the epistle's own en haimati usage (9:22, 25) carry (i)]

### 3.6 Poimen ton probaton ho megas: the title and its echoes, graded

Poimen (Strong's G4166) in Abbott-Smith: "a shepherd... metaphorical, of Christ: John 10:11, 14, 16; Hebrews 13:20; 1 Peter 2:25; of Christian pastors, Ephesians 4:11." The New Testament's shepherd titles for Christ form a graded set: the good shepherd (ho poimen ho kalos, John 10:11, 14), the shepherd and overseer of souls (1 Peter 2:25), the chief shepherd (ho archipoimen, 1 Peter 5:4), and here alone the great shepherd, with megas in its rank sense (Abbott-Smith on megas, sense 3, "of rank; of persons": the same adjective Hebrews gives the "great high priest" of 4:14, so that the epistle's priest and its shepherd are both "great"). The construction ton poimena ton probaton ton megan repeats the article before the adjective, as at John 10:11 (ho poimen ho kalos): "the shepherd of the sheep, the great one," a titular fullness, not a passing description. The echoes behind the clause are not all of one kind, and the study grades them rather than gathering them into a single claim of allusion.

**Zechariah 9:11: lexical, the closest verbal parallel in the sentence.** The LXX reads: "kai su en haimati diathekes sou exapestalas desmious sou ek lakkou ouk echontos hydor," "and you, by the blood of your covenant, sent forth your prisoners from a pit without water." The three-word sequence en haimati diathekes ("by the blood of [the] covenant") is verbally identical to the core of Hebrews' phrase, sou ("your") standing where Hebrews sets aioniou. In Zechariah the blood is the Sinai covenant's sacrificial blood and the release is from exile's waterless pit; in Hebrews the blood is Christ's and the release is a resurrection. The shared wording is exact; the referents are transformed. [Lexical echo, HIGH for the verbal identity; the typological transfer is the author's theological act, tagged in finding 8]

**Isaiah 63:11 LXX: lexical kinship, one step removed.** The verse recalls the exodus: "Where is he who brought up from the sea the shepherd of the sheep (ton poimena ton probaton)?" So Brenton's translation of the LXX, and Clarke's note confirms the versional facts: at Isaiah 63:11 the Hebrew consonants are read in the plural ("shepherds") by most witnesses, but "the Septuagint, Syriac, and Chaldee" read the singular, the shepherd being Moses. In the LXX's form, then, the Old Testament already contains "the shepherd of the sheep" as the object of a divine bringing-up, and the collocation with an anago-family verb (anabibazo, "brought up") matches Hebrews clause for clause in structure: God brings up; the one brought up is the shepherd of the sheep. The verbs differ (anabibazo against anago), the element brought up from differs (the sea against the dead), and the shepherd differs (Moses against Jesus, who is megas, great, where Moses is not). This study records the kinship as strong and structural, not a verbatim quotation. [Lexical-thematic echo, MEDIUM-HIGH]

**Ezekiel 37:24-26: thematic convergence with shared phrase-stock.** One shepherd (David), covenant of peace, everlasting covenant (section 3.3). Every substantive word of Hebrews 13:20 except "blood" and "dead" is present in Ezekiel's oracle, but the combination is Ezekiel's restoration promise read at the level of the canon, not a citation: Hebrews gives no quotation formula and alters every referent (the shepherd is not David but the Davidic Lord, raised, not enthroned in the land). [Thematic echo, MEDIUM]

**Exodus 24:8: mediated, not direct.** "Behold, the blood of the covenant" (Exodus 24:8) is quoted by Hebrews itself at 9:20 ("this is the blood of the covenant," as Owen's exposition notes: the blood of Christ "so called in answer to the blood of the beasts, which was offered and sprinkled in the confirmation of the old covenant; whence it is by Moses called the blood of the covenant, Exodus 24:8; Hebrews 9:20"). At 13:20 the Exodus text is present only through the epistle's own earlier quotation; the echo reaches the benediction at one remove, through Hebrews 9, and through the institution words that stand behind both (Matthew 26:28; Mark 14:24; Luke 22:20, the standard cross-reference set for this clause). [Mediated echo, MEDIUM]

**"The God of peace": benediction formula with covenant resonance, thematic only.** Ho theos tes eirenes is Paul's benediction title (Romans 15:33; 16:20; 2 Corinthians 13:11; Philippians 4:9; 1 Thessalonians 5:23; 2 Thessalonians 3:16, the cross-reference set as displayed in the consulted sources); Hebrews uses it only here. That the God so named is here the God of the covenant of peace's own book (Ezekiel 34:25; 37:26; compare Numbers 25:12) is a resonance a reader may register but the text does not press. [Thematic resonance, LOW]

## 4. Findings

1. **The benediction's grammar makes God the sole actor and Jesus the raised object.** Verse 20 is a participial description of God ("the God of peace, the one who brought up..."), and the sentence's only finite verb is the petition of verse 21. God raises; the shepherd is raised; the readers are equipped. Any Part II use that redistributes these roles has left the text. [Explicit text, HIGH]
2. **The "working" of 13:21 is poieo, not energeo.** There is no verbal link between Hebrews 13:21 and Ephesians 1:11 or Romans 8:28; energeo does not occur in Hebrews, whose only energe- word is energes at 4:12. The parallel between the texts is real but conceptual, and must be tagged as synthesis wherever Part II draws it. [Explicit text, HIGH]
3. **God raised Jesus from the dead: so the verse asserts, in the aorist, as the benediction's ground.** Anagagon is lexically a bringing up, and Abbott-Smith classes its use here under "raising the dead" (with Romans 10:7). The versions' "again" and "back" are supplied, correctly, from the ana- prefix and the sense; the text's own claim is the bringing up itself. [Explicit text, HIGH]
4. **The raising was by the blood of the covenant.** En haimati attaches to anagagon (section 3.5) in the instrumental sense the preposition bears in this epistle's sacrificial diction (9:22, 25): the resurrection is presented as effected in virtue of the covenant blood, not merely subsequent to it. Barnes's alternative attachment to the shepherd title is recorded and declined with reasons. [Necessary inference, MEDIUM-HIGH]
5. **The covenant whose blood this is, is the new covenant of Hebrews' own argument.** Diatheke's field in the epistle is the first/new contrast of chapters 8-10 and 12; the blood is the blood that ratified the new covenant (9:15-20), that sanctified the people at 13:12, and that speaks at 12:24. No other covenant is in play anywhere in the letter, and 13:20 introduces no new one. [Necessary inference from the epistle's usage, HIGH]
6. **That covenant is eternal in the sense of endless and irrevocable, and the adjective at this occurrence does not lexically say more.** Abbott-Smith files diatheke at Hebrews 13:20 under aionios "without end" (section 3.2), in company with the eternal redemption (9:12) and eternal inheritance (9:15) the same blood secures. Beginninglessness is a sense aionios bears elsewhere (Romans 16:26; Hebrews 9:14) but is not the sense the lexica assign here. [Lexical verdict, HIGH for the classification; MEDIUM-HIGH as a limit on Part II's use, since lexicographical classification is itself an interpretive act]
7. **The resurrection is depicted as a covenant act: God's performance, toward the Mediator himself, of what the covenant blood secured.** This is the verse's own logic on findings 4 and 5 read together, and it is how the tradition's greatest expositor of Hebrews read it. Owen's exposition of this verse asks precisely Barnes's question, "how God is said to bring Christ from the dead through the blood of the covenant," and answers: by that blood "atonement was made for sin... the promises of the new covenant confirmed," all grace "purchased for the church, and in the purpose of God was necessarily to ensue," so that the raising of Christ, "the head and well-spring of the whole dispensation of grace," could not have failed once the blood had done its work; had satisfaction not been made, Christ "must have continued under the power of death." On this reading, which the study adopts, the Father's raising of the Son is the covenant's first executed consequence, rendered to the shepherd before it flows to the sheep. [Necessary inference, MEDIUM-HIGH: the instrumental attachment is finding 4; the "rendered to the Mediator" step is inference from the object named (the shepherd, not the sheep, is what the blood raises), and Owen's exposition is mediation, labeled in section 7]
8. **The echo structure is graded, and its strongest member is Zechariah 9:11.** En haimati diathekes is verbally identical at Zechariah 9:11 LXX and Hebrews 13:20 (HIGH as verbal fact); the Isaiah 63:11 LXX shepherd collocation is one step removed (MEDIUM-HIGH); Ezekiel 37:24-26 supplies the phrase-stock cluster thematically (MEDIUM); Exodus 24:8 reaches the verse through Hebrews 9:20 (MEDIUM); the God of peace / covenant of peace resonance is LOW. Part II must cite each echo at its own grade and not borrow Zechariah's grade for Ezekiel's.
9. **What this verse alone cannot carry for Part II, stated as limits.** (a) It names no covenanting parties in eternity: the covenant of 13:20, in the epistle's argument, runs between God and the people whose shepherd was raised, and no second party, stipulation, or pretemporal transaction is mentioned. (b) It does not call the covenant beginningless (finding 6). (c) Its diction is covenantal, not decretal: it can witness to the eternal counsel only as covenant language, and Part II's decree vocabulary (unit 06) must not be read back into it. (d) It states no distinction between a covenant of redemption and a covenant of grace; that distinction, if Part II uses it, is systematic, and this verse is one of its chain members, not its source. [Explicit text for the absences, HIGH: these are silences in a verse whose positive contents findings 1-8 inventory]

## 5. Competing readings

1. **The new-covenant reading: 13:20 refers to the new covenant only, and any covenant-of-redemption use is systematic overlay.** Strongest form: Hebrews' diatheke argument from chapter 8 onward recognizes exactly two covenants, the first and the new; the blood of 13:20 is the new covenant's ratifying blood (9:15-20) shed in history (13:12); aionios qualifies that covenant as the final, unending one (finding 6); the benediction addresses the covenant's beneficiaries and asks their equipping. On this reading the verse's "eternal" looks forward (the covenant will never be superseded, as the first was) and not backward, and the pactum salutis must find its proof texts elsewhere. This is, in substance, the reading the modern critical commentaries assume, and it is Owen's own reading at the level of the verse: his exposition treats the blood as confirming the (new) covenant and purchasing its graces, and locates the resurrection downstream of the satisfaction. *Adjudication.* Accepted as the verse's primary and explicit sense, at HIGH confidence: findings 5 and 6 are this reading's content. Its further claim, however, that the pactum use is therefore illegitimate overlay, does not follow from the verse; it follows only if the mediating chain of reading 3 fails on its other links, which are outside this unit's jurisdiction. The study accepts the reading and declines its veto.
2. **The direct pactum reading: 13:20 is itself a witness to a pretemporal intra-Trinitarian covenant.** Strongest form: the covenant is called eternal without qualification, and a covenant whose blood could be shed in time yet be named eternal before its ratification (as this benediction, written after the resurrection, names it looking back over the whole) is most naturally the covenant in its eternal being, not merely its endless duration; the resurrection "by the blood" presupposes an arrangement under which the Father was bound to raise the Son upon the blood's shedding, and a binding of God toward the Son antecedent to the shedding is precisely the covenant of redemption, of which the new covenant in time is the execution toward sinners. The tradition that so used the verse is weighty and self-aware: Owen, who wrote the era's largest Hebrews exposition, elsewhere names the Father-Son arrangement "the eternal covenant, compact or counsel between the Father and the Son," and the secondary scholarship on Owen and Gillespie describes the covenant of redemption as, for both, "the intra-Trinitarian foundation of the Covenant of Grace," grounded in "the eternal counsels of the persons of the Trinity... having the features of a covenant and including Christ's distinct personal concurrence." *Adjudication.* Declined as an account of what this verse directly denotes, at MEDIUM-HIGH confidence in the declination. Three reasons. (i) The lexical one: aionios at this slot is classified by the consulted lexica as "without end," and a beginningless sense must be imported against the classification (finding 6). (ii) The epistolary one: Hebrews knows no third covenant alongside the first and the new, and reading 13:20's phrase as naming an otherwise unmentioned pretemporal covenant asks the benediction to introduce, in its last sentence, a doctrine the letter never states (finding 5). (iii) The grammatical one: the verse names no parties and no transaction; everything the direct reading needs (a Father who stipulates, a Son who undertakes, terms, a date before time) is absent from a clause whose actual contents are God, the dead, the shepherd, the blood, and a covenant adjective. What the reading rightly senses, the location of the resurrection inside a divine commitment antecedent to it, is preserved entire by reading 3 without the overclaim.
3. **The mediating reading: the verse's own claim is resurrection by the new covenant's blood, and the pactum use is a legitimate necessary-inference chain through other texts.** On this reading, Hebrews 13:20 contributes to the doctrine of the eternal covenant exactly two things, and they are things no other single text contributes in one clause: first, the covenant whose blood was shed is named eternal, so that the historical ratification participates in something the benediction can call by eternity's name (finding 6, within its lexical limit); second, the resurrection itself is an act performed en that blood, so that the Father's dealing with the Son at the tomb is covenantal dealing, the covenant executed Godward before it is applied manward (finding 7). The remaining links of the chain are not this verse's to supply, and the study names them so Part II can see what the chain costs: Psalm 2:7-8, the decree ("You are my Son") with the grant conditioned on asking ("Ask of me, and I will make the nations your heritage"), which gives the Son a promised reward on stated terms; Psalm 89, the sworn covenant with David and his seed forever; Isaiah 53:10-12, where the servant who pours out his soul to death is promised seed, prolonged days, and a portion with the strong, the reward structure on which the resurrection-by-the-blood becomes intelligible as covenant wages; Zechariah 6:13, the priest-king on his throne with "the counsel of peace between the two"; John 6:38-40 (unit 08), the Son who came down not to do his own will but the sender's, charged to lose nothing of what the Father gave him and to raise it at the last day; and John 17:4-6, the work finished and the given ones kept, rendered back to the Father as an account. With those links in place, the inference to a pretemporal covenantal arrangement between the Father and the Son is the tradition's synthesis, and Hebrews 13:20 is a genuine member of its evidence: the member that shows the arrangement being executed at the resurrection. *Adjudication.* Accepted as the disciplined form of the verse's pactum use. [Assessment: accepted, MEDIUM: the chain's exegetical members are as graded in this study and unit 08; the chain's systematic sufficiency is a Phase 2 judgment this study prepares but does not render]
4. **The testament reading: diatheke at 13:20 should be heard as "testament" or "will."** Hebrews itself activates that sense at 9:16-17, and Galatians 3:15 keeps it in view. Declined here, briefly: nothing in 13:20's diction (blood, shepherd, resurrection, peace) engages the death-of-the-testator logic of 9:16-17; the sacrificial and Exodus associations run entirely on the covenant side; and Abbott-Smith files the verse under the covenant sense (section 3.1). A residual nuance may be kept for Part II without building on it: diatheke in its classical core is a disposition one party lays down, which is why the LXX chose it for berith, and the covenant of 13:20 is throughout something God disposes and God performs (finding 1). [Assessment: declined as a rendering, HIGH; the disposition nuance retained as lexical background]

## 6. Textual notes

**13:20 itself is textually stable.** In the sources consulted for this study, no variant at verse 20 is recorded: the NET notes, which annotate this chapter's real variants (13:6; 13:21 twice; 13:25), print no textual note at 13:20; the critical text and the Textus Receptus agree in the interlinear displays consulted; and the consulted variant summary for the verse flags no text-critical issue. The words on which this study's findings rest (anagagon ek nekron; en haimati diathekes aioniou) are not in dispute in any witness the study could reach. Confidence HIGH within the consulted summaries; the NA28 apparatus was not read directly (section 8).

**13:21, en hemin ("in us") against en humin ("in you").** The benediction's "working in us" is itself a variant. NET's textual note: some manuscripts (C P Psi 6 629* 630 1505 pm latt sy-h) read humin, "in you," but hemin "has stronger external support (P46 Aleph A D-vid K 0243 0285 33 81 104 326 365 629c 1175 1739 1881 pm sy-p co). It is also more likely that hemin would have been changed to humin in light of the 'you' which occurs at the beginning of the verse than vice versa." The study follows hemin: the author includes himself inside the working he prays for, which fits finding 2 (the benediction's God works the obedience in the whole company, writer included). [Textual verdict, MEDIUM-HIGH on the NET note's evidence]

**13:21, "forever and ever" against "forever."** NET's second textual note at the verse: most manuscripts (Aleph A [C*] 0243 0285 33 1739 1881 M latt) include "and ever" in the doxology, but the shorter reading (P46 C3 D Psi 6 104 365 1505 al) is preferred on internal grounds, scribes being likelier to assimilate to the common doxological "for ever and ever" than the reverse; "nevertheless, a decision is difficult here," and NA28 prints the phrase in brackets. Nothing in this study rests on the doxology's length.

**A translation note, not a variant.** NET prints "our Lord Jesus Christ" at 13:20; the Greek text in both its critical and received forms reads only ton kyrion hemon Iesoun, "our Lord Jesus," and the versions that print "Jesus our Lord" (NASB) or "our Lord Jesus" (the rest) exhaust the text. NET's "Christ" is a supplied expansion; the study notes it only so that Part II, quoting NET, does not mistake it for a reading.
