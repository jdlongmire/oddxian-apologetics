## 1. Text and translation

The verse, in transliteration: "ou gar echomen archierea me dynamenon sympathesai tais astheneiais hemon, pepeirasmenon de kata panta kath' homoioteta choris hamartias." Literally: "For we do not have a high priest unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who has been tempted in all things according to likeness, apart from sin." The sentence is built as a double negative turned positive: not an unable priest, but a tempted one. The negative (me dynamenon, "not being able") states the disqualification the readers might fear of a priest who has "passed through the heavens" (4:14), and the adversative de removes it: the ground of his sympathy is not a concession wrung from distance but a completed experience, stated in the perfect tense (section 3.1) and bounded at exactly one point, the closing choris hamartias (section 3.4).

Translation comparison for Hebrews 4:15:

| Version | Sympathesai phrase | Temptation phrase | Closing phrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| KJV | cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities | was in all points tempted like as we are | yet without sin |
| ESV | is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses | one who in every respect has been tempted as we are | yet without sin |
| NASB | cannot sympathize with our weaknesses | One who has been tempted in all things as we are | yet without sin |
| NIV | is unable to empathize with our weaknesses | has been tempted in every way, just as we are | yet he did not sin |
| NET | incapable of sympathizing with our weaknesses | has been tempted in every way just as we are | yet without sin |
| CSB | is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses | has been tempted in every way as we are | yet without sin |

Three translation facts matter. First, five of the six render the participle with the English perfect ("has been tempted"), preserving the Greek perfect's abiding-result force; the KJV's "was... tempted" rests on the same perfect in its underlying text (section 6) but flattens it in English, and readers of the KJV alone can miss that the verse predicates a standing state, not only a past episode. Second, the NIV alone converts the closing prepositional phrase into a finite clause, "yet he did not sin": a rendering that decides, in advance and in one direction, the scope question of section 3.4, since a clause about what he did not do speaks to the temptation's outcome, while the Greek phrase, left as a phrase, covers whatever it is allowed to govern. The other five keep "without sin" and leave the scope where the Greek leaves it. Third, no version weakens any of the three load-bearing elements: the sympathy is real (even the KJV's paraphrase "touched with the feeling" reaches for fellow-feeling), the scope is total ("all points," "every respect," "all things," "every way"), and the exception is absolute ("without sin" in every rendering).

## 2. Context

The verse is the hinge of the epistle's first great high-priest paragraph (4:14-5:10) and draws its force from controls the epistle itself supplies on both sides.

**4:14-16, the immediate frame.** Verse 14: "echontes oun archierea megan dielelythota tous ouranous Iesoun ton huion tou theou kratoumen tes homologias" - "Since then we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus the Son of God, let us hold fast the confession." Dielelythota is itself a perfect participle: his passage through the heavens is a completed act with an abiding result, which is precisely what raises the sympathy question verse 15 answers. The exalted one is not therefore remote, because the same verse 15 predicates of him the other perfect, pepeirasmenon. Verse 16 draws the pastoral conclusion the whole construction exists to support: "let us then with confidence (meta parresias) draw near to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need." The doctrine of 4:15 is thus not speculative Christology; in the epistle's own argument it is the stated ground on which wavering readers (the letter's warning passages stand all around, 3:12-4:13 before and 5:11-6:12 after) may approach God. Any reading of the verse that undercuts the approach - a priest too distant to feel, or too compromised to help - contradicts the use the author makes of it.

**2:17-18, the foundation.** "hothen opheilen kata panta tois adelphois homoiothenai, hina eleemon genetai kai pistos archiereus ta pros ton theon" - "Therefore he had to be made like his brothers in every respect (kata panta), so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest in the service of God." The kata panta of 4:15 is this kata panta come back: the total likeness purposed in 2:17 is the total temptation undergone in 4:15. Verse 18 gives the epistle's own logic of sympathy: "en ho gar peponthen autos peirastheis, dynatai tois peirazomenois boethesai" - "for because he himself has suffered (peponthen, perfect) when he was tempted (peirastheis, aorist), he is able to help (boethesai, to run to a cry for aid) those who are being tempted (tois peirazomenois, present)." The tense network is the argument: his tempting is narrated as event (aorist) and confessed as abiding possession (perfect, 4:15); our tempting is present and ongoing; the help runs from his completed experience to our unfinished one. Nothing in this logic conditions his ability to help on his having been able to fall (section 5.1).

**5:1-10, the frame completed.** The human high priest of the old order is "able to deal gently (metriopathein) with the ignorant and wayward, since he himself is beset with weakness (perikeitai astheneian)" and must therefore "offer sacrifice for his own sins just as he does for those of the people" (5:2-3). Two contrasts with 4:15 are built into the vocabulary. The old priest's fellow-feeling is grounded in shared moral failure and capped by it (he deals gently because he too sins, and he can go no further than offering for sins, his own included); Christ's sympathy (sympathesai, a stronger word than metriopathein) is grounded in shared experience of temptation while the shared failure is expressly excluded. And the astheneia that surrounds (perikeitai) the Levitical priest is in 4:15 predicated of us (tais astheneiais hemon) and sympathized with, never predicated of Christ (section 3.3). Verses 7-9 then give the epistle's own portrait of the temptation's cost: prayers and supplications "with loud cries and tears" to the one able to save him from death, a hearing granted "because of his reverence" (apo tes eulabeias) without the cup being removed; "although he was a Son, he learned obedience through what he suffered" (emathen aph' hon epathen ten hypakoen); "and being made perfect (teleiotheis), he became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him." Obedience in this portrait is learned experientially, at cost, through suffering undergone and not escaped: the epistle's internal answer, written before the objection was framed, to the charge that a sinless temptation must be an unreal one. The passage is also the letter's commentary on Gethsemane and binds unit 07's findings to this one.

**7:26-28, the control text.** Treated in full in section 3.5: the abiding separation from sinners that turns 4:15's fact into a state, and the once-for-all offering that presupposes a priest with no sins of his own to offer for.

## 3. Grammar and word studies

### 3.1 Peirazo, peirasmos, and the perfect participle pepeirasmenon

Peirazo (Strong's G3985; Thayer, s.v.) ranges across three senses the context must choose among: to try or test, to ascertain quality (in a good sense, John 6:6; in a malicious sense, the trap-questions of Matthew 16:1; 22:18); to try one's faith, virtue, or character by enticement to sin, "to solicit to sin, to tempt" (Thayer's sense 2c, citing James 1:13; Galatians 6:1; Revelation 2:10; and of the devil's temptations, Matthew 4:1, 3; Mark 1:13; Luke 4:2); and, after the Old Testament usage, of God's proving his people by affliction. The noun peirasmos (G3986) is "a putting to proof," whether as experiment of good, experience of evil, solicitation, discipline, or provocation. The lexical fact that governs everything below is negative: neither the verb nor the noun encodes anything about the outcome's openness. Peirazo names an assault, a trial, a solicitation directed at a subject; whether the subject could yield is a question the word does not ask, let alone answer. A test that cannot be failed by the one tested is not, lexically, any less a test: the wilderness testing of Israel, God's own peirasmos of Abraham (Genesis 22:1, in the Septuagint's verb), and the devil's assault on Job all proceed without the lexeme pausing to certify that failure was metaphysically available. Claims that real temptation requires a real possibility of sinning therefore arrive at this verse from outside its vocabulary (section 5.1).

The form in 4:15 is pepeirasmenon, perfect passive participle, accusative masculine singular, agreeing with archierea: "one who has been tempted." A precision is required here, because the form is easily misread as an aorist (peirasthenta would be the aorist passive participle; it is not what any edition prints). Thayer's own principal-parts note for peirazo lists "perfect participle pepeirasmenos (Hebrews 4:15)," and the critical texts agree: the Nestle 1904 and SBLGNT 2010 texts of the verse are identical, and the Byzantine tradition reads the same perfect from the sister stem peirao (pepeiramenon; section 6). The perfect's force is the standard one: a past event whose result stands in the present. The temptation is finished as episode and unfinished as qualification: he remains, now, in the exalted state of verse 14, the one who has been through it. That is why the English versions reach for "has been tempted," and why the verse can ground a present sympathy and a present approach (4:16): the priest's credential is not a memory but a state. Hebrews itself sets the contrast at 2:18, where the same verb appears as an aorist participle (peirastheis) for the event ("when he was tempted") beside a perfect of suffering (peponthen): the epistle can narrate the tempting as event when it wants to, and at 4:15 it chooses instead to confess it as abiding condition. The choice is the author's, and Part VII should let it bear the weight he put on it. [Explicit text, HIGH]

### 3.2 Kata panta kath' homoioteta: the scope of the likeness

Kata panta is "in all things, in every respect" (the KJV's "in all points" catches the distributive force: point by point). Kath' homoioteta is "according to likeness": homoiotes (G3665, from homoios) is resemblance, likeness, similitude, and the kata phrase marks the standard or manner - the tempting ran according to the likeness between his condition and ours. The exegetical notes consulted (summarizing the TDNT entry under homoios) record the standard observation that the phrase carries a double nuance: it describes the manner of the temptations (he was tempted in the same way we are, under the same human conditions) and it rests on the ground of them (he could be so tempted because of his likeness to us, the likeness confessed programmatically at 2:17, where the verb is homoioo and the scope is again kata panta, "made like his brothers in every respect"). The study adopts both nuances in that order: ground first (2:17), manner as its consequence (4:15).

Two scope disciplines follow. First, kata panta distributes over kinds, not over individual instances: the verse claims that no category of human temptation lay outside his experience - temptation through bodily appetite and weakness, through pain and the fear of death, through the desire to escape appointed suffering, through assault from without by the devil and by men - not that he rehearsed every biographical species of temptation that every century invents. The claim is totality of respect, not an inventory of episodes; this is the sense in which "in every respect" has always been read, and the likeness term itself (homoiotes, resemblance, not tautotes, identity) builds the discipline into the grammar: resemblance is likeness in kind across difference of circumstance. [Necessary inference, HIGH: from the distributive kata, the likeness noun, and the 2:17 parallel] Second, the likeness so scoped is the epistle's own anti-docetic guard: whatever is said below about the exception, the sentence's main clause is an unqualified kata panta, and the exception is handled by its own phrase, not by shrinking this one. Readings that protect the exception by quietly thinning the likeness (a temptation undergone in a condition only resembling ours at a distance) reverse the sentence's own proportions.

### 3.3 Sympathesai and astheneia

Sympatheo (G4834) is to have a fellow feeling with, to sympathize: from syn, with, and pascho, to suffer or be affected - a feeling that suffers alongside (Abbott-Smith: "to have a fellow feeling for or with, sympathize with or in," with the construction at 4:15 noted as dative of the thing, "c. dat. rei, He 4:15," and at 10:34 dative of the person). The verb occurs in the New Testament only in this epistle: here, as the infinitive sympathesai inside the double negative, and at 10:34, where the readers "sympathized with (synepathesate) the prisoners" - sympathy in Hebrews is not a sentiment but an enacted solidarity, there with chains and plundered goods, here with weakness. The KJV's "touched with the feeling of our infirmities" is a paraphrase reaching for exactly this: not pity observed from a height but a feeling had in common.

Astheneia (G769) is feebleness, want of strength: of body, sickness and infirmity; figuratively, incapacity and moral frailty (Strong's; Friberg, as quoted in the commentary material consulted: "as a state of incapacity, weakness, impotence... figuratively, as a quality of character, weakness"). In Hebrews the word occurs at 4:15 (our weaknesses, the object of his sympathy), 5:2 (the Levitical priest beset with it), 7:28 (the men the law appoints as priests are "having weakness," echontas astheneian, in direct contrast to the Son "perfected forever"), and 11:34 (the faithful "were made strong out of weakness"). The distribution is itself an argument: astheneia is predicated of us, of the old priests, and of the heroes of faith, and never of Christ. He sympathizes with weaknesses he underwent the conditions of - hunger, exhaustion, pain, mortality, the whole catalogue of Shedd's "innocent defects and limitations of the finite" - without the word ever being applied to his own constitution. What he shares is the condition of weakness; what the verse will not say is that he shares the sin that in us is weakness's usual tenant. The boundary the epistle draws in vocabulary is the boundary the closing phrase draws in syntax.

### 3.4 Choris hamartias: what "apart from sin" governs

This is the study's assigned crux. Choris (G5565) is an adverb turned preposition (from chora, a space or place): "apart, separately," and with the genitive, "apart from, without, separately from." Hamartias is the genitive of hamartia (G266), sin. The question is one of scope: does the phrase qualify the likeness (he was tempted in every respect as we are, except that the likeness stops at sin), the temptation (the tempting itself was undergone apart from sin), or the whole complex at once, and what follows from each answer?

Four lines of evidence converge on the third answer.

**(a) Position.** The phrase stands last, closing the entire participial complex (pepeirasmenon kata panta kath' homoioteta choris hamartias), and Greek trailing modifiers of this kind naturally govern the whole of what they close unless the sense restricts them. Nothing in the word order interposes between the phrase and the participle, and nothing attaches it to homoioteta alone: it does not stand inside the kata panta kath' homoioteta phrase but after it. A restriction to the likeness alone must be imported; the unrestricted reading is the text's own surface. [Explicit text, HIGH]

**(b) The word's own semantics.** Choris does not merely report absence; it marks separation, a space between. The point is old in the commentary tradition: Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, citing Tittmann, note that if aneu (the simple "without") had been used, sin would be regarded as an object merely absent from the subject; choris instead presents the subject as separated from sin, apart from it across an interval, and Alford's summary in the same note gives the consequence: "throughout His temptations in their origin, process, and result, sin had nothing in Him; He was apart and separate from it." Bengel's dictum, quoted at the same place, states the resulting paradox the verse intends: he was "at the same time tempted without sin, and yet truly tempted." A preposition of separation, governing a complex that includes a process (being tempted) and a comparison (as we are), excludes sin from everything the complex contains: the comparison, the process, and the result alike.

**(c) The epistle's own usage of the phrase.** Choris hamartias occurs in the New Testament one other time, at Hebrews 9:28, again of Christ: "ek deuterou choris hamartias ophthesetai," he "will appear a second time, apart from sin, to those who eagerly wait for him." There the phrase cannot be an outcome qualifier on a process (nothing is being undergone at the parousia that might issue in sin); it states his objective apartness from sin as such - he comes not bearing sin (the first appearing's burden, 9:28a) but separated from it altogether. The phrase in this author's hand is a total-relation term: Christ stands apart from sin, full stop. Reading 4:15's identical phrase as though it merely reported a clean score at the end of the temptations gives the same two words a thinner sense in the earlier verse than the author himself gives them in the later one.

**(d) The cognate at 7:26.** Kechorismenos (section 3.5) is the perfect passive participle of chorizo (G5563), "to place room between, to separate," built on the same chora root as choris: Christ is confessed as one who stands separated (perfect: settled state) from sinners (apo ton hamartolon). Hebrews' Christology of sin is consistently drawn in this spatial vocabulary of apartness, in phrases (4:15), in states (7:26), and in comings (9:28). The study reads the three together, as the epistle's single coherent claim.

**What follows from each candidate scope, stated honestly.** If the phrase governed only the likeness, the claim would be: he is like us in every respect of temptation except that sin forms no part of the comparison - which already excludes sin from the temptation as he underwent it, since what is compared is his tempted experience against ours, and the excluded element is precisely the sin that in our experience accompanies it, including sin as inward motion (James 1:14's mechanism of one's own desire, epithymia, conceiving and giving birth to sin is, on the likeness-only reading, part of what the likeness excepts). If it governed only the temptation-event, the claim would be: the event of his being tempted proceeded and concluded apart from sin - excluding sin from its origin, process, and result (Alford), and thereby also marking the one point where the comparison to our tempted experience fails. The two scopes are not rivals that divide the territory; they are the same exclusion described from its two ends, and the whole-complex scope the grammar favors simply asserts both at once. What no scope yields is the restriction sometimes assumed in the debate: that the phrase certifies the outcome alone (he did not in the end sin) while leaving open whether the process of his temptation might have contained sin as an ingredient. On every grammatical option, sin is denied to something that includes the process; there is no reading of the phrase on which his temptation was partly constituted by sin and merely failed to end in it. The one genuine refinement the study adds to the tradition's formulation is a tagging caution: choris names sin, not desire as such; the specification that the excluded ingredient includes inward sinful desire (as distinct from the sinless natural recoils of unit 07) is secured by this phrase together with the canon (James 1:13-15's mechanism, which requires indwelling epithymia as temptation's internal engine in the fallen; 2 Corinthians 5:21, "knew no sin"; 1 John 3:5, "in him there is no sin"), and is tagged accordingly in the findings. [Necessary inference, HIGH for the whole-complex scope and for the exclusion of sin from process and outcome; the desire specification is treated in finding 5]

### 3.5 The 7:26 control: separation as a state, not only a fact

Hebrews 7:26: "toioutos gar hemin eprepen archiereus, hosios, akakos, amiantos, kechorismenos apo ton hamartolon kai hypseloteros ton ouranon genomenos" - "For it was fitting for us to have such a high priest: holy (hosios, G3741, devout, pious toward God), innocent (akakos, G172, without evil, guileless), undefiled (amiantos, G283, unsoiled, untainted), separated from sinners (kechorismenos apo ton hamartolon), and exalted above the heavens." Kechorismenos is the perfect passive participle of chorizo: not "was once separated" but "stands separated," a settled and abiding state, exactly the aspectual register of 4:15's pepeirasmenon. The two perfects belong together: the priest who has been tempted (4:15) is the priest who stands apart from sinners (7:26), and both are present possessions ("we have," 4:14-15; "it was fitting for us to have," 7:26). Verses 27-28 cash the state out. He has no need, "as those high priests, to offer sacrifices daily, first for his own sins (hyper ton idion hamartion) and then for those of the people, for this he did once for all (ephapax) when he offered up himself"; and where the law appoints priests men "having weakness," the word of the oath appoints "a Son who has been perfected forever" (huion eis ton aiona teteleiomenon, perfect passive participle of teleioo). The epistle's internal movement is therefore: 4:15 states the fact (tempted in full likeness, sin nowhere in it), 7:26 states the fact as abiding character (separated from sinners as what he is), and 7:27-28 builds the cultic consequence (no own-sin offering needed or possible; the once-for-all offering offered). Part VII may not treat 4:15 as an isolated proof-text when the author himself has written its dogmatic expansion three chapters later. [Explicit text, HIGH]

## 4. Findings

1. **The verse asserts real temptation, fully undergone.** A perfect passive participle predicated of the exalted priest: he has been tempted, the tempting was done to him and completed, and its result stands as his present qualification. Peirazo neither requires nor excludes the possibility of yielding; the reality asserted is the reality of the assault and its endurance, and 5:7-9 supplies the epistle's own evidence of its cost (loud cries, tears, obedience learned through suffering). [Explicit text, HIGH]
2. **The likeness is total across kinds and real in manner, on the ground of 2:17.** Kata panta with kath' homoioteta: every category of human temptation, undergone under genuinely our conditions, because he was made like his brothers kata panta. Totality distributes over respects, not over individual episodes (section 3.2). [Explicit text, HIGH; the kinds-not-instances discipline, Necessary inference, HIGH]
3. **Sympathy is grounded in undergone experience, and its stated logic nowhere includes a capacity to fall.** The double negative of 4:15, the tense network of 2:18 (his aorist event and perfect state against our present peirazomenois), and the pastoral conclusion of 4:16 all run the same way: because he has been through it, he can feel with us and help us. The verse's function (the ground of the readers' approach) is fully discharged by the completed experience; ability to sin is not named, implied, or needed anywhere in the argument. [Explicit text, HIGH for the grounding as stated; Necessary inference, HIGH for the exclusion of peccability from the grounding]
4. **Choris hamartias excludes sin from the whole temptation complex.** Position (it closes the whole participial complex), semantics (choris marks separation, not mere absence), the phrase's only other New Testament occurrence (9:28, total apartness), and the chora-root cognate at 7:26 converge: sin is excluded from the comparison, from the process of his being tempted, and from its outcome, all at once. No scope of the phrase permits a temptation partly constituted by sin. [Necessary inference, HIGH]
5. **The exclusion reaches temptation's inward mode as well as its result.** For us, temptation's characteristic internal engine is indwelling desire (James 1:14-15); that engine is itself sin at work, and finding 4 excludes sin from the process. Christ's temptations therefore came as assault and as the pressure of sinless natural appetite and recoil (unit 07's cup; hunger in the wilderness), never as solicitation from a sinful desire within. Owen's summary, that Christ faced the suffering part of temptation while we also face the sinning part, is an accurate compression of this finding. [Necessary inference, HIGH as the exclusion of sin from the process; the identification of the excluded ingredient with James's epithymia mechanism is Theological synthesis, HIGH within the canon read together]
6. **What the verse does not assert, stated as a limit on this study's own control text.** The word non posse peccare, and every modal term like it, is absent: 4:15 confesses that he did not sin and that sin had no place in his tempting; it does not, in so many words, state that he could not sin. Any claim that Hebrews 4:15 by itself teaches impeccability as a lexical entailment overclaims the verse, and the working paper's modal qualification in 6.24 (sinlessness alone does not establish impeccability) is confirmed at exactly this point. [Explicit text, HIGH: an absence verified against the verse's full inventory in section 1]
7. **Within the same epistle, the fact of 4:15 is restated as an abiding state and built into the offering.** Kechorismenos (7:26, perfect) confesses settled separation from sinners; 7:27 presupposes a priest with no sins of his own to offer for; 7:28 sets the Son "perfected forever" over against priests "having weakness"; 1:10-12 and 13:8 predicate the Son's sameness ("you are the same"; "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever") of the same subject to whom 4:15's tempting is predicated. [Explicit text, HIGH]
8. **The impeccability inference: split verdict on the MEDIUM tag.** (a) As an inference from 4:15 alone, impeccability remains what 6.24 tagged it: a theological inference, MEDIUM - the verse supplies the fact of sinlessness and the sympathy ground, and finding 6 records what it does not say. (b) As 6.24 actually constructs it - this verse's fact, restated as abiding state by 7:26-28, predicated of a subject confessed within the same epistle as the unchanging Son (1:12; 13:8), under the canon's statements that God cannot be tempted by evil (James 1:13, apeirastos) and that in Christ sin is not merely uncommitted but absent (1 John 3:5, present tense: "hamartia en auto ouk estin"; 2 Corinthians 5:21, "knew no sin"; John 8:46, the unanswered challenge "which of you convicts me of sin?"), and completed by 6.24's person premise (the willing and acting subject is the one divine Son; the works of each nature are the works of the whole person, WLC 40) - the inference is good and necessary consequence at full strength: a peccable Christ would require the unchanging, sin-separated Son to be capable of acting against his own confessed state and nature, or would require a second, independent center of willing alongside the person, which the two-wills confession (units 07, 08) excludes. The tag is therefore split, not simply raised: MEDIUM for the verse alone, HIGH as theological synthesis on the full premise set, which is the form in which the treatise will actually use it. [Theological synthesis: MEDIUM on the single-verse basis; HIGH on the full premise set]
9. **The contrary premise is philosophical, not exegetical.** "Temptation implies the possibility of sin" (Hodge, section 5.1) asserts a necessity that peirazo's usage does not carry (section 3.1) and that the verse's own sympathy logic contradicts (finding 3). It may be debated as philosophy; it may not be cited as a finding of this text. [Assessment, HIGH]

## 5. Competing readings

1. **The peccability reading: Christ could have sinned, and must have been able to, for his temptation and sympathy to be real.** Strongest form, in its most weighty advocate's own words. Charles Hodge, Princeton's systematician and no outsider to the Reformed confession, denied impeccability outright (Systematic Theology 2:457, as quoted verbatim in Walvoord's survey): "This sinlessness of our Lord, however, does not amount to absolute impeccability. It was not a non potest peccare. If He was a true man, He must have been capable of sinning. That He did not sin under the greatest provocations; that when He was reviled He blessed; when He suffered He threatened not; that He was dumb as a sheep before its shearers, is held up to us as an example. Temptation implies the possibility of sin. If from the constitution of his person it was impossible for Christ to sin, then his temptation was unreal and without effect and He cannot sympathize with his people." The argument deserves its full force: it is aimed at this verse's own stated function (sympathesai), it claims the verse's total-likeness clause for itself (a likeness that stops short of the one feature that makes our temptation what it is - its genuine openness - is, it says, likeness in every respect except the respect that matters), and it draws support from James 1:13 read one way (God is apeirastos, untemptable; Christ was tempted; therefore Christ was tempted precisely in that in him which is not God simpliciter, and the human nature so tempted is the peccable nature Adam had). *Adjudication.* Declined, on grammar and on the epistle's own logic rather than on tradition. (i) Its load-bearing premise ("temptation implies the possibility of sin") is not in the lexicon (section 3.1) and is not in this verse: the author grounds sympathy in undergone and completed experience (findings 1 and 3), and the premise must therefore be supplied from a philosophy of action, where it is contestable and contested (section 5.2), not from Hebrews. (ii) The claim that impossibility makes temptation "unreal" mistakes where the reality of a temptation resides: in the assault's force and in the cost of resistance, both of which the canon maximizes for Christ rather than minimizing - forty days, Gethsemane's cries (5:7), a lifetime with no early surrender to cut the pressure short. Shedd's reply (reading 2) and the exegetical commonplace agree here: the one who yields early never feels temptation's full weight, so a sympathy grounded in having felt the whole of it belongs preeminently to the one who never yielded. (iii) The likeness argument cuts the other way once choris is given its scope (finding 4): the likeness is expressly bounded at sin, and the openness-to-sin that the reading wants to include in the likeness is itself a form of sin's presence in the process, excluded by the exception the reading must therefore shrink to outcome-only, which section 3.4 shows the phrase will not bear. (iv) The cost within this epistle is decisive: peccability makes 7:26's perfect kechorismenos a state that was, throughout the earthly life, one decision away from being false, and makes the ephapax offering of 7:27 rest on a creaturely performance that could have failed; the epistle's confidence in urging the approach of 4:16 is not written in that register. What is retained from Hodge: the temptations were real, the cost was real, the human nature considered in abstraction from the union is the kind of nature that fell in Adam (Shedd concedes exactly this, and the treatise should too), and the sympathy is the verse's point, not a decoration on it. [Assessment: declined, HIGH]
2. **The impeccability reading in its classical Reformed form: non posse peccare, with temptation's reality located in the suffering of resistance.** Strongest form (Shedd, Dogmatic Theology, Christology ch. 5, in extended verbatim quotation as preserved in Walvoord's survey): "It is objected to the doctrine of Christ's impeccability that it is inconsistent with his temptability. A person who cannot sin, it is said, cannot be tempted to sin. This is not correct; any more than it would be correct to say that because an army cannot be conquered, it cannot be attacked. Temptability depends upon the constitutional susceptibility, while impeccability depends upon the will. So far as his natural susceptibility, both physical and mental, was concerned, Jesus Christ was open to all forms of human temptation excepting those that spring out of lust or corruption of nature. But his peccability, or the possibility of being overcome by these temptations, would depend upon the amount of voluntary resistance which he was able to bring to bear against them. Those temptations were very strong, but if the self-determination of his holy will was stronger than they, then they could not induce him to sin, and he would be impeccable. And yet plainly he would be temptable." Shedd's construction has three members (summarized in the Clearly Reformed primer from Dogmatic Theology 659-662): impeccability deduced from Scripture through the Son's immutability (Hebrews 13:8; 1:12) - a peccable Christ would be a Savior trustworthy only in hindsight; impeccability from the constitution of the person - the capacity to sin is measured by "his mightiest nature," as an iron wire welded to an iron bar is rendered immovable, so that the human nature is peccable in abstraction and the theanthropic person is not; and consistency with temptation, as above. His two-wills member, quoted in the same survey, binds this study to units 07 and 08: "Each nature, in order to be complete, entire, and wanting nothing, has its own will; but the finite will never antagonizes the infinite will, but obeys it invariably and perfectly," with the absence from Christ's lips of any Romans 7 conflict, and John 8:46 and 1 John 3:5 as the canon speaking. *Adjudication.* Accepted, with the discipline of finding 8: the reading is the correct dogmatic construction of the premise set, and every exegetical element it needs from this verse it actually has (real temptation, total likeness, total exclusion of sin, sympathy from completed experience). Its one exposure is presentational, and Part VII must avoid it: Shedd's rhetoric can sound as though 4:15 itself asserts the non posse peccare, where the study's finding 6 shows it does not. Stated as synthesis on the full premise set, the reading stands at HIGH (finding 8b); stated as a lexical claim on this verse, it would deserve the demotion its critics give it. [Assessment: accepted as disciplined, HIGH as synthesis on the full premise set; the verse-alone form remains MEDIUM per finding 8a]
3. **The mode-limiting reading of choris hamartias: "without sin" limits the temptation itself, not merely its outcome.** Strongest form: Alford (quoted in Jamieson-Fausset-Brown) - sin had nothing in him "in their origin, process, and result"; Tittmann's lexical ground (choris, separation, against aneu, mere absence); and the modern Reformed summary in the Clearly Reformed primer: the preposition "without" (choris) extends "both to the outcome of the temptations (unlike us, Christ did not sin) and also to the nature of the temptations (unlike ours, Christ's temptations were not sinful)... Or as John Owen put it, Christ faced the suffering part of temptation; we also face the sinning part." *Adjudication.* Accepted in substance and refined in statement (section 3.4, finding 4): the reading is right that the phrase reaches the temptation itself and not only its score; it is imprecise only when phrased as "rather than" the outcome, since its own best statements include both (origin, process, and result; nature and outcome). Its desire-specific component (no solicitation from indwelling epithymia) is a necessary inference from the phrase together with James 1:13-15 and the wider canon, and is tagged so (finding 5), not read off the preposition alone. [Assessment: accepted as refined, HIGH]
4. **The outcome-only reading of choris hamartias: the phrase certifies the result (he did not sin) and says nothing about the temptation's constitution.** Strongest form: it is the reading embodied in the NIV's rendering ("yet he did not sin"), it keeps the doctrine of the verse to the minimum the words most plainly state, and it warns, sincerely, against building a psychology of Christ's inner life on a two-word prepositional phrase at the end of a sentence whose argumentative purpose is pastoral, not speculative. *Adjudication.* Partially accepted: the outcome is certainly included in the phrase's coverage (readings 2 and 3 both insist on it), and the pastoral purpose is real (section 2). Declined as an exhaustive account: it requires restricting a trailing modifier, whose word (choris) denotes separation, to the one member of the complex (the result) that its position least singles out, while the same phrase at 9:28 carries total apartness and the cognate participle at 7:26 confesses the state. A minimum-reading discipline is a virtue in exegesis; here it is purchased by assigning the author's words a narrower sense in 4:15 than he gives them in 9:28, which is not discipline but inconsistency. [Assessment: outcome included, HIGH; outcome-only restriction declined, HIGH]

## 6. Textual notes

The text of 4:15 is stable, and nothing in this study rests on a contested word. The Nestle 1904 and SBLGNT 2010 texts of the verse are identical (so the exegetical comparison consulted), and the points of possible variation reduce to one, which is not really a variant in sense at all. The participle appears as pepeirasmenon (from peirazo) in the critical tradition and as pepeiramenon (from peirao) in the Textus Receptus and Byzantine tradition underlying the KJV: the same perfect passive participle formed on the verb's two stems, identical in tense, voice, and meaning. The KJV's English ("was... tempted") flattens the perfect, but that is a fact about the translation, not about its Greek text, which has the perfect. No variation is reported in the consulted materials at kata panta, kath' homoioteta, or choris hamartias: the scope words and the exception on which the study's argument turns are textually unassailable. The one precision this study insists on, against a common way of citing the verse, is that the participle is perfect, not aorist: aorist forms of the verb (peirastheis) occur at 2:18 for the event, and transferring the aorist's aspect to 4:15 either in citation or in argument misstates the author's chosen register (section 3.1). [Textual, HIGH for the stability of substance and of the load-bearing words; MEDIUM-HIGH for the stem-variant attribution, which rests on edition comparisons and Thayer's principal-parts note rather than on the NA28 apparatus consulted directly]
