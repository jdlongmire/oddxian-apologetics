## 1. Text and translation

"As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today" (ESV). Hebrew: *we'atem chashavtem alay ra'ah; Elohim chashavah letovah, lema'an asot kayyom hazzeh, lehachayot am-rav.* The first clause: "you [plural] devised against me evil." The second: "God intended it [third masculine verb with third feminine singular suffix] for good, in order to bring about, as this day shows, the keeping alive of a numerous people." The verse is one sentence of Joseph's speech, and its two halves are built on the same verb with two subjects. Section 3 takes the construction apart; the suffix on God's verb is the grammatical hinge.

Translation comparison (brothers' clause / God's clause):

| Version | Brothers | God |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | ye thought evil against me | God meant it unto good |
| ESV | you meant evil against me | God meant it for good |
| NASB | you meant evil against me | God meant it for good |
| NIV | You intended to harm me | God intended it for good |
| NET | you meant to harm me | God intended it for a good purpose |
| CSB | You planned evil against me | God planned it for good |
| LEB | you planned evil against me | God planned it for good |
| NRSV | you intended to do harm to me | God intended it for good |

The formal versions preserve the one-verb symmetry almost without exception: ESV and NASB keep "meant" for both subjects; CSB and LEB keep "planned" for both; NIV and NRSV keep "intended" for both. NET differentiates slightly ("meant" / "intended") and expands *letovah* as "for a good purpose." The symmetry is broken in the New Century Version, which renders the second clause "God turned your evil into good": a substitution that settles in translation the interpretive question section 5 examines. The study follows the formal versions: the Hebrew has one verb, and the translation should not be allowed to decide before the exegesis begins.

## 2. Context

The verse stands at the climax of the Joseph narrative and of Genesis. Jacob has died; the protection the brothers assumed his presence gave them is gone, and they state their fear in 50:15: what if Joseph bears a grudge (*yistemenu*, from satam) and pays us back in full (*hashev yashiv*) "for all the evil (*ra'ah*) that we did to him" (*gamal*, we dealt to him). They send a plea, attributed to Jacob's deathbed instruction (50:16-17), confessing "they did evil to you." Joseph weeps, and his answer has three movements: he refuses the place of judge ("Am I in the place of God?" 50:19, *hatachat Elohim ani*); he states the asymmetrical intention (50:20); and he acts on it, providing for them and comforting them (50:21, *vayenachem otam*, "he comforted them," the Piel of nacham, the verb of study 02).

The statement is also the final form of an interpretation Joseph has given twice before. In 45:5-8, at the self-disclosure: "do not be grieved (*al-te'atsevu*, atsav, study 02) or angry with yourselves because you sold me here, for God sent me before you to preserve life... to preserve for you a remnant on earth, and to keep alive (*lehachayot*, the same Hiphil infinitive as 50:20) for you many survivors. So it was not you who sent me here, but God." Behind both statements stand the dreams of Genesis 37, disclosed before the brothers acted, which announced Joseph's elevation; the brothers plotted against the dreamer (37:18, *vayyitnakkelu*, they conspired craftily) and taunted, "we shall see what will become of his dreams" (37:20). Joseph "remembered the dreams" at the moment of recognition (42:9). Within the narrative's own frame, therefore, the outcome was announced in advance of the act that produced it; 50:20 is Joseph's settled, final reading of the whole arc, spoken when revenge was his for the taking and declined.

## 3. Word studies

### 3.1 Chashav (Strong's H2803)

BDB's Qal structure divides by subject. **Of man:** (1) think, account: "not so thinketh his heart" (Isaiah 10:7); with two accusatives, "we accounted him stricken" (Isaiah 53:4); with accusative and lamed, "Judah thought her to be a prostitute" (Genesis 38:15). (2) devise, plan, mean: with accusative *machashevet ra'ah*, a plan of evil (Ezekiel 38:10); *ra'ah*, evil (Psalm 35:4; 140:3; Zechariah 7:10; 8:17); *mezimmot*, schemes (Psalm 10:2; 21:12); *avon*, iniquity (Micah 2:1; Ezekiel 11:2; Psalm 36:5); and the construction *ra'ah al*, "devise evil against": Genesis 50:20, Jeremiah 48:2, Nahum 1:11; with *machashavot al*, plans against: Jeremiah 11:19; 18:18; 49:30; Esther 8:3; 9:25; with infinitive: Saul "to make David fall" (1 Samuel 18:25); Nehemiah's enemies, "they planned to do me harm" (Nehemiah 6:2, 6). (3) charge, impute (2 Samuel 19:20). (4) esteem, value (Isaiah 33:8; 53:3). (5) invent, of the craftsman's devising (Exodus 31:4; Amos 6:5).

**Of God:** (1) think, account one as an enemy (Job 13:24; 19:11; 33:10). (2) devise, plan, mean: with accusative and lamed, *letovah*, "for good," Genesis 50:20, the sole citation BDB gives for this exact construction under God; "devise for me" (Psalm 40:18); accusative of thing with *al*, devising disaster against a people (Micah 2:3; Jeremiah 18:11), plans toward a people (Jeremiah 29:11, "the plans I am planning toward you... for welfare [*leshalom*] and not for evil [*lera'ah*]"); with *el*, against (Jeremiah 49:20; 50:45); with infinitive (Jeremiah 26:3; 36:3; Lamentations 2:8). (3) impute, reckon: "he reckoned it to him as righteousness" (Genesis 15:6); "to whom the LORD does not impute iniquity" (Psalm 32:2).

Two lexical facts matter for the verse. First, the "devise, plan, mean" sense is no strain on the verb: it is BDB's second sense for man and for God alike, and the brothers' clause sits squarely inside a large family of hostile-devising uses (Psalm 35:4; Jeremiah 11:19; Nehemiah 6:2). Second, the verb also carries an accounting sense (Genesis 15:6; Psalm 32:2), and that fact is the entire lexical basis of the competing reading in section 5.1; the two senses are distinguished in BDB by construction, which is why the construction of 50:20 must be examined on its own terms.

### 3.2 The two constructions in Genesis 50:20

The symmetry is real but not total, and the difference inside it is where the verse's theology is carried.

- **The brothers' clause:** *chashavtem alay ra'ah.* Verb, second masculine plural; object, *ra'ah*, evil, as the accusative content of the devising; *alay*, "against me," marking the target. The evil is the thing devised. This is BDB's construction I.2, "devise evil against."
- **God's clause:** *chashavah letovah.* Verb, third masculine singular, carrying a third feminine singular suffix, *-ah*, "it." The suffix's antecedent is the brothers' deed-complex, grammatically nearest to the feminine *ra'ah*: the very thing they devised is the object God takes up. Then *letovah*: lamed prefixed to *tovah*, good, marking destination or result, "for good, toward good." BDB files the clause separately, under II.2, "with accusative and lamed," and Genesis 50:20 is its unique example of the *letovah* destination.
- **The purpose clause:** *lema'an asot kayyom hazzeh lehachayot am-rav.* *Lema'an* with the infinitive construct of *asah*, "in order to bring about," as the present day evidences; then the Hiphil infinitive of *chayah*, *lehachayot*, "to keep alive," with its object *am-rav*, "a numerous people." The content of the *tovah* is not left abstract: it is specified as the preservation of life on a large scale.

The constructions differ at exactly one joint. For the brothers, *evil* is the accusative content of the intention and Joseph is the target of it (*alay*). For God, the accusative is the deed itself (the suffix), and the intention's expressed terminus is not a person targeted but an end, *good*, toward which the deed is directed. One verb, one event, two intentions whose objects and termini differ: that is the grammar before any theology is added.

## 4. Findings

1. **One verb governs both clauses, and the object is the same event.** Joseph predicates *chashav* of the brothers and of God within a single sentence, and the suffix on God's verb takes the brothers' deed as its accusative. The verse does not describe two unrelated events, one evil and one good; it describes one historical complex under two intentions. [Explicit text, HIGH]
2. **The constructions are not identical, and the difference is morally loaded.** The brothers devise evil (accusative) against Joseph; God intends the deed (accusative suffix) for good (lamed of destination). BDB's own arrangement registers the difference, filing the verse under two distinct constructions. God's intention, as the sentence is built, does not have evil as its content; it has the deed as its object and good as its end. [Explicit text, HIGH]
3. **Asymmetrical intention is the verse's own grammar, not a theological gloss laid over it.** From 1 and 2 together: the same act is evil in the intending of the agents who performed it and good in the intending of God who directed it to its end, and the sentence keeps the two from collapsing into each other. Joseph nowhere redescribes the brothers' act as good, and nowhere makes God the deviser of the evil; the asymmetry is carried by the objects and the lamed, not by a distinction imported from systematic theology. [Necessary inference, HIGH]
4. **The good intended is concrete and stated as a purpose.** The *lema'an* clause specifies the end: the keeping alive of a numerous people, already visible "as it is this day." The verse's claim is not that everything turned out vaguely well, but that this preservation was the intention's content. [Explicit text, HIGH]
5. **The statement functions as comfort because vengeance is refused.** Spoken against the brothers' fear of repayment (50:15), prefaced by the refusal of God's judicial place (50:19), and followed by provision and comfort (50:21), the asymmetrical-intention claim is in context an act of mercy, not a detached metaphysical thesis. Its first use in Scripture is pastoral. [Explicit text, HIGH]
6. **The scope of the verse is this historical complex.** Genesis 50:20 warrants the claim for the Joseph events. A universal premise (that God stands in this relation to every event, including every evil) is not stated in the verse and is not smuggled in by this study: it is the burden of Ephesians 1:11 and Romans 8:28-30, units 06 and 05, and of Part II's synthesis. The working paper's finding stands, and is strengthened at the construction level rather than extended. [Theological synthesis, HIGH as a scope judgment]

## 5. Competing readings

1. **The reckoning reading: God's chashav is retrospective assessment, not prior intention.** In its strongest form (argued in the Targuman essay consulted for this study): *chashav* can mean "reckon, account," as in Genesis 15:6, where God reckons Abram's already-existing faith as righteousness; nothing there suggests God ordained the faith he reckoned. Read the second clause of 50:20 the same way: the brothers intended with foresight and planning, but God "reckoned" their act for good, responding to what they had set in motion and transforming it after the fact. On this reading Joseph affirms redemptive overruling, not ordination beforehand, and God is kept from being the planner of the brothers' crime. *Assessment:* the reading is declined as the primary account, on grounds internal to the grammar and the narrative. (a) BDB separates the two senses by construction and files God's clause in 50:20 under "devise, plan, mean" (II.2), not under "impute, reckon" (II.3); the reckoning construction of Genesis 15:6 is different in shape, accusative plus lamed of the person credited plus a bare predicate noun ("reckoned it *to him* [as] *righteousness*"), whereas 50:20 has accusative plus lamed of destination (*letovah*), the shape of Jeremiah 29:11's plans "toward you." (b) The *lema'an* purpose clause states an end the event served; purpose language of this kind attaches naturally to intention, and the reckoning reading must re-read it as the purpose of a retrospective assessment, which is possible but strained. (c) Within the narrative, God's relation to the outcome is not first announced after the fact: the dreams of Genesis 37 precede the brothers' act, Joseph's earlier interpretation speaks of God's *sending* in advance terms ("God sent me before you," 45:5, 7-8), and 50:20 is the third statement of that reading, not a new one. What the reckoning reading rightly protects, this study keeps: God is not presented as the deviser of the evil (finding 2), and no reading of this verse may make him one. Its positive lexical claim, however, asks the second clause to carry the wrong one of BDB's constructions. [Assessment: reading declined, HIGH confidence in the construction argument; the theological concern it guards is retained]
2. **The event-limited reading: the verse licenses a claim about this event and nothing more.** Accepted as scope discipline; it is finding 6, and it is the working paper's own position. Where this reading is pressed further, to deny that the verse teaches divine *intention* at all (reducing Joseph's claim to the observation that God *used* the outcome), it fails on the verb: *chashav* in the divine clause is an intention verb in BDB's arrangement, and "God used it" is weaker than anything Joseph says. The middle position is the text's: real intention, this event, universal premise deferred to the wider canon.
3. **The mainstream providential reading, that the verse states God's prior intent over the whole event** (for example D. A. Carson's use of Joseph and the cross in *How Long, O Lord?*: morally evil things "may not only have a good result but may be good in God's intent even if evil in human intent," quoted in the Targuman piece consulted). Accepted in substance for this event, with two qualifications the text itself imposes: the intent's object, grammatically, is the deed directed to its good end, not the evil as evil (finding 2); and the step from this event to God's intent over morally evil things generally is Carson's canonical step, not this verse's, and belongs to Parts II and V with Ephesians 1:11 and Romans 8:28-30 in hand.

## 6. Textual notes

The Masoretic text of the verse is stable. The witnesses displayed for this study (Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia, Aleppo Codex, Westminster Leningrad Codex, via the StudyLight interlinear) agree word for word, including both *chashav* forms and the suffix on *chashavah*. No variant in the witnesses consulted affects either clause or the verse's claim; the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Targumim were not separately collated for this verse, and that limit is stated rather than passed over. [Textual, HIGH for the witnesses consulted; collation limit noted]

The Septuagint preserves the same-verb symmetry, which is the point of theological weight. Its text (verified in the StudyLight Greek interlinear): *humeis ebouleusasthe kat' emou eis ponera, ho de theos ebouleusato peri emou eis agatha, hopos an genethe hos semeron, hina diatraphe laos polus.* Both clauses take the same verb, *bouleuo* in the middle voice, "to take counsel, resolve, plan": *ebouleusasthe* (second plural) for the brothers, *ebouleusato* (third singular) for God. Brenton renders it with matching English: "Ye took counsel against me for evil, but God took counsel for me for good." The earliest translation therefore did not soften the one-verb claim or split it into two kinds of divine and human action (a marked contrast with the LXX at Genesis 6:6, study 02). Its divergences are translational and lie elsewhere: "concerning me" (*peri emou*) in God's clause where the Hebrew carries the accusative suffix "it," so that the LXX's God counsels *about Joseph* rather than intending *the deed*; *eis agatha*, "for good things," for *letovah*; and a closing *hina diatraphe laos polus*, "so that a numerous people might be fed," reading the preservation concretely as nourishment through the famine where the Hebrew says "to keep alive a numerous people." None of these touches the symmetry of intention that the verse turns on.
