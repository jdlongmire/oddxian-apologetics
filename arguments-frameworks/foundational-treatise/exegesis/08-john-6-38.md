## 1. Text and translation

John 6:38, in transliteration: "hoti katabebeka apo tou ouranou, ouch hina poio to thelema to emon alla to thelema tou pempsantos me." Literally: "For I have come down from heaven, not in order that I might do the will, the mine, but the will of the one who sent me." The working paper's transliteration (katabebeka ek tou ouranou ouch hina poio to thelema to emon alla to thelema tou pempsantos me) agrees in substance; it prints ek, the Byzantine reading, where the critical text reads apo (see section 6). John 5:30, the companion statement: "ou zeto to thelema to emon alla to thelema tou pempsantos me," "I do not seek the will, the mine, but the will of the one who sent me." John 4:34: "emon broma estin hina poieso to thelema tou pempsantos me kai teleioso autou to ergon," "My food is that I do the will of the one who sent me and accomplish his work."

Three observations. First, the versions are nearly unanimous at 6:38; the only real divergences are tense (KJV "came down" against the perfect rendered "have come down" by the rest) and the NIV's making the purpose verb explicit on both sides ("not to do my will but to do the will"). Second, the NIV at 6:38 drops "own" ("my will"), slightly flattening the contrast the Greek marks with the possessive adjective emon; at 5:30 the NIV paraphrases the noun away altogether ("not to please myself"), the one rendering in the tables that loses the will vocabulary the study needs. Third, the KJV at 5:30 names "the Father" where the critical text reads only "him who sent me": a clarifying expansion in the tradition behind the KJV, of the same kind attested as a variant at 6:38 (section 6). The sense is identical, since the sender is the Father throughout both discourses, but the study quotes the critical wording.

## 2. Context

The verse stands inside the bread of life discourse (6:35-58), at the point where the discourse turns from the sign to the gift's security. The sequence matters: Jesus declares himself the bread of life (6:35); states that all the Father gives him will come to him and that he will never cast out the one who comes (6:37); then gives the ground in 6:38 (the descent is for the sender's will, not his own); then states that will's content (6:39-40, section 3.4). The descent claim is what the crowd immediately seizes on: "Is this not Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know? How does he now say, I have come down from heaven?" (6:41-42). The will statement is therefore not an aside; it is carried by the discourse's central origin claim, and the objection it provokes is an objection to origin, not to obedience.

Two formulae run through the discourse and the whole Gospel. The descent verb katabaino and its perfect katabebeka recur at 6:33, 38, 41, 42, 50, 51, 58 (section 3.3). The mission formula "him who sent me" (ho pempsas me) is one of the Fourth Gospel's signature phrases, distributed across the discourses: 5:23-24, 30, 37; 6:38-39, 44; 7:16, 28; 8:16, 18, 26, 29; 12:44-49; 13:20; 14:24; 15:21; 16:5; 20:21. In John, to be sent is the Son's standing self-description: his teaching is the sender's (7:16), his judgment is not his alone but the sender's with him (8:16), his works are the sender's commission (5:36), his words are the sender's commandment (12:49-50), and the mission is finally extended in the same form to the disciples: "As the Father has sent me, even so I am sending you" (20:21). Verse 38 is the will-statement of that mission idiom: the sent one's will is stated only in relation to the sender's.

## 3. Grammar and word studies

### 3.1 Thelema in John (Strong's G2307)

Strong's definition: thelema, from the prolonged form of thelo (G2309, to determine, choose, prefer, wish), is "a determination (properly, the thing), i.e. (actively) choice (specially, purpose, decree; abstractly, volition) or (passively) inclination: desire, pleasure, will." The noun's range thus runs from inclination to settled purpose; context, not the lexeme alone, fixes where a given occurrence falls, and section 3.4 shows the context of 6:38-40 fixing it at the purpose end.

The noun's distribution in the Fourth Gospel is remarkably concentrated. It occurs at 1:13 (twice: "not of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man," thelematos sarkos, thelematos andros, of the human willing that does not produce the new birth); 4:34; 5:30 (twice); 6:38 (twice); 6:39; 6:40; 7:17 ("if anyone's will is to do his will," where the verb thelo and the noun meet: the one who wills to do God's will will know the teaching); and 9:31 ("if anyone is a worshiper of God and does his will, God listens to him"). Apart from 1:13, every occurrence concerns the Father's will as Jesus does it, seeks it, or states its content; the single human-will use is a negation, human willing named precisely as not the source of the decisive act. The verb thelo fills in the surrounding texture (5:40, "you are unwilling to come to me"; 7:17; 21:22-23, the Lord's sovereign "if I will that he remain"), but the noun, the will as a thing that can be done, is in John almost a technical term for the sender's purpose and for Jesus's relation to it. ### 3.2 The grammar of 6:38

Four features carry the sense.

- **Katabebeka is perfect tense.** "I have come down" (so ESV, NASB, NET, CSB against the KJV's simple "came down"): a descent accomplished, with the descended state continuing into the speaking present. The claim is not of a past errand completed but of a present identity: the speaker stands on earth as the one whose origin is heaven, which is exactly the claim the crowd contests in 6:42, quoting the perfect back ("he says, I have come down from heaven").
- **Ouch hina poio is a purpose negation.** Hina with the subjunctive states purpose; ouch negates the purpose clause: the descent was not *in order that* he do his own will. The negation falls on purpose, not on capacity or on the existence of the will named: the verse does not say he has no will of his own, nor that he could not do it, but that doing it was not the descent's aim.
- **Poio is present subjunctive**, a doing viewed as ongoing course rather than a single act: the mission as a continuous doing of the sender's will, matching 4:34 (food, a standing sustenance) and 8:29 ("I always do the things pleasing to him," ta aresta auto poio pantote).
- **The contrast is built from two articular noun phrases**: to thelema to emon, "the will, the mine," with the possessive adjective emon in the emphatic post-article position, against to thelema tou pempsantos me, "the will of the one who sent me," where the will is identified not by an adjective but by a genitive naming its owner through his act of sending (pempsantos, aorist participle: the sending as the settled commission behind the discourse). The second member is elliptical: alla [hina poio] to thelema, "but [in order that I do] the will of the one who sent me." Both wills are grammatically real noun phrases in the same construction; the rhetoric distinguishes them by ownership and aligns them by the purpose clause.

### 3.3 The descent chain

Within chapter 6 the descent is stated in alternating forms: 6:33, the bread of God is "the one who comes down (ho katabainon, present participle) from heaven and gives life to the world"; 6:38, katabebeka, perfect; 6:41, "I am the bread that came down (ho katabas, aorist participle) from heaven"; 6:42, the crowd's quotation of the perfect; 6:50, "the bread that comes down from heaven, so that one may eat of it and not die"; 6:51, "I am the living bread that came down (ho katabas) from heaven"; 6:58, "this is the bread that came down from heaven, not as the fathers ate and died." The present forms state the descent as the bread's defining character; the perfect in 6:38 and its quotation in 6:42 state it as the speaker's accomplished fact; the aorists narrate it as event. Verse 38 is the chain's only first-person perfect self-predication inside the discourse, and it is the link the crowd picks up: in John's presentation, the will statement and the descent claim stand or fall together, because the same verse asserts both.

### 3.4 The will statements as a Johannine set, and the content of thelema in 6:39-40

The set, in discourse order:

- **4:34.** "My food is to do the will of him who sent me and to accomplish (teleioso) his work." The sender's will is sustenance, not burden, and it is paired with a work that has a finish: the will here is a commission with a completion, already the mission sense.
- **5:30.** "I can do nothing on my own. As I hear, I judge, and my judgment is just, because I seek not my own will but the will of him who sent me." The will contrast is set inside the judgment discourse of 5:19-30: the Son's judgment is just *because* it is not self-seeking. Non-self-willing is given as the ground of the judgment's righteousness, which ties the will statements to the treatise's moral archetype as well as to Christology: the judge judges rightly because his willing is wholly referred to the sender.
- **6:38-40.** The descent statement, and then, uniquely in the set, the will's content is spelled out. 6:39: "This is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up at the last day." 6:40: "For this is the will of my Father, that everyone who looks on the Son and believes in him should have eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day." Thelema here is not left as a general divine preference: it is a stated saving purpose with specified objects (all the given ones; everyone who looks and believes) and a specified outcome (nothing lost; resurrection at the last day; eternal life). The sender of 6:39 is named "my Father" in 6:40 without any change of referent.
- **8:29.** "And he who sent me is with me. He has not left me alone, for I always do what is pleasing to him." The standing result of the will alignment: the sender's presence, not abandonment. Alignment is presented as communion maintained, which Part VII should note against any reading of the obedience as bare submission of an inferior to a superior's command.

Read as a set, the statements move from sustenance (4:34) through judgment (5:30) to descent and saving content (6:38-40) to abiding presence (8:29); the same construction (my will negated, the sender's will affirmed) is their spine.

## 4. Findings

1. **Jesus predicates a will of himself, referentially distinct from the sender's will, in a single sentence spoken by a single "I."** To thelema to emon and to thelema tou pempsantos me are two noun phrases, distinguished by ownership, inside one purpose clause. Whatever systematics is built on the verse must start from that datum: John presents one speaker who speaks of "my will" as a real possession and of the sender's will as another, and subordinates the first to the second at the level of purpose. [Explicit text, HIGH]
2. **The relation stated between the wills is non-competition by constitution, not a struggle resolved.** The negation is of purpose (ouch hina): the descent was never aimed at his own will. John gives no scene in which the Son's will resists and is then overridden; the alignment is the mission's definition from the outset (4:34, food; 8:29, always pleasing). The cost and recoil that belong to the human will's obedience appear in John at 12:27 ("Now my soul is troubled; and what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour? No, for this purpose I have come to this hour") and 13:21 (troubled in spirit), where the recoil is real and is overruled from within the mission in the same breath, not narrated as a second will winning against the first. John's contribution to the two-wills dossier is therefore the constancy pole: a human will genuinely present (finding 1) and genuinely troubled (12:27), whose course is unbroken alignment. The petitionary tension itself is Luke's text (22:42, unit 07); Part VII needs both and must not import Luke's form into John or flatten John's constancy into insensibility. [Explicit text, HIGH for the wording of 6:38, 12:27, 13:21; Theological synthesis, MEDIUM for the two-poles framing of the dossier]
3. **In 6:39-40 thelema is specified as a definite saving purpose.** Content: the preservation of all the Father has given the Son (lose nothing), resurrection at the last day, eternal life for the one who looks on the Son and believes. By the time John uses thelema absolutely in this discourse, it denotes a purpose with objects, a guarantee, and an eschatological outcome, which is the purpose/decree end of the word's lexical range (section 3.1), not the inclination end. [Explicit text, HIGH]
4. **Consequently 6:38 is soteriological as well as Christological, and Part VII must use it as both.** The verse's will distinction cannot be treated in Part VII as a bare datum about Christ's constitution and then left there, because the sentence's own continuation defines the will being done as the salvation of the given ones, in terms that parallel unit 05's chain (Romans 8:29-30: conformity and glorification as the decree's stated end) and unit 11's consummation texts. Part VII's use: the obedience of the Son is the execution of the saving decree, so Christology and the decree meet in the person who both wills humanly and carries out the sender's saving purpose; a treatment that used 6:38 only to count wills would underread its own control text. [Necessary inference, HIGH for the content claim; Theological synthesis, MEDIUM for the programmatic instruction to Part VII]
5. **The katabebeka perfect makes the will statement an origin claim as well as an obedience claim.** The one speaking is on earth as having come down from heaven and remaining the descended one; the crowd's objection (6:41-42) targets exactly this. The verse therefore cannot be read as the self-description of a merely human emissary: its own discourse stakes the speaker's heavenly origin, and the will alignment is predicated of that speaker. Any reading that takes the obedience as evidence of a merely creaturely status has to set aside the sentence's first clause and the discourse's reaction to it. [Explicit text, HIGH for the morphology and the crowd's objection; Necessary inference, MEDIUM for the consequence drawn against the creaturely reading]
6. **The Johannine thelema distribution supports reading "my will" as a true human possession rather than a figure.** In John the noun names a will that can be done, sought, and stated with content, and its one human use (1:13) names human willing as a real, though non-decisive, thing. Jesus's to thelema to emon sits in that lexical world: a will one could in principle pursue (5:30: seek, zeto) and programmatically does not. The negation is meaningful precisely because the thing negated is real. [Lexical synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH: distribution as listed in section 3.1; the inference from distribution to the possessive's force is a judgment]
7. **The verse does not, by itself, teach the dyothelite doctrine.** It supplies the doctrine's key exegetical datum (finding 1) inside a mission idiom (section 2), but the formulation "two natural wills, the human will following and subject to the divine, not opposed" belongs to the conciliar grammar (Constantinople III, Phase 2), reached by reading this text with Luke 22:42 (unit 07), the two-natures confession, and the wider canon. This study's deliverable to Part VII is the datum, the constancy pole (finding 2), and the soteriological content (findings 3-4), at the confidences tagged; the conciliar step is flagged, not taken here. [Scope judgment, HIGH]

## 5. Competing readings

1. **The subordinationist (Arian) reading: distinct wills and the sending relation prove unequal natures.** In its strongest form: a sender commands and a sent one obeys; the sent one expressly disowns his own will in favor of another's; at 5:30 he says he can do nothing on his own; at 14:28 he says the Father is greater. Taken together, the Son of these texts is a subordinate being whose obedience is the obedience of an inferior nature to a superior. Response: the reading must carry the whole discourse, and it cannot. The 5:30 statement stands in a discourse that begins because Jesus was "making himself equal with God" (5:18) and that answers with the Son doing whatever the Father does (5:19), having life in himself as the Father has life in himself (5:26), and receiving all judgment (5:22). The 6:38 statement is prefaced by the descent perfect and contested precisely as a heavenly-origin claim (finding 5). Sending in John is the idiom of mission, and mission in this Gospel is predicated symmetrically of the disciples (20:21) without making them lesser gods or the Son a greater disciple; the asymmetry is of role and origin-relation, and the same texts predicate of the Son what no creature is given (life in himself, all judgment, raising the dead at the last day by his own voice, 5:25-29). The will distinction by itself proves a distinction of willing referenced to two owners; converting it into a distinction of worth or nature requires premises John contradicts in the same paragraphs. Declined as exegesis; its strongest texts (14:28) are real and belong to Part VII's wider dossier, where the tradition has always read them within the economy of the incarnation, but 6:38 supplies them no support.
2. **The reductionist reading under monothelite pressure: "my will" is a manner of speaking, with no real human volition behind it.** In its strongest form: two wills in one person would risk opposition, and opposition in Christ is impious; the unity of the person is better protected by taking the possessive as idiom (the will he speaks of is the one divine will, considered as his because the person is his), and John's lack of a Gethsemane struggle supports the idiom reading. Response: John does lack the Gethsemane scene, but he does not lack the human interiority the reduction needs him to lack: the soul troubled at the hour (12:27), the spirit troubled at the betrayal (13:21), the groaning and tears at Bethany (11:33-35). The Fourth Gospel protects the unity differently, by constancy of alignment (finding 2), not by evacuation of affect or volition. And the possessive construction is the same one Luke uses where the tradition, including the monothelites' opponents and eventually the council, took the human will as real; an idiom reading of John 6:38 that would also evacuate Luke 22:42 proves too much, and one that evacuates John alone is special pleading. The exegetical datum of finding 1 stands. Declined, while noting the concern it answers (no opposition of wills in Christ) is one the dyothelite reading equally honors, by following rather than by absence.
3. **The functional reading: the verse is about mission alignment, not about the metaphysics of the will.** In its strongest form: ho pempsas me is agency language (the sent agent acts the sender's commission, and the sender is counted present in the agent's act, compare 13:20); in that idiom, "not my will but the sender's" states the agent's fidelity to commission, and pressing the sentence for a count of natural wills is a category error against its genre. Response: the register claim is right and this study adopts it (section 2): the verse's direct assertion is mission-functional, and its soteriological content (6:39-40) is exactly the commission's content. What does not follow is the closure: that because the assertion is functional, it carries no implication for what Christ is. Agency idiom in John is predicated of one who also claims descent from heaven and life in himself; faithful agency of this kind, sustained without exception (8:29) and costing what 12:27 shows it cost, raises the question of whose willing is being aligned, and the possessive distinction is the text's own. Part VII therefore uses the verse as the functional reading says it must be used, as mission language, and then takes the further synthetic step with the wider dossier, tagged as synthesis (finding 7), rather than pretending the step is exegesis of this verse alone. Partially accepted (register), declined (closure).
4. **The dyothelite reading adopted by the treatise.** The distinction of 6:38 (with Luke 22:42) is a distinction of natural wills within the one person: the Son possesses a true human will (thelema to emon) which, in the economy of the mission, freely and constantly does the divine will of the sender, without opposition and without being erased. Its exegetical basis in this unit: finding 1 (two will-references, one "I"), finding 2 (alignment without evacuation; recoil present, counter-willing absent), finding 6 (the lexical reality of the thing negated), and the set's spine (4:34; 5:30; 8:29). Its limits are stated in finding 7: the two-natures framework and the formula are conciliar, and the full treatment is Phase 2 (Constantinople III and Maximus) drawing on units 07 and 08 together. Adopted, at the level of theological synthesis built on a HIGH-confidence exegetical datum.

## 6. Textual notes

The text of 6:38 is stable in everything that bears on this study: every witness and every version consulted agrees on the descent claim, the purpose negation, and the two will phrases. Three minor variants are attested and are recorded honestly. First, the preposition of the descent: the critical text reads apo tou ouranou at 6:38 (apo, Strong's 575); ek tou ouranou is the Byzantine and Textus Receptus reading at this verse (the critical text does read ek at 6:33, 41, 42, 50, 51, and 58). No finding turns on the preposition. Second, a placement variant for the negation: some witnesses (Sinaiticus and Old Latin b, e, as reported in the AICNT footnotes displayed with the parallel text) read the ou in the order "come down from heaven, not," effectively attaching the negation to the descent rather than to the hina clause; translations divide over reflecting it, and the sense on either placement is that the descent was not for his own will, since the alla clause fixes the contrast. Third, a clarifying substitution: Codex Bezae (D) and Old Latin witnesses (a, d, ff2) read "the Father" in place of "the one who sent me," the same clarification the KJV tradition shows at 5:30, where the critical text likewise lacks the explicit "Father." Neither variant touches thelema, poio, or katabebeka, and neither alters any finding. The NET Bible display consulted carries translator's notes around 6:37-40 (on "never send away," "raise them all up," and the last day) but no textual (tc) note flagging 6:38, consistent with its text following the standard critical text without a contested unit at this verse. [Textual, MEDIUM-HIGH: the substance verdict is secure on the versions and the NET display; the variant attributions rest on the AICNT footnotes as displayed, not on the NA28 apparatus consulted directly, section 8]
