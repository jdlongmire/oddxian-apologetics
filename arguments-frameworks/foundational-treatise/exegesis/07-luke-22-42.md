## 1. Text and translation

Luke's text, in transliteration: "Pater, ei boulei parenegkein touto to poterion ap' emou; plen me to thelema mou alla to son ginestho." Literally: "Father, if you are willing, to carry away this cup from me; nevertheless, not my will but yours be done (come to be)." The request's verb is parenegkein, aorist infinitive of paraphero, "to carry away, remove" (the NET note, citing BDAG 772, observes that Luke's verb is less exact than Matthew's parelthato, "let it pass by," which suggests alteration in the plan without the cup touching him). The turn of the prayer is plen, "nevertheless," and its close is ginestho, aorist imperative of ginomai: let yours come to be.

The parallels, in transliteration:
- **Matthew 26:39:** "Pater mou, ei dynaton estin, parelthato ap' emou to poterion touto; plen ouch hos ego thelo all' hos sy." ("My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will.")
- **Mark 14:36:** "Abba ho pater, panta dynata soi; parenegke to poterion touto ap' emou; all' ou ti ego thelo alla ti sy." ("Abba, Father, all things are possible for you; remove this cup from me; but not what I will, but what you will.")

Matthew adds two further prayers. In the second (26:42) the "my will" clause is gone: "Pater mou, ei ou dynatai touto parelthein ean me auto pio, genetheto to thelema sou" ("My Father, if this cannot pass unless I drink it, your will be done"), and the third prayer (26:44) repeats the same words. The pattern's source is the Lord's Prayer itself: "genetheto to thelema sou" (Matthew 6:10), the exact wording Matthew's Jesus takes up in the second prayer.

Translation comparison for Luke 22:42 (conditional / rendering of plen):

| Version | Conditional | Plen rendered |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | if thou be willing | nevertheless (colon before it) |
| ESV | if you are willing | Nevertheless (new sentence) |
| NASB | if You are willing | yet (semicolon before it) |
| NIV | if you are willing | yet (semicolon before it) |
| NET | if you are willing | Yet (new sentence) |
| CSB | if you are willing | nevertheless (set off by a dash) |

All six keep the conditional as a condition on the Father's willing, none softens "my will" (to thelema mou) into anything weaker, and the variation is confined to how strongly plen breaks the sentence: KJV, ESV, and CSB mark the turn heavily ("nevertheless"), NASB, NIV, and NET more lightly ("yet"). No version translates the recoil away.

## 2. Context

Luke places the prayer on the Mount of Olives after the Supper, inside a frame Jesus himself sets: "Pray that you may not enter into temptation" (22:40), repeated on his return (22:46). He withdraws about a stone's throw, kneels, and prays. The disciples sleep. Three contextual controls. First, the prayer is Jesus enacting, under the real approach of the cup, the very petition he taught: the one who instructed his disciples to pray "your will be done" now prays it where it costs. Second, the scene is private in its narrative presentation: the prayer is overheard tradition, not a public lesson delivered to an audience; the disciples are asleep and at a distance. Third, Luke's own chapter has already put a cup in Jesus' hands: "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" (22:20). The cup he asks to have removed is the same event viewed from the side of wrath; the cup he gives is the same event viewed from the side of covenant. John's Gospel, which has no garden prayer, supplies the canonical cross-light in other words: "Shall I not drink the cup that the Father has given me?" (John 18:11), and John 12:27, where the request "Father, save me from this hour" is raised and refused by Jesus himself in a single breath.

## 3. Grammar and word studies

### 3.1 Ei boulei: the conditional and boulomai

Boulei is present indicative of boulomai (Strong's G1014): to will, wish, intend, with a deliberative coloring stronger than a bare thelo; in the New Testament it is used of settled intention as well as desire. Ei with the indicative presents the condition as a real supposition for the purpose at hand, not as a doubt cast on the fact. Addressed to the Father, "if you are willing" is a deference formula: it locates the decision wholly in the Father's will and asserts nothing about Jesus' uncertainty concerning the Father's power. Mark removes any ambiguity about power by making it a declaration: "all things are possible for you" (panta dynata soi). Matthew's condition runs on a different axis, possibility rather than willingness: "if it is possible" (ei dynaton estin), which his second prayer glosses from within the event: "if this cannot pass unless I drink it." Read together, the three forms agree that the prayer's condition is not ignorance of what the Father can do, and not a proposal that the Father has not considered; it is the form submission takes when a real alternative is really asked for and really yielded. [Explicit text, HIGH]

### 3.2 Poterion: the cup

Poterion (Strong's G4221) is a drinking cup, used figuratively in the Gospels only of suffering (Hastings' Dictionary of the New Testament, article "Cup": Matthew 20:22-23 and Mark 10:38-39, the cup of the passion challenged to James and John; Mark 14:36 and parallels; John 18:11). The figure is the Old Testament's: the cup of the LORD's wrath handed to the nations to drink (Jeremiah 25:15), the cup of fury and the dregs of the cup of staggering drunk by Jerusalem (Isaiah 51:17, 22), the cup in the LORD's hand, full of foaming wine, poured out for the wicked (Psalm 75:8; compare Psalm 11:6, cited in the NET note on this verse). What Jesus recoils from, then, is not death in the abstract and not pain in the abstract: it is the passion as the drinking of the wrath cup, the bearing of judgment in the place of the judged. The recoil's object matters for section 4: aversion to that cup is aversion to its content, and its content is the Father's own appointed judgment, which is why the prayer can ask for its removal only under the condition "if you are willing." [Explicit text, HIGH for the figure's Old Testament background and reference; the substitutionary gloss is the treatise's framework, Theological synthesis, HIGH within it]

### 3.3 Plen and ginestho: the turn and the conclusion

Plen (Strong's G4133) is an adversative particle: "nevertheless, however, but," marking a turn against what precedes without canceling it. The request is not withdrawn; it is subordinated. The prayer's grammar therefore does not say "I did not mean it"; it says "not this, if it comes to the choice, but yours." Ginestho (Luke) and genetheto (Matthew 6:10; 26:42) are aorist imperatives of ginomai, "be done, come to be": the Father's will is petitioned as an event to be realized, in the garden as in the daily prayer, "on earth as in heaven." [Explicit text, HIGH]

### 3.4 To thelema mou, to son: the two possessives

Thelema (Strong's G2307; Thayer, p. 285) denotes both, objectively, "what one wishes or has determined shall be done," and, subjectively, "the act of willing, will, choice, inclination, desire, pleasure." The word occurs 62 times in the New Testament, most often of God's will. In this verse Jesus sets the word over against itself with two possessives: to thelema mou, "my will," and to son, "yours" (the noun carried over). Matthew and Mark use the verb instead: ou... ego thelo alla... sy, "not what I will, but what you will." On any of the three forms, the sentence requires two distinguishable acts or objects of willing, one predicated of Jesus and one of the Father, standing in a relation that the prayer itself resolves: not opposition maintained, and not identity assumed, but subordination freely spoken. The grammar gives the two-wills doctrine its raw material and its limits at once: distinction, yes; the metaphysical analysis of how one person wills in two natures is dogmatics, not grammar, and is deferred to Part VII with its Phase 2 register (Constantinople III; Maximus on the natural and gnomic will). [Explicit text, HIGH for the distinction as predicated; the dogmatic construction is deferred, not claimed]

### 3.5 The parallels compared

| Element | Luke 22:42 | Matthew 26:39 | Mark 14:36 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Address | Pater | Pater mou | Abba ho pater |
| Condition | ei boulei (if you are willing) | ei dynaton estin (if it is possible) | none; panta dynata soi declared |
| Removal verb | parenegkein (carry away) | parelthato (let it pass) | parenegke (carry away) |
| Adversative | plen | plen | alla |
| Will clause | to thelema mou / to son ginestho | ouch hos ego thelo all' hos sy | ou ti ego thelo alla ti sy |
| Repetition | once | three prayers; "my will" clause only in the first | once |

Two features deserve note. Mark's "Abba" is the prayer's most intimate address and stands in the account with the starkest will-contrast; intimacy and distinction are not in tension in the text. And Matthew's repetition pattern is itself exegesis: by the second prayer the contrast clause has done its work and disappears, leaving only "genetheto to thelema sou," the Lord's Prayer unadorned. The narrative shows the alignment completed, not merely announced. [Explicit text, HIGH for the forms; literary observation, MEDIUM for the reading of the repetition pattern]

## 4. Findings

1. **Jesus predicates thelema of himself in real distinction from the Father's.** "Not my will, but yours" is intelligible only if "my will" names something genuinely his and genuinely distinguishable. A reading on which the human will of Jesus is absent, overridden, or a mere appearance must explain why the prayer's central sentence is built on a contrast that, on that reading, does not exist. This is the exegetical root of the two-wills confession, against monothelite and docetic pressure alike. [Explicit text, HIGH]
2. **The recoil is from the cup, and it is sinless.** Its stated object is poterion, the wrath-bearing passion (section 3.2), not the Father's authority and not obedience as such; the conditional submits the request before it is finished being spoken. Natural human aversion to suffering and death is here presented without any mark of fault, correction, or repentance, and Jesus rises from the prayer and goes to meet the arrest (22:47). Aversion to the cup is not a will to disobey. [Necessary inference, HIGH]
3. **The alignment is free and unforced.** Nothing in the scene depicts the human will as bypassed, coerced, or replaced: the submission is itself the human will's own act, spoken in the plen clause. The prayer therefore exhibits what Constantinople III would later confess in form: a human will following and subject to the divine, not opposed; the exegesis supplies the exhibition, the council supplies the formula, and the treatise keeps the two in that order. [Explicit text, HIGH for the exhibition; the conciliar correspondence is a pointer to Phase 2]
4. **The prayer gives the impeccability discussion its exegetical root without settling it.** What the text shows is a human will that articulates a contrary preference at the level of natural recoil and does not, at the level of choice, entertain refusal: the "not my will" is not a temptation resisted at the brink but the prayer's own settled conclusion, repeated in Matthew until only the Father's will is being named. Whether this entails that Jesus could not have willed refusal (impeccability as the tradition argues it, from the hypostatic union and the divine person's willing through the human nature) is a dogmatic inference from this and other texts (John 6:38, unit 08; Hebrews 4:15, unit 09), not a deliverance of this verse alone. The working paper's tag stands: MEDIUM, and this study neither raises nor lowers it. [Scope judgment, HIGH; the inference itself remains Theological synthesis, MEDIUM]
5. **Nothing here rests on 22:43-44.** Every finding above is drawn from verse 42 and its undisputed parallels. The angel and the sweat, whether original or an early insertion (section 6), would illustrate the cost of the praying; they carry none of its logic. [Explicit text / textual, HIGH]

## 5. Competing readings

1. **The purely exemplary reading: the prayer is pedagogy, Jesus modeling how disciples should pray, with no real interior distinction of wills.** Strongest form: Luke's Gospel presents Jesus at prayer more than any other, as the disciples' example; the scene is framed by his command to pray against temptation (22:40, 46); the church has always prayed Gethsemane as its school of submission. Response: the exemplary function is real and this study affirms it (section 2). But as an exhaustive account it fails on the text: the contrast mou / son, the conditional about the cup, and a prayer prayed while the supposed pupils sleep a stone's throw away, all resist reduction to a staged lesson; and a recoil performed rather than felt would be the docetic theater the tradition rejected. A model prayer it is; merely a model, it is not. Partially accepted as function, declined as exhaustion.
2. **The genuinely divided will: Jesus' human will stood in real conflict with the Father's and might have refused.** Strongest form: prayer that cannot fail is not prayer; freedom that cannot choose otherwise is not freedom; Hebrews 5:7's loud cries and tears are not the sounds of a settled outcome; the honesty of the recoil (finding 2) demands the real possibility of the refusal it recoils toward. Response: the valid core is retained: the recoil is real, the asking is real, the freedom is real. But the text nowhere represents refusal as a live option entertained and narrowly defeated; the plen clause is not a victory report after a struggle between two choosers, it is the prayer's own grammar from its first utterance, in all three accounts, and Matthew's repetitions move away from the contrast, not deeper into it. The theological cost of the stronger claim must also be stated: if the human will might have settled against the Father, then the cross's certainty rests on a creaturely decision that could have gone the other way, the union's impeccability becomes a fortunate outcome rather than a confession, and the "will that might refuse" begins to function as an independent center of self-determination alongside the person of the Son, which is the Nestorian pressure the two-wills doctrine exists to refuse. Freedom in the prayer is spontaneous alignment, not indifference between obedience and refusal. Declined as stated.
3. **The two-wills reading adopted by the treatise.** Jesus, one person, wills in his human nature a real thelema distinct from the Father's; the distinction is exhibited in the contrast clause; the subordination is free (finding 3); the recoil is sinless (finding 2); the confession of Constantinople III, two natural wills with the human following and not opposed, is this prayer's dogmatic transcription. Its Phase 2 exposition, including Maximus's distinction between the natural will and the gnomic (deliberative, wavering) mode of willing, is where the remaining difficulties belong; the exegesis here neither needs nor attempts them.

## 6. Textual notes

**Verse 42 itself is secure.** It stands in every witness to the passage; no form of the two-wills material depends on a disputed word. The only variant of note is a transposition in Codex Bezae (D) and some Old Latin witnesses, which reorder the clauses ("not my will, but yours be done; if you wish, remove this cup from me"), leaving the sense intact. The textual dispute in this passage concerns verses 43-44 alone, and it must not be allowed to cast a shadow it does not cast: the prayer of verse 42 is not the bracketed text.

**Verses 43-44** (the angel from heaven strengthening him; the agony, the more earnest prayer, the sweat like great drops, thromboi, of blood falling to the ground; hosei marks a simile, sweat like clots or drops of blood, not a statement that the sweat was blood). The NET textual note reports the evidence as follows. The verses are lacking in P75, the original hand of Sinaiticus, A, B, N, T, W, 579, 1071*, and diverse and widespread versional witnesses. They are present in the corrected Sinaiticus, D, L, Theta, Psi, 0171, family 1, the Byzantine majority, and the Latin tradition, and are cited by Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Hippolytus, and Eusebius; the patristic attestation reaches the second century, earlier than any surviving codex that omits them. Family 13 transposes the verses to follow Matthew 26:39, and a number of manuscripts mark them with an asterisk or obelisk, recording a scribe's judgment against them. Metzger's Textual Commentary (p. 151, quoted in a secondary reproduction) judges it "less likely that the verses were deleted in several different areas of the church by those who felt that the account of Jesus being overwhelmed with human weakness was incompatible with his sharing the divine omnipotence of the Father, than that they were added from an early source, oral or written, of extra-canonical traditions concerning the life and passion of Jesus"; the UBS committee rated the longer text {C}, the scale's mark of genuine difficulty. The two transcriptional theories remain the live options: addition, very early, as an anti-docetic strengthening of Jesus' real humanity (the thesis of Ehrman and Plunkett, CBQ 45 [1983], cited in the NET note), or excision, in some strands of the tradition, from Christological discomfort with a Son who needs strengthening.

**Verdict.** The critical editions (NA28, UBS5) print the verses in double brackets, the convention for material judged an early insertion into the tradition. This study follows the NET note's own disposition: the verses are probably not literarily original to Luke, and probably historically authentic tradition about the event. Confidence MEDIUM: the external evidence is genuinely divided between early weight on both sides, which is why the committee's {C} is the honest rating, and any verdict stronger than MEDIUM would overclaim the apparatus. For the treatise the question is academic in the strict sense: finding 5 records that nothing in Part VII's use of this passage may rest on verses 43-44, and nothing does.
