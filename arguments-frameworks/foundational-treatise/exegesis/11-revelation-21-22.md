# Exegetical Study 11: Revelation 21-22

**Phase 1, unit 11.** Gates treatise Part VIII. This is the final Phase 1 study.
**Builds on:** the working paper abstract and section 3 (outline coverage of the consummation), and unit 01 (Genesis 1:31), whose terms this study must answer: creation pronounced *tov meod*, fitting, excellent of its kind, and approved, with the vocabulary of incorruption and completion (tamim absolute, shalem) withheld from Eden.
**Gap assigned to this study:** a full study of the consummation texts against Genesis 1-3: whether Revelation 21-22 presents the final state as confirmed and incorruptible where Eden was mutable, how much continuity it asserts between the first creation and the new, and what it does not say.
**Status:** Complete (working draft). Greek text as displayed in the interlinear and discourse sources consulted (mediation labeled in section 7); BDAG not consulted directly (open item, section 8). All Greek is transliterated.

- **21:2.** "Kai ten polin ten hagian Ierousalem kainen eidon katabainousan ek tou ouranou apo tou theou, hetoimasmenen hos nymphen kekosmenen to andri autes." ("And I saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband.")

## 2. Context

**Placement in the book.** The vision of chapters 21-22 follows the great white throne judgment (20:11-15), at which death and Hades give up their dead and are themselves thrown into the lake of fire (20:13-14). The sequence inside Revelation's own plot is therefore: judgment, the disposal of death, and then the new heaven and new earth. The "no more death" of 21:4 is narrated as the outcome of 20:14, not as a free-standing symbol. The city descends (21:2, 10) after that judgment; nothing in the two chapters reopens what the judgment closed. At the city's boundary the exclusion is stated twice, at the end of each chapter's description: nothing common enters (21:27), and outside are the finally excluded (22:15); within the city, the conqueror's inheritance and the second death for the unbelieving stand at 21:7-8 as the two sides of the same boundary.

**The canon arc: Genesis 1-3 reversed.** The closing chapters systematically take up and reverse the opening chapters. The echoes are of two kinds, and the study distinguishes them because Part VIII will lean on them with different weights.

Lexical echoes (same Greek lexemes or phrases, through the Septuagint):

- **The tree of life.** Genesis 2:9 LXX names "to xylon tes zoes," the tree of life, "in the midst of the garden" (en meso tou paradeisou). Revelation 22:2 sets "xylon zoes" in the midst (en meso) of the city's street; 22:14 grants a right (exousia) to the tree of life; 22:19 warns against losing one's share in it. The phrase is identical, and the geography is identical: the tree stands in the middle.
- **The river.** Genesis 2:10 LXX has a river (potamos) flowing out of Eden to water the garden. Revelation 22:1 has a river (potamos hydatos zoes) flowing out (ekporeuomenon) from the throne. The nearer formal source is Ezekiel 47:1-12, the river from the temple threshold that makes the sea fresh and feeds trees whose fruit is for food and leaves for healing; Zechariah 14:8 (living waters from Jerusalem) stands behind both. The lexeme potamos and the flowing-out participle are shared; the source has moved from the garden's ground to God's throne.
- **The curse formula.** Zechariah 14:11 LXX closes its Jerusalem vision with "kai anathema ouk estai eti," "and a cursed thing shall be no more." Revelation 22:3 reads "kai pan katathema ouk estai eti." The clause frame (ouk estai eti, "shall not be any longer") and the anathema root are shared; John adds pan ("every") and uses the strengthened compound katathema (section 3.5). This is the lexical bridge between the curse of Genesis 3 and its removal, and it runs through Zechariah, not directly through Genesis's own vocabulary.
- **The crying abolished.** Isaiah 65:19 LXX promises that "there shall no more be heard in her the voice of weeping and the voice of crying" (phone klauthmou kai phone krauges). Revelation 21:4 abolishes krauge (crying) itself, with mourning and pain. Isaiah 65:17 likewise supplies the "new heaven and new earth" (ouranos kainos, ge kaine) that 21:1 takes up; Isaiah 25:8 (God swallows up death and wipes tears from all faces) stands behind 21:4's first two clauses.
- **God's dwelling.** "He skene tou theou meta ton anthropon" (21:3) is the covenant formula of Leviticus 26:11-12 and Ezekiel 37:27 in its consummated form: God tabernacles (skenosei) with his peoples. The verb is the one used of the incarnation at John 1:14 (eskenosen en hemin); at the end, the tabernacling is permanent and unmediated.

Thematic echoes (same realities, different lexemes):

- **The curse itself.** Genesis 3:17 LXX curses the ground with the adjective epikataratos ("epikataratos he ge en tois ergois sou"); the serpent is likewise epikataratos (3:14). Revelation's katathema is a different word from a different tradition (the cherem, the thing devoted to destruction; Thayer gives cherem, Strong's H2764, as anathema's Hebrew equivalent). The reversal of the Genesis curse in 22:3 is therefore canonical and conceptual, not a word-for-word quotation; Part VIII must argue it as the removal of the cursed condition as such (pan, every cursed thing), not as a lexical citation of Genesis 3.
- **Toil and pain.** The ponos of 21:4 answers the sorrows and toil of Genesis 3:16-19 conceptually; the Septuagint's own word there is lype (en lypais, 3:17), not ponos. The study registers the answer as thematic.
- **Death.** Thanatos is named at both ends of the canon (the LXX of Genesis 2:17 and 3:4 speaks of dying by death, thanato), but in Revelation death is nearly a person: it rides (6:8), receives the dead into Hades, and is finally thrown into the lake of fire (20:14) before it is said to be no more (21:4). The abolition in 21:4 is the narrative completion of that arc.
- **Garden to garden-city.** The garden's features return inside a city: river, tree, fruit, leaves. The city is not a return to the garden; the garden is taken up into the city, whose temple is God and the Lamb themselves (21:22, "naon ouk eidon en aute," "I saw no temple in it, for the Lord God Almighty is its temple, and the Lamb"), whose gates are never shut (21:25, with the emphatic ou me), and into which the nations walk by its light and the kings of the earth bring their glory (21:24, 26). Human culture and rule, absent as such from Eden's description, belong to the consummated picture.
- **The sea.** Genesis 1 gathers the seas under God's rule; in Revelation the sea is the beast's place of origin (13:1) and the reservoir of the dead (20:13). Its absence at 21:1 ("he thalassa ouk estin eti") is the removal of the chaos-source and of every separation the sea figures in this book. The new earth is not waterless: it has the river of life (22:1). What is gone is the sea as threat. [Symbolic freight within this book's own usage, HIGH for the distribution; the "separation" gloss is the matrix's shorthand and is consistent with it]

## 3. Word studies

### 3.1 Kainos (Strong's G2537)

Strong's: "new (especially in freshness); while neos (G3501) is properly so with respect to age." Thayer's synonym note (from Trench) runs the same way: neos denotes the new primarily in reference to time, the young and recent; kainos the new primarily in reference to quality, the fresh and unworn, that which is set over against what has seen service and been marred through use. The caution belongs in the record with the distinction: the two words overlap in usage, and the distinction is a tendency of the lexemes, not an invariable law; it cannot by itself decide the continuity question of section 5. But the distribution in the consummation passage is one-sided. John uses kainos for heaven (21:1), earth (21:1), Jerusalem (21:2), and the totality in God's own speech (21:5, kaina poio panta); earlier in the book he uses it for the new name (2:17; 3:12), the new song (5:9; 14:3), and the new name Christ bears (3:12). He never uses neos anywhere in Revelation. The consummation's newness is uniformly kainos-newness: fresh in kind, not merely next in sequence. [Lexical, HIGH for the distribution; MEDIUM for the weight the quality-sense bears in any single occurrence]

### 3.2 The verb frames: apelthan and poio, estai eti

Three grammatical frames carry the passage's claims.

- **The passing verb.** Aperchomai in the aorist: apelthan, "they passed away, departed," at 21:1 (of the first heaven and the first earth) and at 21:4 (ta prota, "the former things," the whole former order summed in a neuter plural). The verb is a departure verb, not an annihilation verb: John does not write that the first creation was destroyed or unmade, but that it went away, as a departed order. The same verb at 18:14 describes Babylon's fruits departing from her. The aorist is constative: the passing is viewed as a completed whole from the standpoint of the new order that has arrived.
- **The making declaration.** At 21:5 the object and the predicate stand in the order kaina poio panta: the adjective kaina is fronted as the predicate complement, poio ("I make," present tense) is the verb, and panta ("all things") is the object. God does not say "I make all new things"; he says he makes all things new. The panta that is made kainos is the same totality the passage has been describing (heaven, earth, city, nations), now stated without remainder. The present tense poio declares the act from the throne as the program of the state John is seeing: the enthroned One speaks here in his own voice, the book's most direct divine utterance since the opening vision (1:8).
- **The no-longer chain.** The adverb eti ("still, any longer") with the future of eimi runs through the passage like a refrain: the sea is no more (ouk estin eti, present, 21:1); death shall be no more (ouk estai eti, 21:4, twice); every cursed thing shall be no more (ouk estai eti, 22:3); night shall be no more (ouk estai eti, 22:5; cf. 21:25). The negatives are absolute within their subjects: not diminished, not intermittent, but no longer existing at all in the described state. At 21:27 the negation is the strongest the language offers, ou me with the subjunctive: by no means, never.

### 3.3 Thanatos, penthos, krauge, ponos (21:4)

The four abolished nouns name the fall's whole economy of sorrow, and each has a profile.

- **Thanatos (G2288), death.** In Revelation death is an agent (6:8) and a power that holds the dead until the judgment (20:13) and is then destroyed (20:14). Its abolition at 21:4 is therefore not the mere observation that people stop dying; the text has narrated the destruction of the destroyer first. The verse also quietly answers unit 05's text: the chain that ends in glorification (Romans 8:30) ends here, in a state where death's non-existence is predicated outright.
- **Penthos (G3997), mourning, grief.** The word's other occurrences in the book fall on Babylon: she says she will never see mourning (18:7), and mourning comes on her in a day (18:8). In the city, the same word is abolished. What Babylon boasts of escaping and then suffers, the bride simply does not have.
- **Krauge (G2906), crying, outcry.** The cry of distress; within Revelation the word occurs only here. Its Septuagintal anchor is Isaiah 65:19 (phone krauges, the voice of crying no longer heard). John strengthens Isaiah: not only is the sound unheard, the crying itself shall not be.
- **Ponos (G4192), pain, toil.** The word covers both labor's toil and suffering's pain; in Revelation the beast's kingdom gnaws its tongues "ek tou ponou" (16:10-11). At 21:4 the whole range is abolished: no pain of body and no toil of curse. This is the Greek-side counterpart to the toil (itsavon) of Genesis 3:17-19 studied in unit 02, at the thematic level (the LXX word there is lype; section 2).

The four nouns are the conditions of the fallen order stated as a closed list, and the clause that grounds them is causal: "hoti ta prota apelthan," because the former things passed away. The abolition is not arbitrary comfort; it follows from the passing of the order whose conditions they were.

### 3.4 Koinon (Strong's G2839) at 21:27: the actual word

The assignment named akathartos for this verse; the Greek text does not have it, and the study reports the word John wrote. The adjective at 21:27 is koinon, from koinos. Strong's: "common, i.e. (by implication) shared by all or several, or profane." In Jewish Greek usage the word's cultic sense dominates: what is common is what is not set apart as holy, and so defiling by contact with the sacred; in Mark 7:2 the disciples eat with koinais hands, common (unwashed, defiled) hands; at Acts 10:14-15 Peter protests that he has never eaten "anything common or unclean (koinon e akatharton)," and the heavenly voice answers, "what God has cleansed, do not call common (koinon)." Two consequences follow. First, koinon and akatharton are neighbors, not synonyms: Peter's speech holds them side by side as distinguishable, and the English versions' "unclean" at 21:27 (ESV, NASB; NIV "impure") reads John's word through its neighbor. The HCSB's "profane" preserves the lexeme's own force. Second, the exclusion at 21:27 is framed as the antithesis of the city's holiness: the city is hagia, holy (21:2, 10), and what is merely common, un-sanctified, does not enter it. The verse's second member then names persons by their practice (the one doing bdelygma, abomination, G946, and pseudos, falsehood), and its third names the sole ground of entrance: inscription in the Lamb's book of life. Exclusion is thus stated in cultic terms, moral terms, and book-of-life terms together.

Akathartos (G169, "unclean, impure," ceremonially, morally, and of demons; Thayer via BibleHub) does occur in Revelation, at 16:13 (three unclean spirits, pneumata akatharta), 17:4 (the akatharta, the impurities, of Babylon's sexual immorality), and 18:2 (Babylon as the haunt of every unclean spirit and bird). The book's uncleanness vocabulary is spent on the demonic and on Babylon; at the gate of the city John reaches instead for the common/holy antithesis. Part VIII should reproduce John's precision rather than the versions' smoothing: the consummated exclusion is not only that the impure are shut out, but that nothing un-sanctified is inside.

### 3.5 Katathema (Strong's G2652) at 22:3

The noun is a New Testament hapax legomenon. Strong's entry (G2652, entered under the fuller form katanathema) derives it from kata (intensive) and anathema (G331): "an imprecation: curse." In the printed text at 22:3 the word stands in the shorter form katathema; Robertson's word study notes that no other example of the katathema form had been found outside the Didache (16:5), that it may be a syncopated form of katanathema, and that the cognate verb (to curse vehemently) appears at Matthew 26:74. The underlying anathema carries the Septuagint's cherem sense: a thing devoted to God without hope of redemption, devoted to destruction (so Thayer on G331: "a thing devoted to God without hope of being redeemed... a person or thing doomed to destruction"). The Hebrew behind Zechariah 14:11's promise is the same cherem: Jerusalem will dwell securely because the ban, the devoted-to-destruction curse, will exist no longer.

Three features of John's clause carry weight. First, **pan**: "every cursed thing," with the universal negation Robertson notes (pan with ouk amounts to "nothing at all"): no remainder is contemplated, no corner of the renewed order where a cursed thing persists. Second, **the clause is unlocalized**: 22:3 does not say the curse is absent only within the walls; it says every cursed thing shall not be, and then, as a further statement, that the throne of God and of the Lamb shall be in the city. The curse's abolition is a fact about the new order as such, and the throne's presence in the city is given as its ruling evidence. Third, **the frame is Zechariah's** (section 2): the promise John takes up was a promise about Jerusalem's security, and John reads it at the scale of the whole consummation.

For the treatise, the finding is that the removal of the curse is stated in the strongest available terms (a hapax of maximum intensity, a universal quantifier, an absolute negation, an eternal frame supplied by 22:5), while its lexical line to Genesis 3 runs through the canon (cherem, Zechariah) rather than through Genesis's own epikataratos vocabulary. The dogmatic connection to Genesis 3 is sound; the citation method must be canonical synthesis, not verbal quotation.

### 3.6 Doulos, latreuo, basileuo (22:3-5): the consummated vocation

The inhabitants of the city are named by their relation and their activity, and the two together correct any picture of the consummation as either idle rest or autonomous rule.

- **Douloi autou (G1401), "his servants,"** literally his slaves: the people belong to God. The word is the book's standing name for God's own (1:1; 7:3; 19:2, 5; 22:6), and it is applied to the consummated community without embarrassment: perfected freedom is confessed in the language of ownership.
- **Latreusousin auto (G3000), "they shall serve him."** Latreuo is the New Testament's cultic service verb: the service rendered to God in worship, which the old order localized in priests and temple. At 7:15 the redeemed "serve him day and night in his temple" (latreusousin auto hemeras kai nyktos en to nao autou); at 22:3 there is no temple building, because God and the Lamb are the temple (21:22), and the service continues without night to interrupt it. Robertson notes the future's linear aspect: they shall keep on serving. The priestly vocation of Exodus 19:6 and 1 Peter 2:9 is the consummated norm, not an interim office; and it is crowned by what no priest of the old order could have: "they shall see his face" (22:4), with his name on their foreheads, as the high priest bore the plate inscribed "Holy to the LORD" (Exodus 28:36-38, thematic).
- **Basileusousin eis tous aionas ton aionon (G936), "they shall reign forever and ever."** The reign promised at 5:10 ("they shall reign on the earth") and shared with Christ at 20:4, 6 is here stated in its final, unending form. The duration formula is the book's maximum: the same eis tous aionas ton aionon measures the life of God (4:9-10) and the praise of the Lamb. Service and reign are not two destinies; the same subjects bear both verbs in consecutive verses. The Adamic commission (rule, Genesis 1:26-28) and the priestly commission (serve and keep, Genesis 2:15) are both fulfilled, permanently, by the same people, at the same time. [The Genesis 1-2 commission linkage is theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH; the simultaneity of the two verbs is explicit text, HIGH]

### 3.7 Xylon and potamos (22:1-2)

Xylon (G3586) is wood, and by extension a tree; it is the Septuagint's word for Eden's trees (Genesis 2:9, every xylon good for food, and to xylon tes zoes) and the New Testament's word for the cross (Acts 5:30; 10:39; Galatians 3:13; 1 Peter 2:24). John's tree of life is grammatically singular with a collective sense: one xylon zoes stands on both sides of the river (enteuthen kai ekeithen), bearing twelve crops of fruit, one for each month, without season's interruption. The perpetual fruitfulness replaces Eden's guarded and finally barred tree (Genesis 3:22-24, the cherubim and the flaming sword keeping "the way of the tree of life"): at 22:14 the right of access is granted (exousia epi to xylon tes zoes), and the gates through which the blessed enter are never shut (21:25). Therapeia (G2322, healing, medical care) for the nations closes the arc of the nations' wounding under the curse; read alongside 21:4 (no more pain), the leaves cannot signify residual sickness inside the consummation, and the study takes them as the standing sign of the nations' wholeness, drawn from Ezekiel 47:12's form, rather than as evidence of continuing infirmity. [Synthesis, MEDIUM: the sign-reading is an inference from the tension with 21:4, which Part VIII must state as an inference]

## 4. Competing readings

1. **Full discontinuity: the first creation is annihilated and replaced.** Strongest form: the first heaven and earth "passed away" (21:1) as the earth and heavens of 2 Peter 3:10-13 are dissolved in fire; the sea, a major feature of the present earth, is simply gone; the repeated "no more" pattern describes a world with no material continuity with this one, and "new" (even kainos) can denote a fresh creation, as a new garment replaces an old one. The reading protects the passage's absolute negations from being diluted into renovation. *Adjudication.* Accepted: the discontinuity of conditions is real and total: death, curse, mourning, sea, and night do not survive the transition in any measure. Declined: annihilation of the totality is not what the text's verbs say. Apelthan is a departure verb (section 3.2), and the making declaration takes the same panta as its object (kaina poio panta, 21:5): the things are made new, not exchanged for other things. Second Peter's dissolution language describes the same event from the side of the old order's perishable elements; it is not part of this unit's text, and this unit's text speaks of a totality that passes in its former condition and is made new in its substance. [Assessment: declined as an account of this text's own verbs, while its protection of the negations is retained, HIGH for the grammatical points]

2. **Renewal and transformation: the same creation, liberated and transfigured.** Strongest form: Romans 8:19-22 has this very creation (ktisis) groaning to be set free from its bondage to corruption into the freedom of the glory of God's children; the resurrection pattern (1 Corinthians 15) is transformation of the same body, not its replacement; Revelation's own grammar (the panta of 21:5, the descending city, the nations and kings of the earth carrying their glory in, 21:24-26) presupposes a world continuous enough to have nations, kings, glory, and history arrive in the consummation rather than be deleted by it. Eden's own features return (river, tree, presence), which is the narrative signature of the same story completed, not another story begun. *Adjudication.* Accepted as the reading this text supports, with the discontinuity of reading 1 held inside it: what passes away is the former order in its fallenness (ta prota, the first things, 21:4, defined by death, mourning, crying, pain, curse, and sea), and what is made new is all things (panta, 21:5). The text alone, without Romans 8, already forces the two-sided verdict: a purely cosmetic renewal cannot carry ouk estai eti, and a pure replacement cannot carry poio panta. [Assessment: accepted as disciplined by reading 1's negations, MEDIUM-HIGH: the grammar is firm; the metaphysical account of how the same totality persists through the passing belongs to Part VIII's synthesis]

3. **Purely symbolic-ecclesial: the new Jerusalem is the present church, and the vision makes no future cosmic claim.** Strongest form: the city is expressly identified as people, "the bride, the wife of the Lamb" (21:9-10); Hebrews 12:22 tells present believers "you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem"; Galatians 4:26 calls the Jerusalem above "our mother"; the gates never shut and the nations stream in (21:24-25) reads naturally as the church's present mission; on this reading, chapters 21-22 are the church's self-portrait under the figure of a city, and "no more death" is the proleptic language of a community that already tastes the powers of the age to come. *Adjudication.* Accepted: the city's identity as the people of God, and the inaugurated presence of the reality (Hebrews 12:22 is a genuine already). Declined: the evacuation of the future and the cosmic referent. The passage's own sequence forbids it: the vision follows the final judgment and the destruction of death (20:11-15; section 2), and its central negation, "death shall be no more," is falsified by every churchyard if it is predicated of the present age. A symbolic reading that must explain away the text's first abolished noun in order to stand has the direction of interpretation backwards. The already of Hebrews 12:22 and the not-yet of Revelation 21:4 are compatible as inauguration and consummation; only the second is this text's subject. [Assessment: partially accepted, HIGH on the sequence point]

## 5. Findings, tagged

1. **The consummated state is described by a chain of absolute negations.** Death, mourning, crying, and pain shall be no more (21:4); every cursed thing shall be no more (22:3); night shall be no more (22:5); the sea is no more (21:1); nothing common shall by any means enter the city (21:27, ou me). The negations are categorical in form, and the versions consulted do not soften them. [Explicit text, HIGH]
2. **Required verdict (a): the state's incorruptibility and confirmation.** As an explicit proposition ("a further fall is impossible"), confirmation is nowhere stated: the text does not discuss possibility at all. As an inference, it is as strong as the passage can make it. The state described excludes every consequence of the fall (death, pain, mourning, curse), every agent of corruption (nothing common enters; the practitioners of abomination and falsehood are outside, 21:27; 22:15; the second death has disposed of the finally impenitent, 21:8), and every occasion of renewal of the fall (no night, no sea of chaos), and it measures the inhabitants' reign by the book's maximum duration (eis tous aionas ton aionon, 22:5) under the immediate presence of God and the Lamb on the throne (21:3; 22:3). A state so defined has no fall left to undergo. The inference is therefore rated HIGH at the level of the state's described permanence. The further dogmatic label, that the saints in glory are confirmed in righteousness, non posse peccare, is a theological synthesis: it joins this text's exclusions to the canon's account of the saints' perseverance and to the Augustinian four-state scheme, and it describes the inhabitants' interior condition, about which Revelation 21-22 speaks only through the city's boundaries and its occupants' service and reign. [Necessary inference for the permanence, HIGH; Theological synthesis for the confirmation label and its application to the saints' wills, MEDIUM-HIGH]
3. **Required verdict (b): continuity and discontinuity.** The text asserts discontinuity of the order and continuity of the totality. The first heaven and earth pass away (apelthan, 21:1), and with them the former things (ta prota, 21:4) pass; but God's own summary of the event is "I make all things new" (kaina poio panta, 21:5), with panta, all things, as the object made kainos, and the book's dramatic movements agree: the city descends to the renewed earth rather than the redeemed ascending away from it (21:2), Eden's river and tree reappear inside the city (22:1-2), and the nations and kings of the earth arrive bearing their glory (21:24-26). Neither replacement without remainder nor improvement without rupture does justice to both verb families. [Grammatical and lexical observations, HIGH; the combined verdict as stated, Theological synthesis from those observations, MEDIUM-HIGH. Romans 8:21's liberation of creation, assigned to unit 05, converges with this verdict and is cited here only as convergence, not as this unit's evidence]
4. **Required verdict (c): what Revelation 21-22 does not say.** Three silences are load-bearing for Part VIII's honesty. (i) The text gives no reason why the first creation was mutable: no clause in the two chapters explains Eden's capacity to fall, and Genesis's own silence on the point (unit 01) is matched, not repaired, here. (ii) The text makes no claim that the fall was necessary to reach this state: it narrates the state that in fact follows redemption, and it nowhere says the river, the tree, the throne, or the reign required sin as their precondition; a felix culpa of the strong form (the fall as instrumentally necessary to the consummation's glory) cannot be cited from these chapters. (iii) The text supplies no mechanism of confirmation: it describes the consummated state's boundaries, occupants, and duration, and grounds the whole in God's dwelling and throne, but it does not state whether the state's permanence rests on the saints' transformed wills, on God's preserving presence, or on both; Part VIII must make that argument from the wider canon (and from the two-wills studies, units 07-08) and present it as synthesis, not as this text's teaching. [Explicit absences in a fully inventoried text, HIGH for each]
5. **The consummation exceeds Eden on Eden's own terms.** Where Genesis 1:31's tov meod asserted fitting excellence without the vocabulary of incorruption (unit 01, finding 2), Revelation 21-22 describes the end with exactly the vocabulary Genesis withheld: death abolished, curse abolished, entrance sealed against everything un-sanctified, reign measured by "forever and ever." The canonical trajectory from tov meod to the consummation is an increase in security and glory, not a restoration to a lost maximum and nothing more. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH: unit 01 rated the trajectory MEDIUM from its side; this unit's exclusions and duration formulas raise the combined confidence, and the rating records the joint assessment]
6. **The consummated human vocation is priestly service and royal reign together.** The inhabitants are God's douloi who render him latreia (22:3), see his face (22:4), and reign forever and ever (22:5). Rest in the consummation is not inactivity, and rule is not autonomy: both verbs describe a communion in which being God's own and exercising dominion coincide permanently. [Explicit text for the simultaneity of service and reign, HIGH; Theological synthesis for the communion framing, MEDIUM-HIGH]
7. **Entrance to the consummation is by the Lamb's book, and access to the tree is a granted right.** Inscription in the book of life is the sole stated ground of entry (21:27), and the right (exousia) to the tree of life belongs to "those who wash their robes" (22:14, on the critical reading; section 6). The consummated state is populated by gift and cleansing, not by attainment; Part VIII must not let the reign of 22:5 be misread as the reward-structure of an achievement. [Explicit text, HIGH, subject to the textual note on 22:14]
8. **The curse's removal is universal in scope and grounded in the throne.** Pan katathema with the absolute negation (22:3) leaves no cursed residue anywhere in the new order, and the immediately following clause locates the throne of God and of the Lamb in the city: the text's own logic ties the curse's absence to the presence, not to the mere passage of time or the exhaustion of evil. [Explicit text for the scope, HIGH; Necessary inference for the grounding, MEDIUM-HIGH]

## 6. Textual notes

**The tradition's limits, stated first.** Revelation's Greek tradition is the thinnest of any New Testament book of its size: some 300 witnesses in total (J. K. Elliott counted 303 manuscripts containing Revelation, citing Aland's Liste; Aune's count is six papyri, eleven uncials, and 292 minuscules), against thousands for the Gospels; the book was never in the Greek lectionary; and its text-types do not map cleanly onto the categories used elsewhere in the New Testament. Critically for this unit: no papyrus reaches chapters 21-22 (P47, the oldest substantial witness, covers 9:10 to 17:2 only). For these two chapters the earliest continuous witnesses are Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Alexandrinus (fourth and fifth centuries), with Codex Ephraemi fragmentary. The study's findings therefore rest, for Revelation 21-22, on the uncial tradition and the versions, at one more remove than the Gospels' text. That limit is real, and it is also bounded: within the witnesses consulted, no variant in 21:1-5, 21:27, or 22:1-5 alters any finding above. The variants that do exist in and around the load-bearing verses are these.

**21:3, laoi or laos ("peoples" or "people").** The critical text prints the plural laoi autou, "they shall be his peoples," which the discourse source consulted calls well attested; a singular reading laos ("his people") also circulates in the tradition. The plural universalizes the covenant formula to the redeemed nations of 21:24 and 22:2; the singular would reproduce the Old Testament formula unexpanded. No finding turns on the choice; finding 2's boundary argument and finding 6's vocation argument stand on either reading.

**21:4, the explicit subject.** Some witnesses make the subject of "will wipe away" explicit, ho theos (so the tradition behind the KJV's "And God shall wipe away all tears"); the critical text leaves the subject carried over from the voice of 21:3. The referent is identical; nothing turns on it.

**21:5, the self-attestation.** "Houtoi hoi logoi pistoi kai alethinoi eisin," "these words are faithful and true," is spoken by the enthroned One himself and sealed with a command to write (grapson). The formula recurs at 22:6 on the angel's lips, and the book's closing attestation (22:18-20) builds on it. The wording in the witnesses consulted is stable. The study notes the attestation's function rather than overclaiming for it: it is the text's own warrant claim for the vision, and Part VIII may cite it as such, as the passage's internal testimony to its own reliability, without treating a self-attestation as external corroboration.

**22:14, "those who wash their robes" or "those who do his commandments."** The critical text reads hoi plynontes tas stolas auton, "those who wash their robes," attested by Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, the Vulgate, and other early witnesses (so William Kelly's collation notes, and Swete, who judged the documentary evidence decidedly in favor of plynontes while granting the graphic similarity of PLYNONTES TAS STOLAS and POIOUNTES TAS ENTOLAS could generate confusion in copying). The Textus Receptus and the Byzantine majority read hoi poiountes tas entolas autou, "those who do his commandments" (hence KJV and NKJV). This study follows the critical reading, which also coheres with the book's own washed-robes figure at 7:14. Finding 7 is stated on that reading; were the TR reading original, finding 7's gift framing would need restating, though the inscription ground of 21:27 would still stand.

**22:19, "tree of life" or "book of life."** Every Greek manuscript reads apo tou xylou tes zoes, "from the tree of life." The "book of life" of the Textus Receptus and the KJV at this verse entered through Erasmus, whose single Revelation manuscript (minuscule 2814) lacked its final leaf; he supplied the closing verses by translating back from the Latin Vulgate, whose libro ("book") he rendered as biblou. Modern versions restore the tree. The point matters beyond correctness: the erroneous reading severs the canon arc at its last joint, replacing the restored tree of Genesis 2-3 with a second book. On the Greek tradition, the book's final warning guards the tree, and the arc of section 2 is unbroken.

**21:27, the participle forms.** The tradition behind the Textus Receptus reads participles (pan koinoun, "anything that defiles"; kai poioun, "and that does") where the critical text reads the adjective koinon and the articular participle ho poion. The sense is unchanged; the critical text's adjective is what grounds section 3.4's cultic reading.
