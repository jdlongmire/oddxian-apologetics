## 1. Text and translation

The critical text (Nestle 1904 and the Textus Receptus agree here, per the OpenBible display) reads, in transliteration: "Oidamen de hoti tois agaposin ton theon panta synergei eis agathon, tois kata prothesin kletois ousin." Literally: "And we know that, for those who love God, panta synergei for good, for those who are called according to purpose." Everything in this study turns on the two words at the center: panta ("all things," a neuter plural whose nominative and accusative forms are identical) and synergei ("works together," present active indicative, third person singular). Verse 29: "because those whom he foreknew (hous proegno) he also predestined (proorisen) to be conformed to the image of his Son (symmorphous tes eikonos tou huiou autou), so that he might be the firstborn among many brothers." Verse 30: "And those he predestined, them he also called (ekalesen); and those he called, he also justified (edikaiosen); and those he justified, he also glorified (edoxasen)."

A substantial minority of witnesses inserts ho theos ("God") after synergei in verse 28, making God the explicit subject and panta the object: "God works all things together for good." Section 6 gives the attestation and the verdict.

Translation comparison for verse 28 (subject chosen / footnote practice):

| Version | Rendering of the clause | Subject | Footnote |
|---|---|---|---|
| KJV | all things work together for good to them that love God | panta | none in standard editions |
| ESV | for those who love God all things work together for good | panta | "Some manuscripts God works all things together for good, or God works in all things for the good" |
| NASB (1995) | God causes all things to work together for good to those who love God | God (supplied, causative) | marker on "God causes"; the note as displayed reads "One early ms reads all things work together for good" |
| NIV | in all things God works for the good of those who love him | God; panta as sphere ("in all things") | "Or that all things work together for good to those who love God, who...; or that in all things God works together with those who love him to bring about what is good..." |

The versions divide exactly where the Greek divides: ESV, NET, and CSB follow the shorter text with panta as subject and footnote the God-subject alternative; NASB and NIV print God as subject (NASB by supplying and causativizing, NIV by construing panta as the sphere "in all things") and footnote the panta-subject alternative. The KJV, translated from the Textus Receptus, which also lacks ho theos, takes panta as subject without a note. No major version hides the alternative; the disagreement is over which construal to print, not over whether the other exists.

## 2. Context

Verse 28 stands at the hinge of Romans 8:17-30. Before it: co-heirs who suffer with Christ in order to be glorified with him (8:17); the sufferings of the present time weighed against coming glory (8:18); creation subjected to futility and groaning in labor pains (8:20-22); believers themselves groaning as they wait in hope for the redemption of their bodies (8:23-25); and the Spirit helping weakness, interceding with groanings too deep for words, while "he who searches hearts" knows the Spirit's mind, because the Spirit intercedes for the saints according to God (8:26-27). Three contextual controls follow.

First, the verse opens with oidamen, "we know." Paul does not argue the premise; he states it as knowledge the community already holds, in a passage otherwise full of groaning and not-yet. The assurance is a premise of the paragraph, not its conclusion. Second, the "all things" of 8:28 is spoken into that frame: its nearest contextual content is the sufferings, futility, and weakness of 8:17-27, not pleasant circumstances. Third, the good (eis agathon) is not defined by anything in 8:28 itself or by temporal outcomes anywhere in the chapter; it is defined forward, in 8:29-30, as conformity to the image of the Son and glorification. Any reading that cashes "good" as present comfort has already left the text. [Explicit text, HIGH]

## 3. Grammar and word studies

### 3.1 Synergeo (Strong's G4903)

Thayer's lexicon (via Blue Letter Bible): "to work together, help in work, be a partner in labour" (1 Corinthians 16:16; 2 Corinthians 6:1); "to put forth power together with and thereby to assist" (Mark 16:20, the Lord working with the apostles); with a dative and eis, "to assist, help one for a thing," which is Thayer's classification of Romans 8:28 itself. The verb occurs five times in the New Testament, and its sense is cooperative in every other occurrence: its subject is personal in three of them (the Lord working with his messengers, Mark 16:20; fellow workers joining the labor, 1 Corinthians 16:16; "we work together with him," 2 Corinthians 6:1), and in the fourth its subject is faith (James 2:22, faith working with Abraham's works), which is not a personal agent but carries the same cooperative sense. Thayer further records, for the longer reading panta synergei ho theos, Fritzsche's analysis of the construction as a breviloquence: God, co-working, provides all things for his people's good (the Revised Version margin: "God worketh all things with them for good"). Two lexical facts matter for the syntax question: the verb's attested use is cooperative, with personal agents as subjects in most occurrences, and the verb is not morphologically causative, so the NASB's "causes" is an interpretive strengthening, not a translation of a causative form. [Explicit text / lexical, HIGH for the occurrence profile; the weight given the profile in section 3.2 is a judgment]

### 3.2 Panta and the subject of synergei: the four construals

Because panta's nominative and accusative forms are identical, and because a Greek finite verb can carry an unexpressed ("embedded") subject, the shorter text supports more than one construal. The longer text (with ho theos) supports essentially one. The options, in the order the study will assess them:

- **(A) Panta as subject, verb intransitive.** "All things work together for good for those who love God." A neuter plural subject with a singular verb is regular Greek, so the agreement of singular synergei with plural panta is no obstacle. On this construal the verse asserts the convergence of all things toward good for the beneficiaries without grammatically naming an agent.
- **(B) Embedded subject "he," that is, God; panta as object, verb transitive.** "He (God) works all things together for good." The NET note explicitly allows this on the shorter text: "the subject is embedded in the verb and 'God' is clearly implied (as in v. 29)." Robertson's grammar, quoted in secondary discussion, makes the same point from the other side: if ho theos is read, synergei is transitive.
- **(C) Embedded subject, the Spirit; panta as object.** "He (the Spirit) works all things together for good." Held, as reported by Berding's survey, by a notable line of interpreters (James P. Wilson, Matthew Black, F. F. Bruce, John A. T. Robinson, Gordon Fee, Robert Jewett), on the ground that the Spirit is the operative subject of 8:26-27; the NEB rendered the verse this way ("he cooperates for good with those who love God," as reported in Mambu's study).
- **(D) God as subject, with the dative read as association.** "God works together with those who love him, in all things, for good," taking tois agaposin as a dative of association (the syn- prefix inviting a partner) and panta as an accusative of reference ("in all things"). This is the construal behind the NIV footnote's second alternative and N. T. Wright's published rendering.

Assessment. The dative tois agaposin ton theon is fronted, before panta, and is most naturally a dative of advantage on any construal: the working is *for* the ones loving God. Construal D's association reading is grammatically possible but asks the one dative to do double duty, and sits awkwardly beside the second dative phrase (section 3.3), which redescribes the same people as called: the verse's interest is in what converges for them, not in their co-labor. Construal C founders on verse 29: the subject of proegno and proorisen in 29-30 must be God (the Son whose image is in view is "his Son"; the Spirit is nowhere said to foreknow persons to conformity to his own Son), so C requires a silent subject change between verses 28 and 29 in a tightly chained argument. Between A and B the grammar alone does not decide; the lexical profile of synergeo (cooperative sense throughout, personal subjects in most occurrences) and the unbroken divine agency of verses 27 and 29-30 tilt the sense toward a personal worker, while the word order, with panta immediately before the verb, tilts the surface grammar toward A. The study's resolution is the one the translations collectively embody: print A or B, footnote the other, and do not build doctrine on the choice, for the reason given in finding 5.

### 3.3 The two datives and prothesis (Strong's G4286)

The beneficiaries are described twice, in two dative phrases that refer to the same people: tois agaposin ton theon, "those who love God" (present participle: the human side, an ongoing posture), and tois kata prothesin kletois ousin, "those who are called according to purpose" (the divine side: their being-called is a settled state grounded in a purpose that precedes it). The second phrase is in apposition to the first; the text itself glosses who the God-lovers are. Prothesis, from protithemi, "to set before," means a setting forth, and figuratively a purpose or plan (Thayer, via BibleHub: sense 2, "a purpose," citing for divine purpose Romans 8:28; 9:11; Ephesians 1:11; 3:11; 2 Timothy 1:9; the word also serves human resolve at Acts 11:23; 27:13; 2 Timothy 3:10, and the showbread in its literal cultic use). Whose purpose is not stated in 8:28, but Paul's usage settles it: Romans 9:11, "the purpose of God according to election" (he kata eklogen prothesis tou theou); Ephesians 1:11, predestined "according to the purpose of him who works all things according to the counsel of his will" (kata prothesin tou ta panta energountos); Ephesians 3:11, "the eternal purpose" realized in Christ; 2 Timothy 1:9, salvation "according to his own purpose and grace" given "before the ages." In 8:28 the purpose is God's, and Ephesians 1:11 supplies, in so many words, the universal divine working (ta panta, with the cognate verb energeo) that Romans 8:28's syntax leaves distributed between its construals. [Explicit text, HIGH]

### 3.4 The chain of 8:29-30

Five verbs, all aorist active indicatives, all with God as their undisputed subject, the text of 29-30 being stable: proegno (he foreknew), proorisen (he predestined), ekalesen (he called), edikaiosen (he justified), edoxasen (he glorified). Three observations. First, the objects throughout are persons (hous, "whom"), not facts about persons: what God foreknows in verse 29 is people, which is why the treatise, with the mainstream Reformed reading, takes proginosko here as fore-appointment of persons rather than bare prescience of their choices; Romans 11:2 uses the same verb the same way ("his people, whom he foreknew"). Second, the goal of predestination is stated before any of the later links: symmorphous tes eikonos tou huiou autou, "conformed to the image of his Son," with the purpose that the Son be prototokos, firstborn among many brothers. Conformity is not a by-product of the chain; it is its stated aim, and it is what "good" in verse 28 means. Third, edoxasen is proleptic: glorification is still future in this very chapter (8:17-18, 21, 23-25, where it is hoped for, not possessed), yet Paul states it in the same aorist series as justification. The chain presents the whole sequence, from foreknowledge to glory, as one unbroken divine act viewed from the side of the decree; no link is conditioned on anything in the beneficiary that the text names. [Explicit text, HIGH for the morphology, the personal objects, and the stated goal; Theological synthesis, MEDIUM for the proleptic-certainty gloss, which is the standard reading but is a reading]

## 4. Findings

1. **The beneficiaries are explicit and doubly described.** The promise is for those who love God, whom the same verse identifies as those called according to God's purpose. It is not addressed to humanity generally, and the text supplies no warrant for universalizing its outcome. [Explicit text, HIGH: as WP 6.15]
2. **Panta is comprehensive in the discourse.** "All things" in a paragraph whose content is suffering, futility, groaning, and weakness asserts a working that includes the evils experienced, not only favorable circumstances; nothing in the verse restricts panta to a subclass. Its nearest contextual anchor is the afflictions of 8:17-27, and its discourse force is total. [Explicit text, HIGH for comprehensiveness as discourse force; the step from discourse totality to a metaphysical "no event lies outside" is tagged in finding 6]
3. **The good is defined forward and Christologically.** Eis agathon in 8:28 is specified by 8:29-30 as conformity to the Son and glorification, not by temporal relief, and the proleptic edoxasen states the end as certain. [Explicit text, HIGH: as WP 6.11 and 6.15]
4. **The shorter reading of 8:28, without ho theos, is most probably original** (section 6). The study therefore does not quote "God works all things" as Paul's wording; it is a well-attested ancient clarification, probably secondary. [Textual verdict, MEDIUM-HIGH: strong consensus of the consulted evidence and argument, resting partly on mediated sources; see sections 6 and 8]
5. **The treatise's governing premise RP does not depend on the variant.** On the longer reading, divine agency is explicit in the verse. On the shorter reading under construal B, it is embedded. On the shorter reading under construal A, this verse asserts the convergence without naming the agent, and RP's agency component is then carried, as WP 6.11 and 6.15 already carry it, by the immediate context, where God is the subject of every verb in 8:29-30 and of the searching and intercession frame in 8:27, by kata prothesin (God's purpose, section 3.3), and by corroborating texts, above all Ephesians 1:11 (God works ta panta) and Genesis 50:20 (unit 03). What the variant changes is the grammatical location of the agency claim, not the beneficiaries, the comprehensiveness, or the telos, which are identical on every attested reading. [Necessary inference, HIGH]
6. **The verse does not teach event-by-event indispensability.** On no reading does eis agathon assert that each particular evil was necessary as an instrument, or that each event is itself good; the working is toward an end, and the moral character of the events is untouched by it (WP 6.11, distinctions 4 and 5). RP must therefore not be quoted as if 8:28 alone proved that no suffering is gratuitous in the strong universal sense; that synthesis remains, as WP 6.15 judged, MEDIUM, and depends on the wider theodicy premises (WP T1-T3), not on this unit. [Explicit text for the negative point about what the verse says, HIGH; Theological synthesis, MEDIUM for the universal no-gratuitousness claim, unchanged from WP 6.15]
7. **The chain of 8:29-30 grounds the assurance in the decree, not in the believer's performance.** Foreknowledge of persons, predestination to conformity, calling, justification, glorification: one subject, one direction, no named human condition. For Part VIII this fixes the consummation's security: glorification is a link in God's act, stated in the aorist, not a contingency. [Theological synthesis, HIGH for the direction of the argument in the text; the decree framing is Paul's own sequence read at face value]

## 5. Competing readings

1. **The scope-restricted reading: panta means only the sufferings and circumstances in view, and the verse is a pastoral word about those, not a universal providence thesis.** Strongest form: oidamen introduces a confidence for the groaning community about its own afflictions; eis agathon is exhausted by 8:29-30's outcome for the called; nothing here licenses a claim about all events whatsoever or about persons outside the call. Response: the beneficiary restriction is explicit and this study affirms it (finding 1); the contextual anchoring of panta in present afflictions is also right as far as it goes. But the restriction proves less than its advocates need: a working that covers all the afflictions of the called, governed by God's prothesis and terminating in glorification, is already the substance of RP. What the restricted reading rightly blocks is only the further step to a universal metaphysical thesis, which finding 6 already declines to take from this verse alone. Partially accepted.
2. **The impersonal reading: panta is subject, the verb is intransitive, and the verse makes no divine-agent claim at all; things simply converge, as a kind of sacred optimism.** This is construal A pressed into a theology. As grammar it is available; as a reading of Paul it is not: the verse sits between the Spirit's intercession kata theon (8:27) and God's foreknowing and predestining (8:29), is governed by kata prothesin, and is introduced as something "we know" within a letter whose God acts in every line. Pressed consistently, the impersonal reading must treat the paragraph's pervasive divine agency as decoration. Declined as exegesis, while its grammatical core (construal A) is retained as a live translation option, with agency supplied contextually, exactly as the NET note's Cranfield quotation puts it: on either translation, what is expressed is confidence in the sovereignty of God.
3. **The Spirit-as-subject reading (construal C).** Serious and held by serious names (section 3.2), and it honors the paragraph's pneumatology. Not adopted: the subject chain of 8:29-30 cannot be the Spirit (the image is of "his Son," and foreknowing persons unto sonship is the Father's act throughout Romans 8; compare 8:14-17, where the Spirit leads and witnesses but the inheritance is from God), so C buys local continuity with 8:26-27 at the price of a silent subject change at the argument's most solemn moment. Recorded, not adopted.
4. **The cooperative reading: God works together *with* those who love him (construal D), so the verse teaches divine-human synergy rather than sovereign ordering.** In its strongest form (Wright's rendering) it takes the syn- of synergeo with full seriousness and the dative as association. Response: the lexical base is real (section 3.1; the verb does denote co-working), and nothing in RP forbids God working with his people as secondary agents. But the dative's position and the parallel second dative favor advantage over association here, and the chain of 29-30, which this reading must also carry, contains no human co-agent at all. The verse will not bear a synergy thesis that dilutes the decree; at most D adds a texture (God's working is not aloof from his people's agency) that Part II may note without building on. Partially accepted, as texture only.
5. **The popular prosperity reading: God works everything out pleasantly, for everyone, in this life.** Declined without remainder: beneficiaries restricted (finding 1), good defined eschatologically (finding 3), context one of groaning (section 2). Recorded because the verse's popular use makes the refusal necessary in a treatise that will quote it often.

## 6. Textual notes: the apparatus for 8:28

**The readings.** (i) The shorter reading: panta synergei eis agathon, with no expressed subject. (ii) The longer reading: panta synergei ho theos eis agathon, ho theos standing after the verb. (iii) A fronted placement, ho theos before panta ("God works all things together"), circulates in later handling of the verse and in teaching materials; the apparatus summaries actually consulted present the variant strictly as the insertion recorded under (ii) and give no separate witness list for a fronted placement, so this study records form (iii) at LOW confidence and rests nothing on it (see section 8).

**Attestation, as reported in the NET textual note.** The longer reading (ii) is read by P46, A (Alexandrinus), B (Vaticanus), minuscule 81, and the Sahidic Coptic (sa). The shorter reading (i) is read by Sinaiticus (Aleph), C, D, F, G, Psi, 33, 1739, 1881, the Byzantine majority (M), the Latin tradition (latt), the Syriac (sy), and the Bohairic Coptic (bo). Metzger's fuller list, in the verbatim quotation reproduced in section 7's sources, agrees and adds detail: for the longer reading, also the Ethiopic and Origen (in two of five Greek citations); for the shorter, additionally K, P, 614, the Byzantine lectionaries, the Old Latin and Vulgate, the Peshitta and Harklean Syriac, the Armenian, and Clement, Eusebius, Lucifer, Cyril of Jerusalem, Chrysostom, and Augustine, with Origen's remaining citations also on the shorter side. The NET note as consulted gives Sinaiticus plainly for the shorter reading, without distinguishing hands or corrections; hand-specific detail for Aleph is an open item (section 8), and this study asserts nothing about it.

**External evidence.** For the longer reading: great antiquity (P46, about AD 200) and the two premier Alexandrian codices A and B, a cluster any critic must respect. For the shorter: breadth across text-types and traditions, Alexandrian (Aleph, C, 33, 1739) and Western (D, F, G, the Latin tradition) together, the Byzantine majority, both Syriac forms, both Coptic dialects divided between the readings, and the earlier and wider patristic citation. Origen, the earliest father in view, cites both forms, predominantly the shorter: the longer form existed very early, and was not the only form an Alexandrian scholar of the third century knew. On the standard weighing, diversity of attestation across independent traditions counts for more than a concentrated early cluster, because a concentrated cluster can descend from a single edited ancestor, which is exactly what the internal evidence next suggests.

**Internal evidence.** Transcriptionally, the addition explains itself and the omission does not. Synergei may be taken to imply a personal subject; a scribe or editor who felt the clause's agent go unnamed had a ready clarification at hand, and the NET note and Metzger give the same diagnosis in nearly the same words: the longer reading is "evidently motivated by a need for clarification" (NET); ho theos "seems to have been a natural explanatory addition made by an Alexandrian editor" (Metzger, the Committee's reasoning, the cited text rated B). No comparable motive explains why scribes across every tradition would delete an explicit "God" and leave a harder, ambiguous clause. Wallace's grammar makes the same point from scribal tendency: explicitness is the direction of travel (quoted in secondary discussion). Intrinsically, the shorter, harder clause is characteristic of the paragraph's compression, and verse 29's unexpressed subject ("those whom *he* foreknew," with God carried over) shows Paul content to leave the divine subject embedded in this very argument.

**Verdict.** The shorter reading is original; ho theos is an ancient, well-attested, and theologically true clarification, but a clarification. Confidence MEDIUM-HIGH: the external and internal arguments converge and represent the critical consensus (NA28 and UBS print the shorter text), and the residual doubt is the honest one, that P46, A, and B are not witnesses to be waved aside. The study's load-bearing conclusion does not need more than this verdict gives, because finding 5 holds on either reading.
