## 1. Text and translation

Hebrew (transliterated): *hannistarot laYHWH Eloheinu, wehanniglot lanu ulevaneinu ad-olam, laasot et-kol-divrei hattorah hazzot.* Literally: "The hidden things [belong] to the LORD our God, and the revealed things to us and to our children forever, in order to do all the words of this law." The verse is Deuteronomy 29:29 in English numbering and 29:28 in the Hebrew text; from 29:1 to 29:29 the English and Hebrew verse numbers differ by one (NET textual note on the chapter).

Translation comparison:

| Version | First clause | Second clause | Final clause |
|---|---|---|---|
| KJV | The secret things belong unto the LORD our God | those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever | that we may do all the words of this law |
| ESV | The secret things belong to the LORD our God | the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever | that we may do all the words of this law |
| NASB | The secret things belong to the LORD our God | the things revealed belong to us and to our sons forever | so that we may follow all the words of this Law |
| NIV | The secret things belong to the LORD our God | the things revealed belong to us and to our children forever | that we may do all the words of this law |
| NET | Secret things belong to the Lord our God | those that are revealed belong to us and our descendants forever | so that we might obey all the words of this law |

The five versions are structurally unanimous: antithesis, belonging, purpose clause. The only variations are lexical (NASB "sons" for *beneinu*, where the NET note observes the Hebrew is "sons" and KJV, NIV, and NRSV read "children"; NET "descendants"; NASB "follow" against "do" elsewhere). No translation decides the interpretive question of this study, which is not how to render the verse but what the two domains contain. Translation is not where the dispute lives; context is.

## 2. Context

The verse closes the Moab covenant sermon and stands at the end of the curse material of chapters 28-29. Its context supplies both domains with concrete referents, and any reading that ignores the sequence will misjudge the verse.

- The assembly of 29:10-15 enters the covenant "and with whoever is not here with us today": the covenant already binds the unborn, which is the temporal ground of "to us and to our children forever" in verse 29.
- Verse 18 warns against "a root bearing poisonous and bitter fruit": a man, woman, clan, or tribe whose heart turns away in secret from the LORD to serve other gods.
- Verse 19 describes that person hearing the curses and blessing himself in his heart: "I shall be safe, though I walk in the stubbornness of my heart." The sin in view is interior and concealed; the community standing around him cannot see it.
- Verses 20-21 assign the response to God alone: the LORD will not forgive him; every curse written in this book settles on him; the LORD blots out his name and sets him apart for calamity. The hidden sinner is judged by revealed terms (the curses "written in this book") at God's sole initiative.
- Verses 22-28 project the future: a later generation and foreigners see the devastated land and ask why the LORD has done this and why his anger burned. The answer given is covenantal (they abandoned the covenant, served other gods, verses 25-26), and it ends in exile, "as it is this day" (verse 28). What the passage does not give the questioners is timing, particulars, or the inner rationale of God's dealings; the future is hidden except at the level of covenant cause.

Two observations control the exegesis. First, the nearest hidden thing in the chapter is a hidden sin: apostasy nursed in the heart while the body stands in the assembly. Second, the nearest future thing in the chapter is a hidden divine action: the exile whose cause is revealed and whose course is not. The verse that follows must be read with both already on the table.

## 3. Word studies

### 3.1 Satar; nistar (Strong's H5641)

*Satar*: to hide, conceal. Stems and field (BDB, consulted through the reproductions named in section 7): Qal, hide or conceal; Niphal, be hidden, hide oneself; Hiphil, hide, especially God's hiding of his face in covenant judgment (Deuteronomy 31:17-18, "I will hide my face from them"; Isaiah 54:8); Hithpael, hide oneself (Isaiah 45:15, God "who hides himself"). The cognate noun *seter* (H5643) is a covering, hiding place, or secrecy: a place of concealment for ambush (Psalm 10:9), and, in the other direction, God's own shelter for his people (Psalm 32:7; 119:114, "you are my hiding place and my shield").

In Deuteronomy 29:29 the form is *hannistarot*: the definite article with the feminine plural Niphal participle, substantivized, "the hidden things." The feminine plural in *-ot* is the standard form for abstract collectives, and the participle denotes things in a state of hiddenness. The decisive grammatical fact is what the clause does not contain: no object, genitive, or qualifier specifies hidden *what*, hidden *from whom*, or hidden *in what respect*. The same participle elsewhere does denote hidden sins: Psalm 19:12 (13 in Hebrew numbering) prays to be declared innocent *min-nistarot*, "from hidden faults," faults hidden even from the one who committed them.

### 3.2 Galah; niglot (Strong's H1540)

*Galah* (189 occurrences, BDB): to uncover, remove. Qal senses include uncovering (nakedness, Leviticus 18), the idiom "uncover the ear" of a person, that is, reveal a matter to him (1 Samuel 9:15; 2 Samuel 7:27), revealing a secret (Amos 3:7, God reveals his counsel, *sod*, to his servants the prophets; Proverbs 20:19), and, by the stripping of captives, going into exile. Niphal senses (BDB): be uncovered; be revealed; reveal oneself, said of God (Genesis 35:7; 1 Samuel 2:27; 3:21, "the LORD revealed himself to Samuel"). The substantivized participle "the things revealed" is, in BDB's distribution, this verse's own usage.

In Deuteronomy 29:29 the form is *hanniglot*, article with feminine plural Niphal participle, exactly parallel to *hannistarot*. The pair is a formal antithesis between two domains of things, and within Deuteronomy the revealed domain has a concrete anchor: the words of "this law" named in the verse's own purpose clause, the same written curses by which verse 20 says the hidden sinner will be judged.

### 3.3 The construction

Both domains are assigned by the lamed of possession: *laYHWH Eloheinu*, "to the LORD our God"; *lanu ulevaneinu*, "to us and to our children." The entrustment is *ad-olam*, forever. The verse ends with a purpose clause, *laasot et-kol-divrei hattorah hazzot*, "in order to do all the words of this law." The antithesis is therefore between domains of belonging, and the telos of the whole sentence is practice: the revealed things are given for doing, and the hidden things are, by the same sentence, excluded from the doing mandate. The verse regulates the use of knowledge, not only its possession. [Grammatical, HIGH]

## 4. Findings

1. **The verse draws a real and permanent boundary of belonging.** A domain of things is reserved to the LORD; a domain is entrusted to Israel and its descendants forever; the entrusted domain is given for obedience. This is the whole of what 6.9's first claim asserts, and the text sustains it without remainder. [Explicit text, HIGH]
2. **The verse does not specify the content of either domain.** Neither participle carries an object. Every specification of the hidden things (hidden sins, unrevealed counsel, the future, the reasons for particular judgments) is supplied from the immediate context or the wider canon, and each such specification must be argued, not assumed from the wording. Any use of the verse that skips that argument overstates the text. [Explicit text, grammatical, HIGH]
3. **In the immediate context, the primary referents are hidden covenant treachery and its judgment on the hidden side, and the law on the revealed side.** Verses 18-21 put a sinner whose apostasy is concealed in his heart before the reader, and assign his detection and judgment to God alone; the revealed side is named by the purpose clause itself, "all the words of this law." This is the reading Rashi gives the verse (it answers the fear that the many will be punished for the secret sin of the one, which no one could have known), and Psalm 19:12 shows the participle carrying exactly this sense. 6.9's second claim, that in context the verse concerns covenant accountability and limits of access, is sustained; the accountability referent is more specific than 6.9 states, and this study corrects the register entry accordingly. [Necessary inference from context, HIGH that these referents are in view; MEDIUM that they exhaust the verse's sense]
4. **The reserved domain also takes in God's unrevealed future dealings, by the chapter's own construction.** Verses 22-28 stage a question about what God will do to the land and answer it only at the level of covenant cause; when, whether, and in what particulars remain unrevealed. The verse's formulation is deliberately abstract ("the hidden things," full stop), and nothing in it restricts the reserved domain to sins. The extension from the judicial referent to unrevealed counsel and future is therefore not imported from later dogmatics; it begins inside the chapter. It remains an extension beyond the primary referent, and finding 2 governs how it may be cited. [Theological synthesis from context, MEDIUM-HIGH]
5. **Assessment of the working paper's use (6.9).** Claims one and two are sustained (findings 1 and 3). Claim three, that the application to providence and the problem of evil is a theological extension rather than a direct answer, is confirmed and sharpened: the extension is legitimate because the reserved domain is defined by belonging rather than by content (finding 2) and because the chapter itself extends it to God's future acts (finding 4); but Deuteronomy 29:29 does not address any particular evil, and Part V must not cite it as though it did. Claims four and five are assessed in findings 6 and 7. [Theological synthesis, HIGH as an assessment of the relation between the text and its use in 6.9]
6. **The epistemic rule of 6.9 is a valid consequence of the verse's structure, not a statement of the verse.** The verse establishes that a real domain of things bearing on God's governance is legitimately withheld from us. Given that category, the inference from "no morally sufficient reason for this evil is visible to us" to "there is no such reason" fails on the verse's own terms; and by the same silence about content (finding 2), the verse supplies no positive evidence about any particular case, so the appeal cannot independently demonstrate that a reason exists in that case. Stated with the decree texts (the treatise's Parts II and V supply them), the rule is sound; stated from this verse alone, its first half is supported and its second half is a limit the verse imposes by silence. 6.9 states the rule accurately and does not overclaim; this study records the exact division of labor between the verse and the wider argument. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM]
7. **The pastoral guardrail of 6.9 is sustained for action, and is not sourced from this verse for lament.** For action: the boundary assigns responsibility rather than removing it, since the revealed domain is entrusted precisely "in order to do," and the chapter's own order requires Israel to purge revealed evil from its midst (Deuteronomy 13; 17:2-7; Rashi on this verse: the revealed things are ours "to destroy the evil from our midst"). An appeal to the hidden things that excused known injustice would contradict the verse's purpose clause. For lament: Deuteronomy 29:29 says nothing about lament either way; 6.9's rule that the appeal must never silence lament rests on the wider canon (the Psalms of lament, Job) and Part V must carry it on those texts, not on this one. [Necessary inference, HIGH for the action half; Theological synthesis, MEDIUM for the lament half, on grounds outside the verse]
8. **The scribal dots over the second clause are early evidence that the contextual reading is ancient.** The rabbinic discussion of the dots (section 6.2) presupposes throughout that the verse allocates responsibility for hidden sins; whatever the dots originally meant, the tradition that pondered them read the verse as this study's finding 3 does. [Reception, MEDIUM]

## 5. Competing readings

1. **The contextual-judicial reading, in strongest form (Rashi; in modern dress, Heiser).** The hidden things are secret transgressions of the law: sins no human court can see, which God himself will judge; the revealed things are the law and its open transgressions, which are Israel's charge to enforce. On this reading the verse is a jurisdictional rule answering the assembly's fear of collective punishment for unknowable sins, and it coheres with the dots tradition, with Psalm 19:12, and with the fact that the chapter's hidden sinner stands at verses 18-21. This study accepts it as the primary contextual sense (finding 3). In its exclusive form, however, it under-reads verses 22-28, where what is hidden is not a human sin but God's own future action, and it must narrow a formulation the text left abstract. Calvin's objection, that the verse's subject is the use of the Law's doctrine rather than the allocation of punishment, identifies a real difference of emphasis but not a contradiction: the jurisdictional rule is one application of the boundary the verse states, and the boundary outruns it.
2. **The counsel reading, in strongest form (Calvin).** By antithesis, the verse compares the doctrine openly set forth in the Law with the hidden and incomprehensible counsel of God, "concerning which it is not lawful to inquire"; God retains secret things "which it neither concerns nor profits us to know," and the verse condemns audacious curiosity while arousing zeal for revealed instruction. Calvin supports the antithesis grammatically (the article on both participles and the adversative force of the copula) and rejects the judicial reading as forced. This study accepts the counsel reading as the verse's theological trajectory and as a strand genuinely present in the chapter (finding 4), but declines Calvin's dismissal of the judicial reading: the hidden sinner of verse 19 is too near, and the dots tradition too early, for the allocation of judgment to be an imposition on the text. The two readings are ordered, not rivals: judicial first in context, counsel by the verse's own abstraction and the chapter's future horizon.
3. **The future-only reading (Barnes and others).** The secret things are the future: when and how the blessings and curses will take effect is God's to determine; man's sphere is the revealed will. This is a true subset of reading 2, well fitted to verses 22-28, and too narrow for verses 18-21. Retained as a component, declined as the whole.
4. **The Vulgate's connected reading.** The Latin tradition could be read as "the secret things which belong to the Lord our God are revealed to us and to our children," collapsing the antithesis. Calvin's grammatical objection stands: the article adjoined to both participles and the copula between them do not permit the clauses to be read connectedly. [Grammatical, HIGH] Declined.
5. **The restrictive use: the verse as a general ban on pressing theological questions, or as a shield for any particular doctrine or any particular evil.** Declined. The verse's purpose clause commands the vigorous use of the revealed, and its own greatest expositor pairs its restraint with zeal for inquiry into what is revealed. A text that assigns domains requires the questioner to ask which domain a question belongs to; it does not forbid the asking. In the treatise's own deployment (Part V), citing this verse to end discussion of a particular evil, rather than to locate the epistemic boundary honestly, would invert the verse: it sends the reader back to the revealed work, not into silence.

## 6. Textual notes

### 6.1 The Masoretic text and numbering

English 29:29 is Hebrew 29:28; the chapter's numbering offset is noted by the NET. No significant variant in the apparatus consulted for this study affects either participle or the purpose clause. The LXX and Targum renderings were not consulted in this session and are carried as an open item (section 8); no claim in this study rests on them.

### 6.2 The puncta extraordinaria

In the Masoretic tradition, dots (puncta extraordinaria) stand over eleven letters of this verse: over each letter of *lanu ulevaneinu*, "to us and to our children," and over the ayin of *ad* in *ad-olam*, "forever." This is one of ten dotted passages in the Torah (fifteen in the Tanakh as a whole). Two families of explanation are standard:

- **Scribal.** In ancient scribal practice, dots over letters function like erasure marks, signaling that the dotted letters were doubted or marked for deletion by an earlier copyist. On this account the dots record textual hesitation, later reverently preserved rather than acted on. Modern textual scholars have called this verse's dots "cryptic" (Tov) and "extremely enigmatic" (Talmon), as relayed in the secondary summary cited in section 7; a recent proposal reads them as a proto-Masoretic polemic against claims of secret laws (the Qumran community's *nistarot*), which this study notes without adopting.
- **Rabbinic.** The Talmud (b. Sanhedrin 43b) asks why the dots stand where they do, taking them to weaken the force of the dotted words. Rabbi Yehuda: God did not punish the nation for hidden sins until Israel crossed the Jordan. Rabbi Nehemiah answers from the verse itself: one is never punished for the hidden things (they belong to the LORD, "forever"); rather, just as with the hidden, so also with the revealed, collective punishment began only after the crossing, when Israel accepted mutual responsibility. Rashi adopts the Jordan line for the hidden things and ties it to the oaths at Gerizim and Ebal, where Israel became guarantors for one another (compare b. Shevuot 39a on mutual responsibility).

What the dots are usually taken to signal, across both families, is qualification rather than deletion: the dotted words stand in the text, but stand under a mark. For this study the load-bearing point is narrower and is recorded as finding 8: every ancient discussion of the dots presupposes the verse's accountability context. [Textual and reception, MEDIUM: the placement and the Sanhedrin dispute are well attested; the original purpose of the dots is unresolved in the scholarship]

### 6.3 Reception pointer (Phase 2 material; not developed here)

Calvin's commentary on this verse (section 5, reading 2) made it the Reformed tradition's charter text for the distinction between God's secret and revealed will, carried into his Institutes' rule for predestination: when the Lord closes his holy lips, the way to inquiry closes with them (as relayed by the secondary source in section 7). Turretin, in the locus on the divine decree, affirms the distinction of the will of decree and the will of precept, secret and revealed, with this verse among its standard proofs. The Westminster Confession cites Deuteronomy 29:29 in the proof texts of 3.8 (with Romans 9:20 and 11:33) for the direction that the doctrine of predestination "is to be handled with special prudence and care," by men "attending the will of God revealed in his Word." Note the exact shape of the confessional use: the verse governs the *manner* of handling the decree locus (prudence, attention to the revealed will) rather than supplying the locus's *matter*. Edition-specific entries for Calvin, Turretin, and the Confession's proof apparatus belong to the Phase 2 Appendix C register for Parts II and V.
