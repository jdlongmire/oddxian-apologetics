## 1. Text and translation

"And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good" (ESV). Hebrew: *we-hinneh tov meod*, "and behold, good exceedingly." The clause is the seventh and climactic divine evaluation in Genesis 1 (after 1:4, 10, 12, 18, 21, 25). Two features distinguish it from the previous six: its scope is comprehensive ("everything that he had made," not one domain at a time), and it alone carries the intensifier *meod*. No significant textual variant affects the clause.

## 2. Context

Genesis 1 evaluates each work as it is made, before the narrative introduces any human act, obedience, or failure. The evaluation is God's own, pronounced over a finished creative act, within a chapter whose verbs are speaking, separating, making, setting, ruling, blessing, and sanctifying. Nothing in the chapter speaks of testing, probation, growth, or consummation; those categories enter the narrative in Genesis 2 (the command, the "not good" of 2:18, the garden to be worked and kept) and are consummated only in Revelation 21-22. The study therefore asks a lexical question with theological weight: what does *tov meod* itself claim, and what does it not claim?

## 3. Word studies

### 3.1 Tov (Strong's H2896, adjective; root verb H2895)

BDB's sense structure for the adjective: (1) good, pleasant, agreeable: pleasant to the senses; pleasant to the higher nature; good, excellent of its kind; good, rich, valuable in estimation; good, appropriate, becoming; kind, benign; good, right (ethical). As a noun: a good thing, benefit, welfare, prosperity. The word is among the most frequent adjectives in the Hebrew Bible (about 740 occurrences, per Barščevski 2023, counting the verb and adjective together).

Contextual distribution in Genesis itself displays the range: excellent of kind and pleasing (1:4-25, of light, land, vegetation, luminaries, creatures); fitting for a purpose (2:9, trees good for food; 2:12, gold of the land is good, that is, of high quality); ethical (2:9, 17, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; 3:5-6); beneficial outcome in providence (50:20, "God meant it for good"); and negated where a created state lacks something proper to it (2:18, "it is not good that the man should be alone").

Finding: in Genesis 1 the operative senses are *excellent of its kind*, *appropriate and fitting*, and *approved by God*, applied in turn to non-moral entities (light, dry land, luminaries) as much as to the whole. The ethical sense the word can bear elsewhere is not the sense the chapter's usage selects. [Explicit text / lexical, HIGH]

### 3.2 Meod (Strong's H3966)

Adverbial intensifier: "exceedingly, very." In Genesis 1:31 it raises the comprehensive evaluation above the six preceding ones. It intensifies degree; it does not change category. *Tov meod* claims the whole is very good, not that the whole belongs to a different order of goodness than the parts had severally. [Explicit text / lexical, HIGH]

### 3.3 Tamim (Strong's H8549; from tamam, to be complete)

BDB: adjective, "complete, sound" (91 occurrences). Usage clusters:
- Sacrificial and cultic: "without blemish, without defect," the largest cluster (about half the occurrences; Exodus 12:5; Leviticus and Ezekiel passim).
- Persons: Noah, "righteous, tamim in his generations" (Genesis 6:9); the command to Abraham, "walk before me, and be tamim" (Genesis 17:1); Israel, "you shall be tamim with the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 18:13). The NET textual note on Genesis 6:9 observes that of men the word denotes blamelessness as integrity and non-violation in context, not sinless perfection.
- God and his works: "his work is tamim (perfect)" (Deuteronomy 32:4); God's way (Psalm 18:30); God's law (Psalm 19:7).
- Time and measure: a whole day (Joshua 10:13), complete Sabbaths and years (Leviticus 23:15; 25:30).

Finding: Hebrew possesses, and the Torah uses, a word for wholeness-without-defect, applied to animals, to men in their generations, and supremely to God's own work. That word is never applied to Adam, to Eden, or to the created order in Genesis 1-2. The absence is a distributional fact, and arguments from it must be weighted accordingly: it corroborates, and does not by itself prove, that Genesis 1:31 does not assert defect-free consummation. [Lexical distribution, HIGH for the distribution; the inference drawn from it is tagged below]

### 3.4 Shalem (Strong's H8003; from shalam, to be complete)

BDB: adjective, "complete, safe, at peace" (about 28 occurrences). Usage clusters:
- Fullness of measure: "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet shalem (complete)" (Genesis 15:16); a full weight and full measure (Deuteronomy 25:15); a full reward (Ruth 2:12).
- Finished work: stones finished at the quarry (1 Kings 6:7); the temple finished (2 Chronicles 8:16).
- Wholeness of state: whole, unhewn stones for the altar (Deuteronomy 27:6; Joshua 8:31); a person arriving shalem, safe and whole (Genesis 33:18).
- Undivided heart: "let your heart be shalem with the LORD your God" (1 Kings 8:61), the standing royal test (1 Kings 11:4; 15:3).

Finding: *shalem* is the vocabulary of completion reached: a measure that has filled up, a work that is finished, a heart that is undivided. None of its uses describes a beginning. Genesis 1:31, at the beginning, does not use it. [Lexical, HIGH]

### 3.5 The comparison the working paper asked for

The four words mark distinguishable claims:
- **Tov**: good, fitting, excellent of kind, approved. A verdict on quality relative to the Maker's purpose.
- **Meod**: degree only. Very.
- **Tamim**: whole and without defect, in the respect and context specified (a lamb, a man's walk in his generation, God's work absolutely).
- **Shalem**: complete, finished, undivided. A verdict on completion relative to a measure or an end.

*Tov meod* therefore asserts, lexically, that the completed creation was very good: fitting, excellent, approved in every part and in the whole. It does not assert, lexically, that the creation was tamim in the absolute respect in which God's own work is tamim, nor that it was shalem, finished toward its end. The words for those claims existed and were not used. [Theological synthesis from lexical data, MEDIUM: the data are firm; the weight placed on the selection among available words is a judgment, and is weighted further only in combination with section 4]

## 4. Canonical controls

- **Genesis 2:18.** Within the good creation, before any sin, God pronounces one state "not good": the man alone. The lack is supplied inside the creation order itself (2:21-24), and the comprehensive *tov meod* of 1:31 is spoken after the supply. The control shows that *tov* evaluation in Genesis operates relative to completeness of design: a thing can be genuinely of God's making, and the narrative can still register that something proper to it is not yet present. [Explicit text, HIGH]
- **Ecclesiastes 7:29.** Humanity was made *yashar*, upright. The treatise's claim of original uprightness rests here and on WCF 4.2, not on *tov*. [Explicit text, HIGH]
- **1 Corinthians 15:42-49 and Revelation 21-22.** The resurrection state is described in categories Genesis 1 does not use for Eden: incorruptible, immortal, no curse, no death, no mourning. The canonical trajectory from *tov meod* to the consummation is an increase in security and glory, not a mere restoration of a lost maximum. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM]

## 5. Competing readings

1. **"Very good" as maximal perfection, such that any later state can only restore, never exceed, Eden.** This reading loads *tov meod* with the content of tamim-absolute and shalem together. The lexical comparison does not support the load; and 1 Corinthians 15 describes the last Adam's state in terms the first Adam's never carried. Declined, while granting that the reading correctly protects Eden from being called defective.
2. **The traditional Reformed reading: creation in knowledge, righteousness, and holiness, with immortality conditional on obedience (WCF 4.2, 6.1).** Accepted in substance. The study's point is narrower: those attributes rest on the wider canon (Ecclesiastes 7:29; Ephesians 4:24; Colossians 3:10), and conditional immortality itself confirms that Edenic goodness was mutable, which is the treatise's thesis. No conflict.
3. **The functional reading: "good" means functioning as intended within the cosmic-temple order.** Compatible with the lexical findings (fitting, appropriate, excellent of kind) and accepted as a component of the sense, not the whole of it: Genesis 50:20 and the ethical uses show *tov* exceeds bare function.
4. **Eden as defective or as a failed first attempt.** Rejected outright. *Tov meod* is God's own verdict of excellence over the whole; the treatise's distinction is between goodness that is mutable and goodness that is confirmed, not between bad and good beginnings.

## 6. Findings, tagged

1. Genesis 1:31 asserts comprehensive divine approval: everything made was *tov*, and the whole was *tov meod*. [Explicit text, HIGH]
2. *Tov* in Genesis 1 operates in the senses fitting / excellent of kind / approved, including over non-moral entities; it does not lexically encode incorruptible, immutable, or consummated. [Lexical, HIGH]
3. *Meod* intensifies degree and does not change category. [Lexical, HIGH]
4. *Tamim* and *shalem*, the available vocabulary of wholeness-without-defect and of completion, are not used of creation in Genesis 1-2; *shalem* in all its uses describes completion reached, not a beginning. [Lexical distribution, HIGH]
5. The two-states thesis (very good and mutable vs perfected and incorruptible) is consistent with the lexical data and is positively supported by the canonical controls of section 4; it is not a lexical entailment of Genesis 1:31 taken alone. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM]
6. Nothing in the text or the lexicon supports calling Eden defective. [Explicit text with inference, HIGH]
