## 1. The question and the claim

This treatise answers one question: how does the God who is eternal triune loving communion govern a creation he pronounced very good, through a fall he did not author and yet did not merely permit, to a consummation more secure than the beginning, without evil becoming necessary, without the Fall becoming good, and without any particular evil becoming explicable on demand? The question joins four loci usually treated apart: the doctrine of God, the eternal decree, the root of sin, and the problem of evil. The treatise's wager, stated as a claim to be argued and not assumed, is that these are one locus, cohering only when read from a single archetype.

The claim has two load-bearing members.

**First member: the two states of created goodness.** Scripture's creation arc runs between two distinct states of the same created goodness. The first state is very good, upright, and mutable: God's verdict in Genesis 1:31 (tov meod, "good exceedingly") is comprehensive approval of a finished work, fitting and excellent of its kind, and the goodness vocabulary of Genesis 1 does not lexically encode incorruptible, immutable, or consummated (study 01; axioms III.1, III.2). Nothing in text or lexicon supports calling that state defective (axiom III.4): Adam stood upright, his will pliable in either direction, able to stand and able to fall (axiom III.5). The final state is confirmed and incorruptible: described by absolute negations (no death, no mourning, no crying, no pain, no cursed thing, no night), populated by gift, and measured by the maximum duration (Revelation 21-22; study 11; axioms VIII.1, VIII.2). The end exceeds the beginning on Eden's own terms, and the two states must not be collapsed. Between them stands the Fall: genuinely evil, genuinely regrettable, and necessary only relative to the redemptive decree God actually made, not metaphysically necessary, and nowhere said to have been required by the consummation it historically precedes (axiom V.5; audit item (iii)).

**Second member: one archetype.** Triune loving communion, God's own eternal life of love, is the moral archetype of the created order (axiom I.2). From that single principle the treatise draws three things the fields usually derive from three different sources. (a) The good: the Euthyphro dilemma dissolves because the good is neither a standard external to God nor an arbitrary product of bare will; God's will is itself the highest rule of perfection, and it is the will of the triune communion whose character is love, so that what God wills is good because of who God is (axiom IX.2; Calvin, Institutes 3.23.2, as carried in the research note on Augustine and Calvin). (b) Sin: sin's root is the privation and opposite of communion, the self closed in on itself, taking itself instead of God and neighbor as governing center (axiom IV.1, in the qualified form adopted by JD on 2026-11-01; sec. 4.6). (c) The end: consummation is communion perfected, priestly service and royal reign together in God's immediate presence (axiom VIII.5), the archetype's created image confirmed, not a different good substituted for the first.

Around these two members the treatise builds its account of the decree: God works all things according to the counsel of his will, with no remainder outside his counsel, that text specifying the domain of the working and not its mode (Ephesians 1:11; axioms II.1, II.2); all things are worked together toward the good for those who love God, the good defined forward and Christologically as conformity to the Son and glorification (Romans 8:28-30; axiom II.3); and in any single event the intentions of God and of wicked men are asymmetrical, God intending the deed for good and never devising the evil as evil (Genesis 50:20; axiom V.1). The eternal counsel is stated covenantally in the pactum (sec. 8.3), and the whole is bounded by the revealed/secret distinction of Deuteronomy 29:29 (sec. 5).

These are the treatise's theses. They are stated here without priority claims. Whether the two-states integration, or the archetype's triple use, has been stated elsewhere in the same form is a question the Appendix C registers exist to answer, and where they have not yet answered it the Prolegomena says so (sec. 9).

## 2. The aim and its three bars

The treatise is written to be a foundational contribution to Christian apologetics and systematics as fields, not only a synthesis of its author's corpus (work package, sec. 1; JD clarification of 2026-11-01). Synthesis is the means; a contribution the fields can build on, argue with, and cite is the aim. That aim sets three bars, recorded here because every Part is measured against them.

**Originality, honestly located.** Each Part carries a contribution statement naming what it adds beyond its sources: a new synthesis, a new argument, a resolution of a standing tension, or a sharper statement of an existing position. No originality claim stands until the Appendix C register for that Part has checked the broader corpus for the same claim in the same form; where the field has already said it, the treatise says so and positions itself as synthesis or extension rather than invention. Section 9 states the Prolegomena's own contribution under the same rule, including the checks not yet run.

**Field-facing rigor.** The treatise is addressed to competent readers in apologetics and systematics, including readers who disagree. Counter-positions are stated in their strongest form before they are answered (sec. 4.5). Every major claim is tagged so a reviewer can see exactly what is text, what is inference, and what is conjecture (sec. 4.3). Quotations are verified against the cited edition before use, and mediation, where it exists, is printed, not hidden (sec. 4.4).

**Fruitfulness.** Foundational means load-bearing for further work: stable definitions (sec. 6), an explicit argument architecture (54 axioms, a derivation map, and a 16-item consistency audit, all produced in Phase 2), and matrices others can use, test, and extend: the Scripture matrix (Appendix A), the unified argument map and epistemic ledger (Appendix B), and the per-locus broader-corpus registers (Appendix C, 189 entries across Parts I-X).

The treatise's confessional position is Reformed. Engagement with other traditions is not neutrality, and the treatise does not pretend otherwise; its disagreements are argued, and where disagreement remains after argument, Part X records it as genuine.

## 3. Sources: two corpora, one hierarchy

The treatise synthesizes two corpora and keeps their roles distinct.

**The internal corpus** is the author's prior work in this repository, verified on main as of 2026-11-01: the Covenant and Communion systematic theology (the axiomatic backbone); the Triune Loving Communion systematic theology, v0.1 (the governing thesis in compact form); its governing work package (canonical thesis, quality gates, claim tagging scheme); the argument map (dependency structure and epistemic ledger); the corpus-alignment record (integration decisions and the outstanding scrutiny list); the articles "The Eternal Trinitarian Covenant" and "A Systematic Declaration of Faith" (the latter used as a check on the treatise's own axiom set); "The Communion Argument"; the working paper "From Very Good to Perfected Loving Communion" (the decree volume: the two states, the problem of evil, modal analysis, impeccability, sections 6.1-6.24); and two teaching plates. The wider oddXian works (Consilience, Designism, the LPI and hydrotectonic material) stand outside this boundary: the treatise may cite them but does not absorb them.

**The broader corpus** is Christian scholarship across the tradition, engaged per locus and recorded in the Appendix C registers: creeds and councils from the conciliar definitions themselves (Nicaea, Ephesus, Chalcedon, Constantinople III); the fathers (Irenaeus, Athanasius, Maximus, Augustine); the medievals (Anselm, Aquinas); Reformation and Reformed orthodoxy in edition-specific citation (Calvin, Turretin, Owen, Witsius, Cocceius, the Westminster standards, Edwards); the later Reformed (Bavinck, Vos, Berkhof, Murray, Van Til); and contemporary scholarship and philosophy of religion, including critical interlocutors (Plantinga; skeptical theism in Wykstra and Bergmann; Marilyn McCord Adams on horrendous evils; Hick's soul-making theodicy and process and open-theist accounts, as counter-positions). Each register entry gives the edition, the position taken or contested, the treatise's disposition (adopt, adapt, or contest, with reasons), and the verification status.

**The hierarchy of authority across both corpora is fixed.** Scripture is primary and governing. The Westminster Confession and Catechisms are subordinate summaries, treated as boundary markers: they fix the fence inside which the treatise argues, and where the treatise goes beyond their letter it claims consistency and consequence from them, never confessional formulation it does not have (the pattern is stated at axiom VII.7 and applied throughout). Scholarship in every era is an interlocutor: cited from primary texts where available, edition-specific, never treated as proof by reputation, and answered where it disagrees; the registers record each disposition, with reasons.

The existing internal manuscripts are preserved as sources and are not silently overwritten. The treatise is the synthesis layer; the sources remain the record of how each part was developed. Where a source and the treatise differ (live cases: the sin-root claim and the Noahic binary; sec. 4.6), the difference is handled in the treatise's own voice, on the record, and the source stands unrevised.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 The two tracks

The treatise runs on two tracks, and no claim advances on one track alone where the other applies.

**Track A, exegetical.** Verse-by-verse primary-source exegesis of the load-bearing texts, with competing readings stated and weighed, before any claim built on a text advances past working-draft status. Phase 1 executed this track for the eleven anchor texts (sec. 5), one study per text, each closing the gaps the existing corpus had already named for that text. Each study follows a fixed order: text and translation (with textual-critical notes where a variant matters); context, with no claim from an isolated clause; word studies from the lexicon, with mediation labeled and semantic claims resting on usage in context rather than etymology; competing readings, with the reasons each is adopted or declined; findings with claim tags; and a gap statement naming what remains open as precisely as the findings name what is settled.

**Track B, logical.** Deduction by good and necessary consequence: every non-explicit claim shows its premises and its inference, in axiom, theorem, and lemma form, including formal consistency testing and modal analysis of the kind the working paper carries in its sections 6.5, 6.13, and 6.20. Phase 2 executed this track's foundation: one unified axiom set reconciling the internal corpus's three axiom systems, a derivation map, and a consistency audit, summarized in sec. 4.6 where its disciplines bind the reader-facing text.

### 4.2 The inferential rule: good and necessary consequence

The treatise's rule of inference is the Westminster Confession's own: "The whole counsel of God concerning all things necessary for His own glory, man's salvation, faith and life, is either expressly set down in Scripture, or by good and necessary consequence may be deduced from Scripture" (WCF 1.6). Two disciplines follow. First, a consequence must be good as well as necessary: the premises must be the text's own, the inference must actually follow, and both must be shown (axiom PRO.3). Second, the rule licenses warranted non-assertion as well as assertion: where Scripture and consequence run out, the treatise stops, tags the stopping place, and registers the question as open (Part X) rather than filling the silence with system. The standing example is Revelation 21-22's three silences: no reason given for the first creation's mutability, no claim that the Fall was necessary to reach the consummated state, no mechanism of confirmation (study 11, finding 4); each, where the treatise argues it at all, is argued as synthesis from the wider canon, labeled as such.

### 4.3 Claim tags and confidence ratings

Every major claim carries one of four tags, and a confidence rating, so that the reader never has to guess the epistemic kind of what is being asserted.

- **Explicit text:** the claim is stated in the cited text itself, on the reading the supporting study defends.
- **Necessary inference:** the claim follows from stated premises by good and necessary consequence; the premises and the inference are shown.
- **Theological synthesis:** the claim is constructed by collation across texts, or across text and tradition, and no single cited text states it in full. Synthesis is a respectable category in this treatise, and most of its architecture lives there; it is never dressed as either of the first two.
- **Philosophical conjecture:** the claim exceeds what text and consequence supply and is offered as reasoned speculation, to be weighed as such.

Confidence ratings are HIGH, MEDIUM, LOW, and UNCERTAIN, assigned per claim and inherited honestly: an argument carries the rating of its weakest load-bearing premise, and where a claim's rating differs by basis, both bases are printed. The standing example is impeccability, whose printed calibration the audit fixed and Part VII carries verbatim (sec. 4.6). Mediation limits recorded in the studies and notes are likewise inherited by every axiom and Part resting on them; they are collected in sec. 8.4.

### 4.4 Mediation honesty

Every broader-corpus source carries exactly one verification status in its register entry, and Parts may rest claims only on what the status supports. CONSULTED DIRECTLY: the text itself was read, in the edition or reproduction named. CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION: the content reached the project through a named intermediary, which is part of the entry and part of the citation. NOT YET CONSULTED: the source is registered as required, and nothing may rest on it until it is opened and checked. A scholar's own claim is distinguished from later summaries of it, and a source's silence is reported as silence, never filled from reputation. The model case is confessional: the Westminster standards do not name a covenant of redemption, and formulate neither two wills in Christ nor his impeccability (axioms II.6, VII.7); the treatise therefore teaches those doctrines as consequence and synthesis, at their printed calibrations, and never quotes the standards as confessing what they do not say.

### 4.5 The strongest-form rule

Opposing positions are stated fairly, and in their strongest available form, before they are answered, and the answer addresses the position as stated. The rule binds in both directions. Against opponents: Hodge's objection that temptation implies the possibility of sin is answered as a philosophical premise on its own level, not caricatured as an exegesis of Hebrews 4:15 (study 09, finding 9; axiom X.1); the corporate-election reading of Ephesians 1 is met on the passage's grammar, its true observations about the passage's corporate, in-Christ form retained (study 06). Against the treatise's own side: where a theologian of weight inside the treatise's own tradition dissents from a synthesis the treatise adopts, the dissent is recorded and the claim printed at its contested calibration (axiom X.2). And against the internal corpus: the Noahic binary in the slavery manuscript was tested against its own text, found unargued exactly where it claimed necessity, and is replaced in this treatise by a weaker, argued form (sec. 4.6), the manuscript left unrevised and the replacement argued in the open.

### 4.6 Standing disciplines from the consistency audit

The Phase 2 consistency audit tested the axiom set pairwise (16 items). Five items were disposed CONSISTENT outright; ten were disposed CONSISTENT WITH STATED DISCIPLINE, meaning the claims cohere only under disciplines the set itself states; one was disposed TENSION REQUIRING RESOLUTION at the corpus level, with its resolution path adopted. The disciplines that bind the reader-facing text are collected here, so that a reader of any Part can see the rules the whole treatise is under.

1. **Impeccability, one printed calibration.** "Impeccability is theological synthesis. From Hebrews 4:15 alone it stands at MEDIUM. On the treatise's full premise set (Hebrews 4:15 with 7:26-28, 1:12, 13:8; James 1:13; 1 John 3:5; 2 Corinthians 5:21; John 8:46; and the one-person premise of WLC 40) it stands at HIGH. It is not a conciliar definition and not a confessional article, and it is contested within the Reformed tradition (Hodge)." The calibration distinguishes the doctrine's tradition-status (neither conciliarly defined nor confessionally formulated, and dissented from by Hodge: MEDIUM as a tradition claim) from the strength of the treatise's own inference on its stated premise set (good and necessary consequence: HIGH as synthesis). Neither half may be printed without the other.
2. **Two ratings that must not borrow from each other.** The permanence of the consummated state is Necessary inference, HIGH (axiom VIII.2); the label for the saints' interior condition, confirmed and non posse peccare, is Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH (axiom VIII.3). No Part VIII argument may spend the first rating on the second claim, and no mechanism of confirmation may be presented as Revelation's teaching (axiom VIII.7).
3. **Felix culpa, three members or none.** The Fall is necessary relative to God's actual redemptive decree; it is not metaphysically necessary, Adam having been able to stand (axioms V.5, III.5); and no text, Revelation 21-22 expressly, claims the consummation required the Fall. "Relative to" is load-bearing, and no event-by-event indispensability may be inferred (axiom II.4).
4. **Domain and telos are divided between two texts, and neither does the other's work.** Ephesians 1:11 supplies the agency and domain of God's working (axiom II.1) and may not be cited for its mode, for a per-event benevolent telos, or for reprobation (axiom II.2). Romans 8:28-30 supplies the beneficiaries and the telos (axiom II.3) and may not be quoted as though it alone proved that no suffering is gratuitous; that universal claim is synthesis at MEDIUM, on the wider theodicy premises (axiom V.4). The governing premise does not depend on the Romans 8:28 variant: beneficiaries, comprehensiveness, and telos are identical on every attested reading (study 05).
5. **The boundary text is cited under its own discipline.** Deuteronomy 29:29 enters as the boundary of belonging (axiom PRO.1) and as an epistemic rule held by consequence, tagged synthesis at MEDIUM, never as a statement of the verse (axiom PRO.2; audit item (v)). It may not be cited to address any particular evil, to end a discussion, or to silence lament; the lament guardrail is carried on the Psalms and Job (axioms V.6, X.3).
6. **Three levels never merged.** The eternal triune life (Level 1), the eternal decree (Level 2), and the pactum as the decree's covenantal form toward the Mediator (Level 3) are distinct in every sentence that touches them (audit item (vi)); the pactum's MEDIUM tag attaches to Level 3's scriptural explicitness only. Guards: one divine will appropriated distinctly to the persons, never two wills bargaining (axioms I.3, II.6); the Spirit named explicitly in any statement of the pactum; Zechariah 6:13 never cited as a proof text; Hebrews 13:20 carried as a member of the pactum's evidence chain, not its source, its "eternal" being endless rather than beginningless (axiom II.7).
7. **The sin-root claim in its adopted form only.** Axiom IV.1 is universal at the level of root and orientation and deliberately non-committal at the level of individual motive, under qualifications Q1-Q4: guilt is not motive-indexed (Leviticus 4-5; Numbers 15); "closed" is the load-bearing term, and "interest" includes the negative preference for remaining undisturbed; self-preservation is a created good, sinful only when absolutized (Proverbs 29:25); and where Scripture does not disclose a motive, none may be asserted (2 Samuel 6). Earlier corpus formulations, quoted exactly where they are quoted, are glossed with Q1-Q4 in the treatise's own voice, so no reader can take the treatise to assert the unqualified form the counterexample test refuted (audit item (vii)).

9. **Levels in objections.** An objection is answered at its own level (text, inference, philosophy), and no premise may change levels in transit (axiom X.1). ## 5. The boundary at the outset, and the anchor texts

The Prolegomena's own anchor text is Deuteronomy 29:29: "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever, that we may do all the words of this law." Study 04 established what the verse does and does not say. It specifies belonging, not content: the hidden things belong to God, the revealed things to us, forever, for obedience (axiom PRO.1, Explicit text, HIGH). Its primary contextual referents are more specific than a general doctrine of mystery: hidden covenant treachery and its judgment on the hidden side, the law on the revealed side. Its extension to God's unrevealed counsel is legitimate, beginning inside its own chapter, but remains an extension. And the epistemic rule the theodicy needs, that from "no morally sufficient reason for this evil is visible to us" it does not follow that there is none, is a valid consequence of the verse's structure, not a statement of it (axiom PRO.2, Theological synthesis, MEDIUM); the verse supplies no positive evidence that a reason exists in any particular case. The boundary cuts both ways: the treatise may not use the secret things to end an argument, nor pretend the revealed things say less than they do. The revealed domain is entrusted precisely in order to do (axiom V.6).

The eleven anchor texts of Phase 1, each with its completed study, are the exegetical foundation on which the Parts stand; they are assigned to their Parts in sec. 7, and the full Scripture matrix, with each text's claim load, corpus coverage, and open items, is Appendix A.

## 6. Definitions used throughout

The following terms carry fixed senses in every Part. Where a term is the tradition's label rather than an author's own words, the definition says so.

- **Triune loving communion.** God's own eternal life: one God, three persons, whose mutual love requires no created object to complete it (John 17:24; 1 John 4:8, as carried in the internal corpus). As archetype (axiom I.2), it is the pattern from which the treatise draws its account of the good, its definition of sin, and the shape of the consummated state.

- **Very good (tov meod).** God's comprehensive favorable evaluation of the finished creation (Genesis 1:31): fitting and excellent of its kind, approved by God. The phrase intensifies degree; it does not change category, and does not itself specify immutability or consummation (study 01).
- **The two states.** The first state of created goodness: very good, upright, mutable. The final state: confirmed and incorruptible. Distinct states of one created totality under different orders (axioms III.3, VIII.4).
- **The four states of created freedom.** The Augustinian scheme as verified in the research note on Augustine and Calvin: (a) Adam before the fall, posse peccare et posse non peccare, able to sin and able not to sin, with a leavable aid (adjutorium sine quo non); (b) fallen man, in the condition the tradition tags non posse non peccare, not able not to sin (the tag is the tradition's label for Augustine's prose, not found verbatim in any Augustinian text opened, and never printed as his own words); (c) man under grace, posse non peccare, by an efficacious help bestowing perseverance itself, yet never in fact sinless in this life; (d) man in glory, non posse peccare, the last liberty much greater than the first (De correptione et gratia 12.33; Enchiridion 104-105).
- **Closed self-interest.** The root of sin in its adopted form: the fallen self, made for triune loving communion, taking itself rather than God and neighbor as its governing center; a condition inherited before it is chosen, proceeding in grasping, defensive, inert, and arbitral modes, claimed at the level of root and orientation, not of individual motive (axiom IV.1, with Q1-Q4). The tradition's Latin tag is incurvatus in se.
- **Asymmetrical intention.** In one and the same historical complex, human agents devising evil while God intends the deed itself for good, the evil never being the object of God's intention as evil (Genesis 50:20; axiom V.1).
- **Decretal necessity.** The certainty of what God has freely decreed, conditional upon that decree; distinguished from metaphysical necessity and from any claim that each particular event was instrumentally indispensable (axiom II.4).
- **Apparently / actually gratuitous suffering.** Apparent: suffering whose morally sufficient divine purpose is not discernible to a finite observer. Actual: suffering lacking any morally sufficient purpose. The framework excludes the second on its wider premises, at synthesis MEDIUM (axiom V.4), while fully admitting the first as the ordinary human experience of evil.
- **Accommodation.** God's regulation of a fallen structure, within the Noahic constraint and under stated principles, rather than its immediate abolition: staged covenantal administration, neither endorsement nor moral compromise (axioms VI.1, IX.3).
- **Pactum (pactum salutis).** The covenantal form in which the tradition states the eternal counsel of redemption toward the Mediator: the Father appointing the Son and promising reward and headship, the Son undertaking suretyship for the elect, the Spirit named explicitly; a construction by collation, formally distinct from the decree, not a second decree (axiom II.6; sec. 8.3).
- **Confirmation.** The securing of a will in righteousness such that it cannot fall: predicated of Christ by his impeccability (sec. 4.6, discipline 1) and of the saints in glory as synthesis at MEDIUM-HIGH (axiom VIII.3), never as a mechanism Revelation itself supplies.

## 7. The map of the treatise

The treatise proceeds in systematic order. Each Part is named below with its governing question, its anchor texts, its governing axioms, and its thesis as the derivation map states it. Appendix A gives the Scripture matrix; Appendix B the unified argument map; Appendix C the interlocutor registers (Parts I-V, 119 entries; Parts VI-X, 70 entries).

**Prolegomena (Part 0).** Question: by what authority, method, and boundaries does the treatise argue? Anchor text: Deuteronomy 29:29 (study 04). Axioms: PRO.1-PRO.3. Thesis: the boundary of belonging and the WCF 1.6 rule together fix what the treatise may assert, what it must show, and where it must stop.

**Part I: Theology proper.** Question: what is God eternally, and how is his own life the moral archetype of the created order? Anchor texts: Genesis 6:5-7 for the two poles of divine affection (study 02); John 17:24 and 1 John 4:8 as carried in the internal corpus. Axioms: I.1-I.3. Theses: T-I.1, God's eternal triune life is loving communion and the moral archetype of the created order; T-I.2, impassibility rightly stated denies involuntary and destabilizing passion, not genuine affection (the reconciliation is argued in Part V).

**Part II: The eternal covenant and God's good decree.** Question: how is God's one eternal counsel ordered toward redemption? Anchor texts: Ephesians 1:3-14 (study 06), Romans 8:28-30 (study 05), Hebrews 13:20 (study 10), with Genesis 50:20 and Genesis 1:31 in support. Axioms: II.1-II.8. Theses: T-II.1, the decree is one eternal counsel and the pactum its covenantal form toward the Mediator, formally distinct, never a negotiation between wills; T-II.2, election is unconditional and pretemporal, corporate in form and Christ-located, personal in its objects; T-II.3, the governing premise of Romans 8:28 is secure against its variant because Ephesians 1:11 supplies the agency; T-II.4, the resurrection is the covenant's first executed consequence, rendered to the Mediator on the ground of the covenant blood.

**Part III: Creation.** Question: what did God make, and in what state? Anchor text: Genesis 1:31 (study 01), with Revelation 21-22 read back under discipline (study 11). Axioms: III.1-III.5. Theses: T-III.1, the creation arc runs from a first state very good and mutable to a final state confirmed and incorruptible, the end exceeding the beginning on Eden's own terms; T-III.2, evil's origin is not in creation: the first state was upright and non-defective, its mutability a capacity, not a flaw.

**Part IV: Fall and sin.** Question: what is sin at its root, and what happened to created freedom? Anchor text: Genesis 6:5-7 (study 02), with the Augustinian four-state loci. Axioms: IV.1-IV.5. Theses: T-IV.1, every sin proceeds from the one root, the self closed in on itself, in its four modes, universal at the level of root and orientation, non-committal at the level of motive; T-IV.2, the four states order the whole biblical economy of the will; T-IV.3, the Fall was man's own act and fault, its root pride, the evil will preceding the evil deed.

**Part V: The decree and the problem of evil.** Question: how can the good decree govern evil without authoring it, excusing it, or explaining it on demand? Anchor texts: Genesis 50:20, Deuteronomy 29:29, Genesis 6:5-7, Romans 8:28-30, Ephesians 1:11 (studies 03, 04, 02, 05, 06). Axioms: V.1-V.6, under PRO.1-PRO.2. ## 8. Conventions
