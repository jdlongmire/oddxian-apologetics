## 1. The question of this Part and its theses

Part III has placed the first state before the reader: creation very good, man upright, his will in highest rectitude and pliable in either direction, possessed of a leavable aid and not yet of constancy to persevere. Part IV asks what happened, and what has been happening since. Its question is double. What was the fall, stated exactly, such that its fault is wholly man's and its root is one? And what is sin, stated exactly, such that one root can be claimed for all of it without claiming what Scripture refuses to give: a self-interested motive inside every sinful act?

The Part's theses, as the derivation map states them, are three.

**T-IV.1.** Every sin proceeds from the one root, the self closed in on itself, in its grasping, defensive, inert, and arbitral modes; the claim is universal at the level of root and orientation and non-committal at the level of individual motive. Depends on: IV.1, I.2.

**T-IV.2.** The four states of created freedom (posse peccare et posse non peccare; non posse non peccare; posse non peccare under efficacious grace; non posse peccare) order the whole biblical economy of the will. Depends on: III.5, IV.2, IV.4, VIII.3.

**T-IV.3.** The fall was man's own act and fault, and its root was pride, the evil will preceding the evil deed. Depends on: IV.3, V.3.

One discipline governs every sentence that follows, and it is stated here rather than discovered later. The sin-root claim is printed in this Part only in the qualified form JD adopted on 2026-11-01, on the evidence of the stress-test that the corpus-alignment scrutiny (item 1) required. The unqualified form, every sin derives from a self-interested motive, was tested against the four hardest classes of case (ignorance, omission, fear, misguided altruism) and failed: Leviticus 4-5 and Numbers 15 establish guilt where no self-interested willing is present or discoverable, and 2 Samuel 6 judges a man whose motive the text does not disclose. Drafting in the unqualified form is therefore barred, not as a stylistic preference but as a conclusion of evidence. Where this Part quotes the internal corpus's earlier, compact formulations of the root claim, it quotes them exactly and glosses them with the four qualifications (Q1-Q4) in the treatise's own voice, under the standing rule of the consistency audit, item (vii): the sources are preserved as the record and are not silently overwritten, and no reader is to be left able to take the treatise to assert the unqualified form. Section 12 performs that glossing locus by locus.

## 2. The fall: an evil will before an evil act

Genesis 3 narrates the fall as a sequence with an interior before it has an exterior. The serpent's proposal does not begin with the fruit but with a re-description of God and of man's place: the command is recast as deprivation ("Did God actually say..."), the threatened death is denied ("You will not surely die"), and the forbidden knowledge is offered as elevation ("You will be like God, knowing good and evil," Genesis 3:1, 4-5). The woman's act follows a judgment already made: the tree is seen to be "good for food," "a delight to the eyes," and "to be desired to make one wise" (3:6), a verdict rendered by creaturely judgment and desire elevated above the divine command, as the internal corpus's first description of the chapter puts it. The hand takes what the will has already taken. [Explicit text for the sequence; the corpus's description is its own, quoted in substance.]

Augustine states the order as a principle. "Our first parents fell into open disobedience because already they were secretly corrupted; for the evil act had never been done had not an evil will preceded it. And what is the origin of our evil will but pride? For 'pride is the beginning of sin.' And what is pride but the craving for undue exaltation? And this is undue exaltation, when the soul abandons Him to whom it ought to cleave as its end, and becomes a kind of end to itself. This happens when it becomes its own satisfaction" (City of God XIV.13, Dods translation, read directly; the Sirach citation stands at 10:13 in the NPNF note and 10:15 in the Clementine Vulgate numbering, and the dual citation 10:13/15 is retained for that reason; Augustine's exact Latin wording in this chapter was not verified). Axiom IV.3 receives the statement: pride, the craving for undue exaltation and self-sufficiency, is the beginning of sin, and the evil will precedes the evil act. Later in the same chapter Augustine compresses the mechanism into one sentence: "By craving to be more, man becomes less; and by aspiring to be self-sufficing, he fell away from Him who truly suffices him." The serpent's promise ("Ye shall be as gods," in the older rendering Augustine quotes) is the external form of an internal event that had, on his analysis, already occurred: man "had already begun to live for himself" before the devil could ensnare him in the open act. [Theological synthesis, HIGH as a report of the locus.]

The same root is older than the human fall. Of the devil, Augustine writes that he sinned not from the beginning of his created existence "but from the beginning of his sin, which began to be sin from his pride" (City of God XI.15, Latin read directly: ab initio peccati, quod ab ipsius superbia coeperit esse peccatum). Pride is therefore not a human peculiarity that the fall invented; it is the form creaturely defection takes, in the angel who fell before Eden and in the man who fell in it. Part IV states the root claim for human sin, as its texts require, but it does not present the closed self as an anthropological accident. [Theological synthesis, HIGH as a report of the locus; axiom IV.3.]

Calvin fixes the fall's ownership. Adam's state, carried from Part III (axiom III.5), is Institutes 1.15.8 in Beveridge's translation, read directly: "Adam, therefore, might have stood if he chose, since it was only by his own will that he fell; but it was because his will was pliable in either direction and he had not received constancy to persevere, that he so easily fell. Still he had a free choice of good and evil; and not only so, but in the mind and will there was the highest rectitude..." Two negations ride on that description, and this Part asserts both. The fall was not a defect of the constitution: a will pliable in either direction, in a state Augustine names posse peccare et posse non peccare, is mutability, not flaw (Part III, axiom III.4: Eden not defective). And the fall was not God's fault under cover of providence. At Institutes 3.23.8 Calvin holds the two clauses in one sentence, and the Part prints both with their mediation labeled: in Battles's rendering, "Man falls according as God's providence ordains, but he falls by his own fault" (Battles as quoted on secondary pages, cross-checked, SECONDARY); in Beveridge's, read directly, "Man therefore falls, divine providence so ordaining, but he falls by his own fault," with the ground added: God had declared all he made very good (Genesis 1:31), so depravity is not from creation; "man's own wickedness corrupted the pure nature which he had received from God, and his ruin brought with it the destruction of all his posterity." The Latin tag for the key clause, suo vitio cadit, reaches the treatise through Shedd's quotation of Calvin (SECONDARY). The providence clause belongs to Part V's locus, where the fall's standing under the decree is argued; what Part IV takes from the locus is the fault clause, entire and unsoftened: suo vitio, by his own fault. T-IV.3 depends at this joint on axiom V.3, and the dependence is recorded, not hidden. [Theological synthesis, HIGH as a report of the loci.]

The fall, then, in this Part's exact statement: a creature upright and able to stand, aided but not confirmed, first willed himself as his own end (pride, self-sufficiency, the soul become a kind of end to itself), and then acted what he had willed; the fault is wholly his; and the root of the act is the same root this Part will find under every subsequent sin, not as a motive inside each act but as the orientation the fall installed and every sinner inherits. Whether that root claim can be made universal, and in what form, is the work of the next six sections.

## 3. The root stated: the adopted formulation and its architecture

The treatise's root claim is printed here in full, in the formulation the stress-test proposed and JD adopted on 2026-11-01 (research note on the sin-root claim, sec. 7; axiom IV.1):

"Sin's root is the self closed in on itself. Made for triune loving communion, the fallen self takes itself, rather than God and neighbor, as its governing center; this closure is a condition inherited before it is chosen, and it is the orientation from which particular sins proceed. It proceeds in distinguishable modes: grasping, as the self asserts and acquires for itself; defensive, as self-preservation is absolutized and fear governs; inert, as the self rests undisturbed and love does not go out to the neighbor; and arbitral, as the self sets its own judgment of the good in place of God's word, most subtly in religion itself. Particular sins need not each carry a conscious self-interested motive: Scripture holds men guilty of sins they did not know they were committing, because guilt rests on the disordered condition and the act against God's order, not on the agent's awareness of his own curvature, which he characteristically does not perceive. The claim is therefore universal at the level of root and orientation, and deliberately non-committal at the level of individual motive, where Scripture itself is often silent."

Four features of that paragraph carry the whole Part, and each is a qualification with a name.

The claim's level is condition and orientation (Q1). "Root" names the fallen state and settled direction of the self, inherited before it is chosen, from which particular sins proceed. It does not name the proximate motive of each sinful act. The stress-test found that every counterexample difficulty arose from confusing these two levels; at the motive level the universal claim fails, and at the root level it stands. Sections 4-7 expound the qualifications one by one.

The load-bearing word is "closed," not "interest" (Q2). Closure is defined against Part I's archetype: the self made for triune loving communion, whose good is to be opened by love toward God and neighbor, instead takes itself as its governing center. "Interest" is retained from the corpus's vocabulary but is expressly defined to cover the negative preference for remaining undisturbed, since the inert mode grasps nothing. If closure were defined as advantage-seeking, omission would refute the claim; defined as the self not opened by love, omission becomes its purest specimen. The definition is stated, not smuggled, and the confidence of the omission class is carried at MEDIUM precisely because this definitional step is where the claim could cheat (sec. 5).

The modes are four, and they are modes of one root, not four roots. Grasping: the self asserts and acquires for itself (pride in its acquisitive form, spoil, exploitation). Defensive: self-preservation, a created good, is absolutized until fear governs conduct in place of God (Q3). Inert: the self rests undisturbed; love does not go out; the neighbor does not register. Arbitral: the self sets its own judgment of the good in place of God's word, most subtly inside religion (Q4). The modes overlap in concrete sins, as section 6's fearful servant shows (defensive fear resting on a false picture, the falsehood note), and the treatise claims the differentiation, not a taxonomy of watertight compartments. The tradition's own differentiation of sin's forms under one incurvature is the warrant for the plurality: Augustine states the root as two loves, an orientation, not a motive inventory (City of God XIV.28, Dods, read directly: "two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self"); Luther predicates the curvature of nature itself (sec. 4); and Barth's three forms, pride, sloth, and falsehood, developed, in Jenson's summary, "first and foremost by broken relationships in which people live for themselves rather than for God and others," show the incurvatus tradition itself refusing to flatten sin into grasping alone (Barth, Church Dogmatics IV/1-3, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION: Matt Jenson, The Gravity of Sin, as reported in the Themelios review and the Princeton Center for Barth Studies summary; no direct CD text was consulted, and nothing in this Part rests on Barth beyond this labeled mediation).

One epistemic ground underlies the whole construction, and it is a text, not a temperament. "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it? 'I the LORD search the heart and test the mind'" (Jeremiah 17:9-10). The Hebrew carries more than the rendering shows. The word for "deceitful" is akov, crooked, from the root of the heel and the supplanter: the heart is bent, not straight, twisted back on its own track. The word for its sickness is anush, incurable, beyond remedy by its bearer. And the question "who can know it?" is answered from the other side: not the heart's owner, but the LORD who searches it. Proverbs states the same asymmetry from inside the agent: "All the ways of a man are pure in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the spirit" (Proverbs 16:2; compare 21:2). Man's ways look clean to the eyes that chose them; the weighing belongs to God. This is why the root claim is non-committal about individual motives as a matter of principle rather than caution: the agent is, by the text's own diagnosis, the least reliable witness to the state of his own heart, and a hamartiology that required an audited self-interested motive in every sin would be asking the deceitful heart to certify the evidence against itself. Luther saw exactly this, and made Jeremiah 17:9, with Psalm 19:12 ("hidden faults"), his proof texts for the curvature's characteristic unconsciousness (sec. 4).

## 4. Q1: the root is a condition, not a motive (ignorance absorbed)

The ignorance class is the claim's hardest test and its clearest vindication at the qualified level. Leviticus 4-5 institutes atonement for sins committed unintentionally by every rank of the community: the anointed priest (4:3), the whole congregation, where "the thing is hidden from the eyes of the assembly" (4:13), a leader (4:22), a common person (4:27). In each case the formula is the same: "when the sin which they have committed becomes known," atonement is made and forgiveness follows, which means the guilt was real before it was known. Leviticus 5:17 states the principle without remainder: "If anyone sins... though he did not know it, then he shall be guilty and shall bear his iniquity." No self-interested willing is anywhere in view, and inventing one would contradict the texts, whose whole point is that the guilt does not wait for a motive. [Explicit text, HIGH.]

Numbers 15:22-29 extends the provision and then, at verses 30-31, sets the contrast class that grades the whole field: the sin "with a high hand," whose perpetrator "has despised the word of the LORD," is not covered by the provision for error. The text itself, therefore, grades sin by orientation (mistake versus contempt, inadvertence versus despising the word) and not by surface psychology. That grading is Scripture's own version of the distinction this Part is built on. The aggravated root, where Scripture names one, is an orientation toward God; the guilt of the ignorant case rests on the act against God's order and the condition of the actor, not on any motive the actor could have reported. [Explicit text for the two provisions and the contrast; the orientation reading is the study tradition's own grading made explicit, Necessary inference, HIGH.]

The New Testament holds the same two truths in one breath. Ignorance mitigates: "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do" (Luke 23:34) pleads ignorance as ground for intercession; Peter tells the Jerusalem crowd, "I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers" (Acts 3:17), and calls the same audience to repent in the same sermon (3:19); Paul "received mercy because I had acted ignorantly in unbelief" (1 Timothy 1:13) and in the same passage names himself "the foremost of sinners" (1:15). Mitigation without innocence: the ignorance is real, the sinner status is real, and neither cancels the other. A root claim at the level of motive cannot process these texts without violence to one side or the other; a root claim at the level of condition processes them exactly as written. The ignorant sinner sins from inside a condition he did not choose at that moment and cannot inspect: a humanity curved inward is characteristically inattentive to the law it has not studied and the neighbor it has not noticed, and its errors about God's commands are the default of the condition, not exceptions to it. Leviticus 5:1, the witness who hears a public adjuration and does not speak, sits precisely on the seam between this class and the next: an ignorant sin that is also an omission. [Theological synthesis, HIGH for the class adjudication, per the stress-test's verdict.]

Luther's scholion on Romans 5 supplies the tradition's own statement of the level at which the claim operates. In the full text consulted directly (uecf.net display; the same passage is cited as Luther's Works vol. 25, p. 345 in the Mockingbird glossary, that citation form CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION), he writes that "our nature, by the corruption of the first sin, [is] so deeply curved in on itself that it not only bends the best gifts of God towards itself and enjoys them... or rather even uses God himself in order to attain these gifts, but it also fails to realize that it so wickedly, curvedly, and viciously seeks all things, even God, for its own sake," and he proves the unconsciousness from Jeremiah 17:9 and Psalm 19:12. Three contributions are taken here and used throughout the Part. Incurvature is predicated of nature by the corruption of the first sin: of the inherited condition, which is the level Q1 assigns. Its paradigm theater is religion itself, which section 7's arbitral mode requires. And it is characteristically unconscious, "fails to realize," which is the dogmatic form of Jeremiah 17:9 and the reason guilt cannot be motive-indexed without absurdity: the curvature's bearer is the last to know. [Theological synthesis, HIGH as a report of the locus.]

Q1 therefore stands as follows. The root is the fallen condition and orientation of the self, inherited before it is chosen; guilt is not motive-indexed (Leviticus 5:17; Numbers 15); ignorant sins are rooted in the closed condition and its inherited disorder even where no self-interested willing is present or discoverable. The unqualified form's first pressure point was here, and it was refused here: nowhere does this Part write, or permit its readers to infer, that the priest of Leviticus 4 or the erring congregation of Numbers 15 was pursuing an advantage. [Stress-test verdict: ignorance absorbed with qualification Q1, HIGH.]

## 5. Q2: closure is the load-bearing term (omission absorbed, modes named)

Omission is the class where the word "interest" is at risk of stretching until the claim proves itself by equivocation, and the stress-test rated this class MEDIUM for exactly that reason. The cases are stark. In Matthew 25:41-46 the goats are condemned entirely for things not done: no feeding, no welcome, no clothing, no visit. Their defense is a profession of not having seen: "Lord, when did we see you hungry or thirsty..." (25:44). The King's reply treats the failure to serve "the least of these" as failure toward himself (25:45). Nothing in the text suggests advantage-seeking. The goats gained nothing by their neglect; their sin is that love never went out, that the suffering neighbor simply never registered on a life enclosed in its own round. James 4:17 supplies the easier end of the class: "whoever knows the right thing to do and fails to do it, for him it is sin," where knowledge is built into the definition and the failure is a knowing refusal or neglect. Luke 10:30-32 supplies the visual form: the priest and the Levite each saw the wounded man "and passed by on the other side." Passing by is deliberate non-approach with the victim in view. Their motives (fear of robbers on a dangerous road, purity concerns over a possibly dead body, cost and delay) are not stated in the text, and under Q4 they are not asserted here; what is stated is the non-approach itself.

If "self-interest" meant active pursuit of one's own advantage, these cases would refute the root claim, and this Part says so plainly rather than rescuing the claim by redefinition after the fact. The rescue, such as it is, was built into the adopted formulation before the cases were pressed: the load-bearing term is "closed." The closed self is the self not opened by love toward God and neighbor, and omission is not an embarrassment to that definition but its purest specimen: closure with no grasping at all, the self remaining undisturbed inside its own circuit. There is also a defensible negative sense of interest, and Q2 names it expressly so that it is a definition and not a smuggle: the omission preserves the agent's existing state (comfort, schedule, safety, purity) against the interruption the neighbor represents, and the priest's and Levite's visible avoidance, crossing the road to keep the interruption at distance, fits that reading. The negative preference for remaining undisturbed is a real preference, and "interest" is defined in this Part to include it. With the definition stated, the absorption is real; the stress-test's MEDIUM is retained because the definitional step is the claim's most contestable joint, and the treatise does not pretend otherwise (sec. 13, objection (e)).

The inert mode therefore stands in the formulation as a named mode, not a footnote: the self rests undisturbed and love does not go out to the neighbor. Barth's second form of sin, sloth, gives the mode dogmatic standing inside the incurvatus tradition itself: an inert refusal of one's God-given freedom, distinct from pride's grasping and held together with it under the one root (Barth via Jenson, SECONDARY mediation as labeled in sec. 3; Jenson's own reading holds pride and sloth together as distinct expressions of the one incurvature). The tradition distinguishes the modes rather than pretending every sin grasps, and this Part follows the tradition's differentiation rather than flattening it.

Q2 stands as follows. "Closed" is primary: the self not opened by love toward God and neighbor. The modes are named: grasping, defensive, inert, arbitral. "Interest" expressly includes the negative preference for remaining undisturbed. Absent those definitions the absorption of omission would be verbal, not real; with them, omission is located without residue: it is the root in its resting state. [Stress-test verdict: omission absorbed with qualification Q2, MEDIUM.]

## 6. Q3: the defensive mode (fear absorbed; preservation itself is good)

The fear class presses a different point: not that the agent grasped nothing, but that what moved him, self-preservation, is not obviously a sin at all. The Part concedes the premise in full. Self-preservation is a created good, and the treatise must not identify it with closed self-interest. Flight from danger is commended or permitted in Scripture: "when they persecute you in one town, flee to the next" (Matthew 10:23); Paul is lowered in a basket and escapes Damascus (Acts 9:25). A doctrine that made the instinct of self-preservation sinful as such would condemn what the canon blesses, and would confuse the fall with finitude.

Fear becomes sin at a specifiable point: where the good of safety is absolutized, ranked above faithfulness and truth, so that the self's survival and standing govern conduct in place of God. That is a disorder of loves, and it is incurvature in a defensive posture: the self contracted around its own preservation, closed against the claim of God and the witness owed to the neighbor. The cases fall exactly on that line. Peter, who had protested undying loyalty hours earlier (Matthew 26:33-35), denies Jesus three times before servants and bystanders (26:69-75): no advantage is sought beyond safety and standing, and that is the point; preservation has been enthroned over confession. The parents of the man born blind deflect the interrogation ("He is of age; ask him") "for fear of the Jews," because expulsion from the synagogue had been agreed for confessors (John 9:22): social self-preservation produces evasive speech that falls short of bearing witness. Proverbs 29:25 names the mechanism and its alternative in one line: "The fear of man lays a snare, but whoever trusts in the LORD is safe." Scripture locates the fear-sin in the trust order, not in appetite: the snare is a misdirected reliance, preservation trusted in place of God. And the one-talent servant states his own motive: "I was afraid, and I went and hid your talent in the ground" (Matthew 25:24-25). The master's verdict joins the modes: "You wicked and slothful servant" (25:26), defensive fear and inert sloth in a single sentence, and the fear itself rests on a false picture of the master ("a hard man"), which ties the case to the falsehood note in the tradition's differentiation (Barth via Jenson, SECONDARY, as labeled). Revelation 12:11 supplies the positive counter-image: the conquerors "loved not their lives even unto death," showing that what fear-sins surrender is precisely the ordered subordination of one's life to God. [Explicit text for the cases; the absolutization criterion is the formulation's own, Necessary inference from the trust framing of Proverbs 29:25, HIGH.]

Q3 stands as follows. Creaturely self-preservation is good and is sin only when absolutized over faithfulness and truth; fear is the named defensive mode of the closed self, not a reduction of every fear to advantage-seeking. [Stress-test verdict: fear absorbed with qualification Q3, HIGH.]

## 7. Q4: the arbitral mode and the motive-opacity rule (misguided altruism absorbed; Uzzah the residue)

The fourth class is the strongest of the four, because its agents mean well, or say they do, and because its mechanism is the one Luther identified as incurvature's paradigm theater: religion itself. The mechanism can be named from the texts: the self as arbiter of the good, substituting its own judgment of what God should want for God's stated word.

Saul (1 Samuel 15) is the fullest case. The sparing of Agag and of "the best of the sheep and of the oxen... all that was good" (15:9) is defended as altruism toward God's honor: "the people spared the best of the sheep and of the oxen, to sacrifice to the LORD your God" (15:15, 21). The narrative refuses to let the rationale stand at face value. Samuel names the act "pounce on the spoil" (15:19) and pronounces the rule that governs the whole class: "to obey is better than sacrifice" (15:22). Saul blames the people (15:15, 21, 24) while claiming "I have obeyed" (15:20); and 15:12 records, almost in passing, that Saul had "set up a monument for himself" at Carmel. Here self-interest is not merely inferable; the text arranges the evidence of it around the pious explanation, grasping and arbitration together: the spoil kept, the verdict self-rendered, the monument raised. Peter's case is purer and therefore more diagnostic. "Far be it from you, Lord! This shall never happen to you" (Matthew 16:22) is spoken out of evident love for Jesus, and Jesus' diagnosis is precise: "you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man" (16:23). The sin is located not in appetite but in arbitration: Peter's own conception of the good, sincerely benevolent, set against the declared will of God. Martha (Luke 10:40-42) is borderline as sin at all, and the Part carries her at the weight the stress-test gave her: "distracted with much serving," she asks Jesus to conscript Mary into her own pattern of service, and the gentle correction ("anxious and troubled about many things... one thing is necessary") diagnoses service curved back into self-reference: one's own standard of devotion imposed on another. If it is sin, its root is the arbitral mode in miniature.

The arbitral mode has a canonical headline, and it is a refrain about an age: "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 17:6; 21:25). Deuteronomy 12:8 gives the warning in advance, at the threshold of the land, forbidding exactly that arrangement ("You shall not do according to all that we are doing here today, everyone doing whatever is right in his own eyes") at the point where a norm outside the self is being established. Judges then narrates the norm's absence, twice adding the diagnosis, "In those days there was no king in Israel." Note what the refrain does and does not say. It does not say they did what was evil in their own eyes. The eyes render the verdict "right," and the eyes belong to the accused: this is Jeremiah 17:9 operating socially, the deceitful heart issuing its own acquittal, and Proverbs 16:2 standing over it ("All the ways of a man are pure in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the spirit"). When the closed self is collectivized, a whole order does right in its own eyes, and self-report becomes the least reliable evidence in the case. Saul's "I have obeyed," said sincerely over the bleating of the spared sheep, is the refrain in one man's mouth. The arbitral mode is therefore not a private quirk of religious temperaments; it is the Bible's own diagnosis of what a self, or a society, becomes when its judgment of the good answers to nothing above itself. [Explicit text for the refrain and its placement; the incurvature reading is the Part's synthesis, HIGH for the textual facts, MEDIUM-HIGH for the synthesis.]

Against that strength stands the residue, and the Part states it without flinching. Uzzah (2 Samuel 6:6-7): when the oxen stumbled at the threshing floor of Nacon, "Uzzah put out his hand to the ark of God and took hold of it," and God struck him down "because of his error." The text does not state Uzzah's motive. Care for the ark is a reasonable inference from the act; it is not a datum, and this Part does not assert it. What is textual is the surrounding order: the ark was being carried on a new cart, Philistine fashion (compare 1 Samuel 6:7-8), against the prescribed carrying by poles on the shoulders of the Kohathites (Exodus 25:13-15; Numbers 4:15, "they must not touch the holy things, lest they die"; Numbers 7:9). Modern commentary consulted for the stress-test (Crossway, "Tough Passages," on 2 Samuel 6, read directly) agrees that the action was "not motivated by irreverence" yet was "still sacrilegious, violating the holiness of God," and observes that Uzzah would not have been in that position had the transport been ordered rightly in the first place. The case is therefore absorbed at the level of the disordered act and its ordering, and not at the level of a proven motive: a procession designed by human judgment in place of the revealed order is the arbitral mode at the corporate level, David's before it is Uzzah's, and Uzzah's act is judged as unauthorized handling within that disorder. At the level of Uzzah's individual psychology the treatise claims nothing, because Scripture claims nothing. A thesis that required a self-interested motive in Uzzah himself would assert what the text withholds, and the refusal to assert it is not a gap in the claim but one of its stated rules.

That rule is Q4's second half, the motive-opacity rule: where Scripture judges an act without disclosing a motive, the treatise claims the root at the level of the disordered act and its ordering, and asserts no motive. The rule is not an escape hatch manufactured for this case. It is Jeremiah 17:9 applied as method: the heart is akov and anush, unknowable to its bearer and unsearchable by his neighbors; Scripture itself, which could disclose motives, often declines to; and a treatise that invents motives where its governing text is silent has ceased to do exegesis. The same rule governed this Part's handling of the priest and the Levite in section 5. [Stress-test verdict: misguided altruism absorbed with qualification Q4, MEDIUM; Uzzah the honest residue, absorbed at the level of act and order only.]

## 8. The four classes adjudicated in summary; a fifth test from the legacy corpus

The adjudications stand as follows, and the Part prints them together so that its claim's exact strength is visible in one place. Ignorance: absorbed with qualification Q1, HIGH; guilt is not motive-indexed, and the root is claimed at the level of condition and inherited disorder. Omission: absorbed with qualification Q2, MEDIUM; closure is the load-bearing term, the inert mode is named, and "interest" is defined to cover the negative preference for remaining undisturbed. Fear: absorbed with qualification Q3, HIGH; preservation is a created good, sinful only when absolutized, and fear is the defensive mode. Misguided altruism: absorbed with qualification Q4, MEDIUM; the arbitral mode is named, and the motive-opacity rule bounds every motive attribution, with Uzzah as the stated residue at the level of motive and an absorbed case at the level of act and order. In every class the difficulty came from one ambiguity, root as proximate motive or root as orientation and condition; read in the first sense the claim fails, is strained, or overreaches, and read in the second the classes are not merely absorbed but positively mapped: the closed self grasps, contracts, rests inert, and directs itself religiously, while its inherited disorder generates guilt-bearing error where the will is not engaged in the particular act. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH for the qualified claim as a whole, inheriting the class verdicts: axiom IV.1's calibration.]

The legacy corpus named a fifth test alongside these four, and the Part answers it here rather than leaving it unaddressed: "action against conscience (Romans 14:23)," in the v0.1 systematic theology's own list of what the universal assertion must account for (quoted and glossed in sec. 12). Paul's ruling is that "whoever has doubts is condemned if he eats, because the eating is not from faith. For whatever does not proceed from faith is sin" (Romans 14:23). The act may be, in itself, indifferent (the eating of meat); the sin lies in the agent's acting against his own apprehension of God's rule for him. This locates without strain at the orientation level: the self divided against its own judgment of God's will is the arbitral structure inverted, the agent overriding in act the verdict his conscience has rendered, which is closure against God at the point of conscience itself. No self-interested motive need be present or asserted; the disordered relation between the agent's judgment and his act is the datum, and the root claim covers it as it covers Uzzah, at the level of the disordered act and its ordering. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM: the stress-test did not adjudicate this case as a separate class, and the Part records its treatment as its own extension of the note's architecture, not as one of the note's verdicts.]

## 9. Transmission: the condition inherited before it is chosen

The formulation's phrase "a condition inherited before it is chosen" requires its own section, because Q1's whole adjudication of the ignorance class depends on it: ignorant sins are rooted in "the closed condition and its inherited disorder," and if there is no inherited disorder, that sentence is empty. The treatise's warrant, stated at the level the sources support, is threefold.

First, the apostolic frame as the notes carry it. Luther's scholion is a scholion on Romans 5, and its predicate is nature under "the corruption of the first sin": the curvature belongs to what man now is by propagation, before any act of his own, which is why it can be unconscious and why it can generate guilt where the will is not engaged. Romans 5:12-21 stands behind that reading as the canon's own architecture of the two humanities, in Adam and in Christ: sin and death spread to all through the one man's trespass, and the condition precedes its members' individual acts in the text's own ordering ("death reigned" over those whose sinning was not like Adam's transgression, 5:14). This Part states the frame as the notes carry it and does not build a mechanics of propagation on it. [Theological synthesis, HIGH for the frame as carried; the mechanics are expressly left open, sec. 15.]

Second, Augustine's prose for the resulting state, which axiom IV.2 receives. In the Enchiridion (ch. 30 in the NPNF chaptering, Shaw translation, read directly; the modern CCL section number for the passage was not verified), Augustine argues from the suicide analogy: as the man who kills himself cannot restore himself to life, so "when man by his own free-will sinned, then sin being victorious over him, the freedom of his will was lost," for "of whom a man is overcome, of the same is he brought in bondage" (2 Peter 2:19). The state is then described, not tagged: "he who is the servant of sin is free to sin. And hence he will not be free to do right, until, being freed from sin, he shall begin to be the servant of righteousness." De correptione et gratia ch. 12 (Wallis, read directly) states the loss in one clause: "But now, since that great freedom has been lost by the desert of sin, our weakness has remained to be aided by still greater gifts." The tradition's tag for this condition is non posse non peccare, and this Part uses the tag under the discipline axiom IV.2 itself records: the tag was not found verbatim in any Augustinian text opened for this treatise; it is the later tradition's label (used, for example, in the editor's note in Bavinck, Reformed Ethics, vol. 1, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION) for the condition Augustine describes in the prose just quoted. The treatise prints the tag as the tradition's, and the prose as Augustine's, and never the tag inside quotation marks as Augustine's own words. [Theological synthesis, HIGH as a report of the loci: axiom IV.2.]

Third, Calvin's transmission clause, already quoted in section 2: man's "ruin brought with it the destruction of all his posterity" (Institutes 3.23.8, Beveridge, read directly). The ruin is not Adam's private misfortune; posterity is in it. Together the three warrants support exactly what the formulation asserts and no more: the closed condition is inherited before it is chosen; the fall's freedom was lost by desert, not by constitution (sec. 2); and the state of the inheritors is servitude to sin with freedom to sin remaining. What they do not supply, the Part does not invent. Edwards's Original Sin, the work package's named early-modern control on the propagation of the corrupt disposition, stands at NOT YET CONSULTED in this Part's register, and Calvin's own loci on transmission (Institutes 2.1.4-5 and 2.2) were not opened in the research pass and stand at NOT YET CONSULTED likewise. The inherited-condition claim therefore rests on the loci quoted, at their stated strength, and the fuller mechanics of propagation are registered as open work (sec. 15), not filled in from reputation.

## 10. The four states as this Part's frame (T-IV.2)

The root claim describes what sin is; the fourfold scheme describes where the will stands, in each economy, relative to it. The scheme is Augustine's, from De correptione et gratia 12.33 (Latin and Wallis translation, read directly): "Therefore the first liberty of the will was to be able not to sin, the last will be much greater, not to be able to sin; the first immortality was to be able not to die, the last will be much greater, not to be able to die; the first was the power of perseverance, to be able not to forsake good, the last will be the felicity of perseverance, not to be able to forsake good." The tag set that labels the four states is, like the state (b) tag, the later tradition's standardization of Augustine's distinctions (Bavinck's editor's note, SECONDARY, as labeled); Augustine's verified verbatim collocations are posse non peccare, non posse peccare, posse non mori, non posse mori, and his prose. The Part uses the tags under the same discipline as axiom IV.2. A different fourfold in the Enchiridion (31.118: ante legem, sub lege, sub gratia, in pace, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION through Janssen's citation) orders man's historical economies, not his will's capacities, and is not conflated with the posse scheme anywhere in this treatise.

State (a), before the fall: posse peccare et posse non peccare. Adam upright, his aid an adjutorium sine quo non, a help "without which he could not of free will persevere," but not a help by which perseverance was effected (De correptione et gratia ch. 12, Wallis, read directly); Calvin's counterpart is 1.15.8 as quoted in section 2. This state is Part III's ground, presupposed here.

State (b), fallen: the condition of section 9. The first freedom is lost "by the desert of sin"; the servant of sin is free to sin and not free to do right until freed. Every analysis in sections 4-8 is an analysis of the will in this state: its guilt without willing (Q1), its rest without openness (Q2), its contraction around preservation (Q3), and its self-arbitration (Q4) are the phenomenology of a will that cannot not sin, stated at the level of orientation rather than of metaphysical compulsion in each act.

State (c), under grace: posse non peccare, recovered but never, in via, sinlessness in fact. The mechanism is the contrast of the aids. To the predestined, Augustine writes, "the aid of perseverance that is given is not such as the former, but such that to them perseverance itself is bestowed; not only so that without that gift they cannot persevere, but, moreover, so that by means of this gift they cannot help persevering" (De correptione et gratia ch. 12, Wallis, read directly; axiom IV.4). Yet the same passage holds the other pole without embarrassment: "although as long as they live here they are fighting against sinful lusts, and some sins creep upon them unawares, on account of which they daily say, 'Forgive us our debts,' yet they do not any more obey the sin which is unto death." Two disciplines follow, and both are standing rules of this treatise. First, state (c) is never stated as attained sinlessness: no saint in via is in fact without sin. Augustine's sole exception, the Virgin Mary "by a singular grace," is registered as his judgment, quoted from De natura et gratia 36.42 (PL 44:267, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION through Juniper Carol's study: "with the exception of the holy Virgin Mary in regard to whom, out of respect for the Lord, I do not propose to have a single question raised on the subject of sin"), and is flagged as Augustine's judgment rather than adopted as a claim of this treatise (axiom IV.4). Second, the tag for state (c) is carried in both its forms: the short form posse non peccare, and the fuller form the tradition also uses, posse peccare aut non peccare (Bavinck's editor, SECONDARY), the fuller form guarding the same point the prose makes, that the graced will in via can still sin and daily does, while no longer obeying sin unto death. Enchiridion 28.104-107 corroborates the scheme from the other end (Outler translation, read directly): man was created able to will good and evil, and in the future life will be unable to will evil, "and yet this will not thereby restrict his free will. Indeed, his will will be much freer."

State (d), in glory: non posse peccare, the "much greater" last liberty, where the aid is no longer leavable and the will cannot forsake the good. The angels who stood received this confirmation as reward, with "the fullest certainty of always abiding in it," and Augustine's maxim crowns the scheme: "what shall be more free than free will, when it shall not be able to serve sin?" (De correptione et gratia ch. 11, Wallis, read directly). State (d) is Part VIII's ground (axiom VIII.3); it is named here because T-IV.2 claims the four states together order the whole biblical economy of the will, and because the root claim's final contrast is eschatological: the closed self is not the will's last word, and the state in which closure is impossible is described by the tradition as more free, not less. [Theological synthesis, HIGH as a report of the loci throughout this section: axioms IV.2, IV.4, III.5, VIII.3.]

## 11. The flood generation: the root displayed, and the grief deferred

Genesis 6:5 gives the root claim its narrative display at civilizational scale: "The LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every intention of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually." The verse is about orientation before it is about acts: intention, thoughts, heart, the governing center of section 3, totalized. A humanity whose inner direction is only evil continually is the closed condition of axiom IV.1 photographed from above, and the chapter's root echo sharpens it (study 02, finding 4): Lamech hopes for nacham, comfort, from itsavon, toil, through Noah (5:29); instead human wickedness brings nacham, regret, and atsav, grief, to God, the same two roots with the subject reversed. The comfort men wanted from their condition arrives only through judgment and remnant. [Literary observation, HIGH for the root repetition; theological synthesis, MEDIUM for the reading of it, per study 02.]

What follows in the text is the datum this Part is charged to present and forbidden to reconcile. God's regret (nacham) over human wickedness is real and moral in object, "grounded in the moral verdict of 6:5," and is not a narrator's loose figure the canon corrects (study 02, finding 1; the construction recurs at 1 Samuel 15 for Saul's appointment). His grief (atsav, in the Hithpael, "to his heart") is real felt pain, in a pattern Scripture repeats (Psalm 78:40; Isaiah 63:10), so that a reading evacuating the affection "must explain away a settled biblical pattern, not one verse" (finding 2). And the two are not set against government: "Regret and decree are not set against each other in this text. Verse 7 makes the regret itself the stated ground of an act of judgment that the wider canon presents as purposeful" (finding 5). Axiom IV.5 receives exactly this: God's regret and grief in Genesis 6:5-7 are grounded in his moral verdict on what humanity had become; the regret is the stated ground of a purposeful act of judgment; regret and decree are not set against each other in the text. [Explicit text, HIGH: axiom IV.5.]

The other pole stands in the same canon and, at 1 Samuel 15, in the same chapter: neither verb asserts ignorance, error, or reversal of character, and the canon's qualification (Numbers 23:19; 1 Samuel 15:29) "denies man-like relenting, lying, and caprice, while affirming regret in the same breath," so that "the text fixes two poles: genuine divine affection in time, and no instability of character or counsel" (finding 3). Part IV prints both poles and stops. The metaphysical reconciliation of genuine affection with immutable counsel is Part V's work, assigned there by the study itself and by the consistency audit (item (viii): the axiom set "carries the poles as data... and does not anywhere assert a reconciliation it does not have"; the impassibility account is named as Part V work and remains unwritten). The two readings that would dissolve the datum from either side, open theism's learning and revising God and the pure-metaphor evacuation, were both declined by the study on textual grounds (sec. 5 of study 02), and this Part declines them by reference. What the flood narrative contributes to Part IV is therefore precise: the root's totalized display in man, and, in God, a grief that is a verdict's grief, issuing in purposeful judgment: evil at this scale is not met by indifference in the God whose communion it offends. The asymmetrical intention of Genesis 50:20, where God intends the deed for good that brothers devised for evil, belongs to the same complex of texts and is argued in Part V (axiom V.1); it is registered here so that Part IV's silence about it is principled rather than inadvertent.

## 12. The legacy formulations, quoted and glossed (audit item (vii))

The consistency audit found one tension in this whole program that is not inside the axiom set but between the axiom set and the corpus: earlier formulations of the root claim "can be read in the unqualified sense the stress test refuted," and its resolution is execution, locus by locus, in this Part. The audit's rule is exact quotation plus gloss with Q1-Q4. The loci are four, all from the Triune Loving Communion systematic theology, v0.1, the corpus's compact statement of the governing thesis.

First locus, the study's abstract: "The proposed universal root of sin is closed self-interest, while individual sins are disordered expressions of that root." Gloss: the sentence is exact as a statement about root (Q1's level), and its own adjective does quiet work: the root is "proposed," and individual sins are "expressions" of it, a relation of derivation from a condition, not a census of motives. Read with Q1, the sentence is this Part's claim. Read as "each individual sin expresses a self-interested motive," it asserts what Leviticus 4-5 refute, and the treatise does not assert it.

Second locus, the canonical thesis: "The Fall explains its corruption and the true nature of sin: closed self-interest." Gloss: the thesis names the fall's corruption and sin's nature at the level of root and orientation. "True nature" is a claim about what sin is at its root (the self closed against communion, Part I's archetype inverted), not about the psychology of each sinner in each act; Q1 fixes the level, Q2 fixes the operative term ("closed"), and Q4 forbids completing the sentence with invented motives where Scripture is silent.

Third locus, the definition: "**Closed self-interest** denotes the creature treating self as the ultimate practical reference point, displacing God and neighbor. Pride, greed, hatred, idolatry, exploitation, and neglect are proposed manifestations." Gloss: the definition survives almost entire, and its own list already contains a mode the unqualified reading cannot process: neglect, which grasps nothing. "Ultimate practical reference point" is an orientation predicate, and it is Q1's level stated in other words. Q2 adds what the definition leaves implicit, that the manifestations run in four modes (greed grasping; hatred and exploitation between grasping and arbitral; neglect inert), and that "interest" in the term covers the negative preference for remaining undisturbed, without which "neglect" sits in the list unexplained. The gloss does not correct the definition; it unfolds it.

Fourth locus, the corpus's own program: "The universal assertion that *every* sin stems from closed self-interest remains to be demonstrated. It must account for culpable ignorance, omissions (James 4:17), action against conscience (Romans 14:23), fear, and apparently altruistic wrongdoing. It would be circular to define closed self-interest simply as whatever makes an action sinful. Distinguish ultimate moral orientation, conscious motivation, culpability, and consequences." Gloss: this locus is the stress-test's charter, written into the corpus before the test existed, and the Part records the fact plainly: the corpus proposed the claim, named its hardest cases (the four classes of the scrutiny item, plus Romans 14:23, answered in sec. 8), forbade the circular rescue, and demanded exactly the distinction (orientation versus conscious motivation) that Q1 enacts. The qualified form is therefore not an external correction imposed on the corpus but the corpus's own program executed. Where the compact statements of the first three loci outrun their qualifications on their face, the fourth locus supplies the qualifications' warrant from inside the same document; the treatise's gloss follows the corpus's own instruction, and the sources stand unaltered as the record, per the work package's standing rule.

## 13. Counter-positions in strongest form

(a) The Pelagian position, as Augustine faced it. In De natura et gratia (415), Pelagius "alleged the possibility of not sinning to be inherent in man; and this he attributed to God's grace, on the ground that God is the author of that nature in which is inseparably inherent this possibility of avoiding sin" (argument as summarized in Schaff's editorial introduction, NPNF 1-05 front matter, SECONDARY). In its strongest form the position is not a denial of grace but a location of it: grace is the endowment of nature itself, and the posse non peccare travels with the nature wherever the nature is propagated, so that the fallen state differs from Adam's in performance, not in capacity. If that is right, sections 9 and 10 of this Part are wrong at their foundation: there is no lost freedom to describe, and state (b) is state (a) with a worse record. Augustine's reply, as the same summary carries it (with Retractationes II.42), defends grace "not indeed as in opposition to nature, but as that which liberates and controls nature": the posse was the first state's gift under a leavable aid, not an inseparable property of nature as now propagated, and the Enchiridion's prose (sec. 9) states what propagation actually delivers, a will victorious over itself, free to sin and not free to do right until freed. The Part adopts Augustine's reply because its textual ground is the stronger: Romans 5's reign of death over those who did not sin like Adam, and Jeremiah 17:9's incurable heart, describe a condition, not a run of bad performances. But the objection is recorded at full strength because the register's discipline requires it, and because the Part's inherited-condition claim is the point at which a Pelagian reader will, rightly, concentrate his fire.

(b) The circularity objection. The legacy corpus states it against itself, and the Part takes it in that unsparing form: "It would be circular to define closed self-interest simply as whatever makes an action sinful" (v0.1, sec. 4, quoted in sec. 12). Pressed fully: if "closed" means no more than "the condition of a self that sins," then the claim "every sin proceeds from the closed self" is a tautology wearing a thesis's clothes, and its survival of the counterexample classes was rigged in advance, since every apparent counterexample can be reclassified as closure by definition. The answer has three members, and the Part states them as its defense, not as a victory lap. First, closure has independent content: it is defined against Part I's archetype as the self taking itself, rather than God and neighbor, as its governing center, a description that could fail to fit (a sin whose orientation was God-ward and neighbor-ward would refute it, and the claim's history shows the description is not infinitely elastic: at the motive level it did fail, on Leviticus 4-5, and the failure was conceded and the claim narrowed under evidence, which is not what tautologies do). Second, the modes are descriptively differentiated and separately evidenced (grasping at the spoil heap, contraction in the courtyard, rest on the Jericho road, arbitration at Carmel), so the claim makes distinct, checkable assertions per class rather than one unfalsifiable gesture. Third, Q4 is a falsification-shaped restraint: it forbids the very move (invent a motive) by which a circular claim would protect itself, and it leaves Uzzah standing in the text as a case the claim declines to explain at the level where explanation would be invention. A claim that publishes its own residue is not defining its way out of trouble.

(c) The flattening objection. A single root, it is said, flattens sin's diversity and insults moral psychology: pride and sloth, cruelty and cowardice, the tyrant and the truant are not one thing, and a theory that calls them one thing has explained the differences away rather than explaining them. The answer is that the unity claimed is a unity of root and orientation, and the plurality claimed alongside it is the tradition's own: Augustine's two loves generate two cities' worth of differentiated life; Luther's incurvature bends even the best gifts and even God himself toward the self, a mechanism with indefinitely many surface forms; and Barth's three forms, pride, sloth, and falsehood, are developed as genuinely distinct shapes of the one broken relation (via Jenson, SECONDARY, as labeled). This Part's four modes are a differentiation of exactly that kind, keyed to the counterexample classes rather than to a speculative psychology: the classes forced the modes, not the reverse. The objection would land against a claim that every sin is pride, or every sin is greed; against a claim that every sin proceeds from one closed orientation in differentiated modes, it mistakes the level of the claim, the same confusion Q1 exists to prevent.

(d) The Uzzah objection, pressed hardest. A root claim that cannot say what was wrong in Uzzah himself, its critic says, explains nothing about the case Scripture bothers to judge most severely: a man struck dead at the ark, and the treatise's answer is a shrug dressed as method ("the order was disordered; the motive is opaque"). Either the claim covers Uzzah or it does not, and if the covering is done by the procession's design rather than by the man, the claim has quietly changed its subject from sinners to systems. The Part answers without moving. The claim's subject was never the auditable interior of each sinner; Q1 fixed that before Uzzah was reached. What the claim asserts about Uzzah's case is specific and checkable: an act of unauthorized handling (Numbers 4:15), inside an order designed by human judgment against the revealed prescription (the cart against the poles), was judged as error; the corporate design is the arbitral mode at the level where the text does disclose judgment, David's procession being arranged "by human judgment in place of the revealed order." What the claim declines is motive attribution, and the declination is principled by Jeremiah 17:9 and by the text's own silence, and it is corroborated from outside the treatise's commitments: the Crossway commentary consulted agrees the act was "not motivated by irreverence" and yet sacrilegious. The alternative to the residue is not a stronger claim; it is a fabricated psychology for a dead man, which this treatise will not print. The residue is therefore left standing in the open, as the stress-test left it, and the objection is answered by the claim's stated scope rather than denied.

(e) The equivocation objection against Q2. Section 5's own concession is the strongest form of this objection: if "interest" covers both seizing an advantage and preferring not to be disturbed, it covers everything a self can do or omit, and a term that covers everything distinguishes nothing. The Part's answer is the one the stress-test gave, with its confidence rating left where the note put it. The claim does not rest on "interest"; it rests on "closed," defined by a determinate contrary: opened by love toward God and neighbor. A self can be described, in any given act, as opened or as not opened, and the description can be wrong in particular cases, which is what makes it a description. "Interest" survives in the formulation because it is the corpus's word and because its negative sense, the preference for remaining undisturbed, names something real in the omission cases (the road crossed, the schedule kept, the purity preserved); but where the two words pull apart, closure governs, and the formulation says so. The objection is the reason this class stands at MEDIUM rather than HIGH, and the Part regards that rating as the honest price of the absorption, not as a defect to be argued away.
