## 1. The question of this Part and its theses

Part II stated God's one good counsel and the covenantal form in which the tradition states it toward the Mediator. Part IV stated the fall: the root from which evil proceeds in creatures, and the fault that owns it. Between those two statements stands the question this Part exists to answer, in the Prolegomena's words: how can the good decree govern evil without authoring it, excusing it, or explaining it on demand? The three negations are the question's shape. A theodicy that makes God the author of sin has lost the archetype of Part I, for whom evil is the contradiction of his own life. A theodicy that excuses the wicked has lost Part IV, in which the fall is man's own fault and guilt is not dissolved by providence. A theodicy that explains every evil on demand has lost the boundary Deuteronomy sets, and will be refuted by the first sufferer who asks it a question it cannot answer. This Part argues that the treatise's premises sustain a position that refuses all three losses, and it marks, at each step, exactly how much is claimed and at what calibration.

This Part also carries work three earlier loci expressly deferred to it. Study 02 fixed the two poles of divine affection as textual data (real grief; no instability of character or counsel) and assigned their metaphysical reconciliation here. Part I stated the poles and stopped (thesis T-I.2), and Part IV displayed the flood generation's root and the grief it caused, and stopped ("the grief deferred," Part IV, sec. 11). Section 3 of this Part performs the reconciliation. Second, the consistency audit fixed the felix culpa boundary as a joint position (audit item (iii), axiom V.5); section 5 states it in full, with the modal analysis that keeps its three members from collapsing into one another. Third, the governing premise of Romans 8:28 (axiom II.3) was gated: the universal claim that no suffering is actually gratuitous stands at MEDIUM, resting on the working paper's wider theodicy premises (T1-T3) and not on that verse alone (axiom V.4); section 5 performs that gating argument and states the measure of the claim. A fourth, smaller debt is paid in section 6: the Noahic research note flagged the relation of 2 Peter 3:7's reservation to Genesis 9:11 as owing a short treatment here.

The Part's theses, as the derivation map states them, are four.

**T-V.1.** There is no bare permission: God governs wicked acts by his secret counsel without authoring their evil, the intentions in the one act being asymmetrical. Depends on axioms V.1, V.2, II.1, I.3.

**T-V.2.** The fall stands under the ordaining providence of God and rests wholly on man's fault. Depends on axioms V.3, III.5.

**T-V.3.** The epistemic boundary disciplines the evidential problem of evil without demonstrating a reason for any particular case, and it neither silences lament nor excuses inaction. Depends on axioms PRO.1, PRO.2, V.4, V.6, X.3.

**T-V.4.** Felix culpa is affirmed only in the decree-relative sense: the fall was necessary given the redemptive decree God actually made, and the consummation is nowhere said to have required it. Depends on axioms V.5, II.4.

The calibrations that govern the whole Part are stated once, here. The textual findings the Part builds on (the asymmetrical constructions of Genesis 50:20; the two poles of Genesis 6 and 1 Samuel 15; the boundary's grammar; RP's variant-independence and its refusal of event-by-event indispensability) stand at HIGH, on Phase 1 findings so tagged. The reconciliation of section 3 stands at Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH, for reasons argued at its close. ## 2. The grammar of the decree toward evil

### 2.1 Asymmetrical intention: the canonical pattern

The treatise's account of how the decree stands toward evil begins where the canon itself gives the pattern in a single sentence. Genesis 50:20 predicates one verb, chashav (to devise, plan, intend), of two subjects within one historical complex, and builds the two predications differently (study 03, findings 1-2). The brothers' clause takes evil as the accusative content of the devising and Joseph as its target: chashavtem alay ra'ah, "you devised evil against me." God's clause takes the deed itself as its accusative, by the suffix on chashavah, "he intended it," and gives the intention a destination instead of a target: letovah, "for good," which the lema'an clause specifying the keeping alive of a numerous people (am-rav) shows to be concrete preservation, not a vague favorable outcome (finding 4). BDB's own arrangement files the verse under two distinct constructions, one for each clause. The grammar therefore carries, before any systematic theology is added, the distinction the theodicy needs: "the same act is evil in the intending of the agents who performed it and good in the intending of God who directed it to its end, and the sentence keeps the two from collapsing into each other. Joseph nowhere redescribes the brothers' act as good, and nowhere makes God the deviser of the evil" (finding 3). [Explicit text for the constructions, HIGH; Necessary inference for the asymmetry, HIGH.]

Axiom V.1 states the pattern: in one and the same historical complex, human agents may devise evil against their brother while God intends the deed itself for good; God's intention has the deed as its object and good as its end, never the evil as evil. Two scope disciplines bind the axiom's use, both from the study itself. First, the verse warrants the claim for the Joseph complex; the universal premise, that God stands in this relation to events generally, is not smuggled out of this verse but is supplied by Ephesians 1:11 and Romans 8:28-30 and by Part II's synthesis (study 03, finding 6; audit item (x)). Second, the asymmetry is not a distinction between a strong divine act and a weak divine acquiescence: the verb is the same verb, an intention verb in BDB's arrangement, predicated of God without dilution. What differs between the clauses is not the reality of the intending but its object and end. The reckoning reading pressed in the study's competing readings (God's chashav as retrospective assessment, as at Genesis 15:6) was declined on construction grounds, and its guard retained: no reading of this verse may make God the deviser of the evil (study 03, sec. 5.1). The pattern Part V generalizes is therefore exact: one event, two intentions, differing in object (the evil devised; the deed taken up) and in end (harm against a brother; the preservation of a people), with the evil's evilness located wholly in the intention that devised it.

The crucifixion stands in the canon as the pattern's supreme instance, and the working paper's premise P3 rests on it: Acts 2:23 joins the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God to the lawless hands that crucified, and Acts 4:27-28 gathers Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles, and the peoples of Israel as doing "whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place," bouleh and proorizo in tandem, the same pairing as Ephesians 1:11 (study 06, secs. 3.2 and 3.5). The worst act in history is the one the canon most explicitly places inside the decree, and it places it there without transferring its guilt: the men who did it are indicted in the same speeches that announce the counsel (Acts 2:23, "you crucified and killed"; 3:13-15). Asymmetrical intention is not a theory the treatise imposes on reluctant texts; it is the shape of the canon's two most explicit decree-and-evil statements.

### 2.2 Against bare permission: Calvin, Institutes 1.18

If Genesis 50:20 gives the pattern, Calvin's eighteenth chapter of the first book gives the Part's charter for how the decree's relation to wicked acts may and may not be described (axiom V.2; the note on Augustine and Calvin, secs. 3.1-3.4; Beveridge translation, read directly). The distinction Calvin attacks is the one a certain kind of theodicy reaches for instinctively: God does not do the wicked act; he permits it. Calvin's verdict on that distinction, used to insulate God from the government of wicked acts, is that it is invented: "Hence a distinction has been invented between doing and permitting because to many it seemed altogether inexplicable how Satan and all the wicked are so under the hand and authority of God, that he directs their malice to whatever end he pleases, and employs their iniquities to execute his Judgments." Against the evasion he sets God's own self-declaration: "He himself, however, openly declaring that he does this, repudiates the evasion. That men do nothing save at the secret instigation of God, and do not discuss and deliberate on any thing but what he has previously decreed with himself and brings to pass by his secret direction, is proved by numberless clear passages of Scripture." The chapter's two narrative exhibits fix the claim. In Job 1, Satan "appears in the presence of God to receive his orders" and "cannot attempt anything without the will of God"; the seeming permission is resolved by Job's own confession, "The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away," from which Calvin infers "that God was the author of that trial of which Satan and wicked robbers were merely the instruments." At Ahab's blinding (1 Kings 22): "If the blinding and infatuation of Ahab is a Judgment from God, the fiction of bare permission is at an end; for it would be ridiculous for a judge only to permit, and not also to decree, what he wishes to be done at the very time that he commits the execution of it to his ministers." The section closes with the watch-tower sentence, which Part V adopts as the picture its doctrine excludes: "it is perfectly clear that it is the merest trifling to substitute a bare permission for the providence of God, as if he sat in a watch-tower waiting for fortuitous events, his Judgments meanwhile depending on the will of man."

Three precisions keep the charter from being overread, all from the same chapter. First, what Calvin rejects is permission as insulation, permission as the claim that the act lies outside God's willing government; the word itself, subordinately used, survives in his own sentence: Satan "succeeds only in so far as he is permitted" (1.18.2), where the permission is itself an act of the governing will, a measured allowance by the hand that could restrain and sometimes does. The treatise's discipline is therefore verbal as well as doctrinal: "permission" may be used only where it names a mode of the decree's government, never where it names an absence of government. Second, the hardening texts are, for Calvin, judgments inflicted, not abandonments permitted: the blindness and infatuation "are inflicted by the just Judgment of God," and the chapter's sum is that "the will of God is said to be the cause of all things," so that God "not only exerts his power in the elect, who are guided by the Holy Spirit, but also forces the reprobate to do him service" (1.18.2). Third, and most important for the architecture: Calvin does not resolve the government of evil by dividing God's will against itself. Section 4 below takes up his operative distinctions; the government claim of this section stands on its own: the decree's domain over wicked acts is total, and its mode is counsel, not spectatorship.

### 2.3 One will, two manners; will is not precept; differing reasons

The objection Calvin himself states at 1.18.3 is the one Part V must answer in his terms before it answers it in its own: "if nothing happens without the will of God, he must have two contrary wills, decreeing by a secret counsel what he has openly forbidden in his law." His answer is axiom I.3's basis, and Part I has already received it: "the will of God is not at variance with itself. It undergoes no change. He makes no pretence of not willing what he wills, but while in himself the will is one and undivided, to us it appears manifold, because, from the feebleness of our intellect, we cannot comprehend how, though after a different manner, he wills and wills not the very same thing." The distinction the treatise operates is therefore not between two wills but between the one will as secret counsel and as revealed precept, a distinction of manner and of our apprehension (axiom I.3; Turretin's form of the distinction, will of decree and will of precept with Deuteronomy 29:29 among its standard proofs, is the register's systematic frame, consulted via mediation). God wills the event in counsel; he forbids the act in precept; the event and the act are one history under two relations to the one will, and neither relation is a fiction.

Section 1.18.4 draws the distinction's ethical edge, and Part V prints it as a standing rule: "Here will is improperly confounded with precept, though it is obvious, from innumerable examples, that there is the greatest difference between them. ... Thus we must hold, that while by means of the wicked God performs what he had secretly decreed, they are not excusable as if they were obeying his precept, which of set purpose they violate according to their lust." The wicked man who fulfills the counsel does not obey God; he violates the precept, and his guilt is measured by the precept he knew, not by the counsel he could not know. Calvin adopts Augustine's resolution of the one-act problem, and states it as the section's sum: in the one act (his example is the delivering up of the Son, willed by the Father, by Christ, and by Judas), God is just and man guilty "just because in the one act which they did, the reasons for which they did it are different." [The Augustinian sentence reaches this project through Calvin's quotation; the letter number in Calvin's citation varies by edition and is flagged, not resolved (Augustine and Calvin note, sec. 3.4).] Differing reasons is the ethical form of asymmetrical intention: Genesis 50:20 states it grammatically for intention; Calvin states it morally for culpability. The act is one; the intending of it is not one; and judgment follows the intending proper to each agent.

Axiom V.3 joins this to the fall itself, and T-V.2 states the thesis: "Man falls according as God's providence ordains, but he falls by his own fault" (Institutes 3.23.8, Battles as quoted; the Beveridge parallel, read directly: "Man therefore falls, divine providence so ordaining, but he falls by his own fault"; Latin tag suo vitio cadit, through Shedd). The sentence's two halves are not a compromise between two pressures; each is the whole truth at its own level. At the level of counsel, the fall stands inside the ordination, as section 5 argues with the modal distinctions kept intact. At the level of the agent, the fall rests wholly on man's fault: Adam stood in posse peccare et posse non peccare, upright, with a will pliable in either direction and a leavable aid, and "he fell by his own will" (axiom III.5; Institutes 1.15.8). Calvin's own continuation of 3.23.8 makes the same joint from Genesis 1:31: God had declared all he made very good; "man's own wickedness corrupted the pure nature which he had received from God," so depravity is argued out of creation by creation's verdict (axiom III.4). Providence ordains; man falls by his fault; and the treatise knows no third level at which the two halves could be traded against each other.

### 2.4 The decree's execution is not contingent at its center

One consequence of the grammar belongs here, at the joint the register marks between this Part and Part VII. A decree that governs evil asymmetrically and ordains the fall under man's fault would still leave redemption's success exposed if the Mediator's obedience were a contingent outcome the decree merely foresaw. The treatise's decree treatment requires the stronger claim, and its register base states it: the Mediator's success is guaranteed, not merely foreseen. The conciliar ground is Constantinople III's confession that Christ's human will follows the divine, "not as resisting and reluctant, but rather as subject to it," the wills not contrary (Definition of Faith, Session XVIII, read directly in the Schaff translation; the council's own anchor in the Definition is John 6:38). Turretin's loci ground the Son's sinlessness in the constitution of his person and endowment (Thirteenth Topic, VII.xiv on the two wills; V, XI-XIII on the assumed nature, the Spirit without measure, and graces permanent and fixed; Giger/Dennison, read directly), and Shedd's defense gives the joint its classical Reformed form: the person was characterized by non posse peccare, and the finite will never antagonizes the infinite (Dogmatic Theology, vol. 2, read directly in reproduction). The calibration is Part VII's printed one and is observed here: impeccability is Theological synthesis, MEDIUM as an inference from Hebrews 4:15 alone, HIGH on the treatise's full premise set, and it is neither a conciliar definition nor a confessional article (audit item (i)); the council defines no modal inability, and Hodge's dissent inside the tradition is recorded, not suppressed (axiom X.2). For Part V the point is narrower than the doctrine: the same counsel that governs the brothers' malice and Judas's betrayal also constitutes the obedience by which those evils are answered, so that the decree does not govern evil from outside redemption's story while hoping the story succeeds. Its center is as ordained as its circumference.

## 3. The reconciliation owed: divine grief and immutable counsel

### 3.1 The poles, as the texts fix them

Study 02's assignment to this Part was exact, and its discipline governs the attempt: both poles are textual, not imported, and the reconciliation may not be purchased by discounting either. The first pole: God's regret and grief over human wickedness are real. Nacham in Genesis 6:6-7, in the narrator's report and then in God's own speech ("for I regret that I have made them"), "expresses sorrow and regret over what humanity had become, grounded in the moral verdict of 6:5," and the construction is one the canon repeats, at 1 Samuel 15:11 and 15:35 for Saul's appointment, so that it cannot be quarantined as one narrator's loose figure (finding 1). Atsav in the Hithpael, intensified by el-libbo, "to his heart," predicates felt pain of God, in a pattern Scripture sustains: "How often they rebelled against him in the wilderness and grieved him in the desert" (Psalm 78:40); "they rebelled and grieved his Holy Spirit" (Isaiah 63:10). [Explicit text, HIGH, both findings.] The second pole: neither verb asserts ignorance, error, or reversal of character or counsel. Nacham is not shuv, the turning-from-sin verb; of God it never carries confession of error; its range includes pity toward sufferers and relenting toward the penitent, neither of which implies a prior mistake. And the canon qualifies its own usage in terms: "God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind (yinnachem)" (Numbers 23:19); "the Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret, for he is not a man, that he should have regret (lehinnachem)" (1 Samuel 15:29). [Canonical synthesis, HIGH for the two poles as textual data (study 02, finding 3).]

First Samuel 15 is the reconciliation's home ground precisely because it refuses to distribute the poles between distant books, where a harmonizer could assign one to an early stratum and the other to a later correction. In one chapter, about one appointment, the text says "I regret that I have made Saul king" (15:11), records Samuel's grief and the LORD's regret again at the chapter's end (15:35), and between them states, in Samuel's rebuke of Saul, that God will not have regret as a man has it (15:29). The chapter is not confused about what it is doing. The denial carries its qualification inside itself: he is not a man, that he should have regret. What is denied is regret in the human manner, and the chapter has just finished telling us what the human manner includes: Saul's manner, pretense, excuse, and self-deception in the verses surrounding (15:13-23), and behind the formula the standard failures Numbers 23:19 names, lying and caprice and the change of mind that comes from learning what one did not know. What is affirmed in the same breath is regret simpliciter, predicated of God twice. The canon therefore does not leave the reconciliation to be constructed out of two texts facing each other across the canon; it performs the qualification inside the affirmation. Any account of the poles must begin there: the denial is comparative in form, and its comparandum is man.

Study 02 adds the joint Part IV displayed: "Regret and decree are not set against each other in this text. Verse 7 makes the regret itself the stated ground of an act of judgment that the wider canon presents as purposeful" (finding 5; axiom IV.5). The grief of Genesis 6 is not a sentiment that interrupts the government and is then overridden by it; it is the government's stated motive for the flood. Grief and counsel are not competitors in the text's own presentation. Something in the reconciliation must preserve that: whatever God's grief is, it is the kind of thing that can ground a decree's execution without remainder or embarrassment.

### 3.2 What the denial excludes, and what it leaves standing

The reconciliation proceeds by asking, with the texts, what exactly the second pole negates. Four negations can be read off the qualifications themselves and out of nacham's range. (1) **Ignorance and surprise.** The regret of Genesis 6 responds to a moral state evaluated (6:5), in a narrative where the judgment's trajectory is already announced (6:3, the hundred and twenty years; 6:7, 13); nothing in either Genesis 6 or 1 Samuel 15 presents God as acting on new information, and Numbers 23:19's context is precisely a promised word that cannot be overtaken by events. Regret born of misprediction is man's regret; it is excluded. (2) **Error and self-correction.** Nacham of God never confesses a mistake; God's regret over Saul's kingship and over man's creation is sorrow over what the creature became under a good appointment, not acknowledgment that the appointment was a blunder (study 02, sec. 3.1). (3) **Caprice and instability of character.** The Glory of Israel does not lie: his griefs and relentings are not moods succeeding one another in a mutable temperament but the steady expressions of a character that does not alter, holiness answering wickedness and mercy answering repentance, predictably, because he is who he is. Jeremiah 18:7-10 states the policy as a standing rule, which is the opposite of caprice. (4) **Reversal of counsel.** The decree that announced Saul's kingdom also contained, in its own order, the judgment on his disobedience; the regret announces no change in what God had eternally purposed concerning the history, and Genesis 6's regret issues in the flood the narrative presents as purposeful, not in an abandoned creation project (finding 5).

What the denial leaves standing is everything the first pole asserts: real sorrow, real felt grief, real relenting toward the penitent (Exodus 32:14; Amos 7:3, 6; Jonah 3:10, all within nacham's attested range), and a real relation between God's affections and the temporal states of his creatures, such that the same God is truly said to grieve at one point of the history and to relent at another, and truly said never to repent as man repents. The two poles are therefore not two assertions competing for one predicate. They are assertions at different joints: the affirmations predicate affections of God toward temporal objects; the denials predicate the constancy of his character and counsel in having them. The contradiction appears only if the affections are assumed to be, as they are in us, episodes that befall a subject who did not know they were coming and is other than he was because they came. That assumption is the human manner of having affections; it is what "he is not a man" excludes. The reconciliation is the disciplined refusal to import it.

### 3.3 Impassibility, rightly stated

The classical doctrine names the excluded assumption. Impassibility, as study 02 states it and Part I adopts the statement (T-I.2), "does not require this evacuation; it denies involuntary, destabilizing passion, not genuine affection." The distinction is between passion as pathos in the strict sense, an affection suffered involuntarily, imposed from outside on a subject who is acted upon, destabilized, and carried where he did not choose to go, and affection as the free, knowing, constant expression of a character toward its objects. Human grief includes the first: it ambushes us, it informs us of losses we did not foresee, it can unmake our purposes. Nothing in Genesis 6 requires God's grief to include any of that, and the second pole excludes each element in turn (sec. 3.2). What remains is grief without pathos in the excluded sense: sorrow that is fully known, freely God's own, perfectly consonant with his settled counsel, and no less real for being unconquered by surprise. A grief that cannot destabilize is not thereby a pretend grief, unless reality in an affection is measured by its power to overwhelm, which is a creaturely measure.

Positively, the reconciliation states the relation of the affections to the counsel as manifestation in sequence. God's character and counsel are one and eternal (axiom I.3); the history in which that counsel unfolds is sequential, and its objects change: man is made upright, man becomes only evil continually, Saul is appointed, Saul rebels, Nineveh repents. The eternal holiness that wills man's good encounters, in time, objects standing in different relations to it, and its one unchanging moral posture is expressed toward those objects in the affections the canon names: delight in the upright work, grief at the corruption, relenting at the repentance, wrath at the impenitent. The sequence is located in the objects and in the expressions, not in a character revised by its own experiences. An unchanging verdict on evil, met by a world that falls into evil, is grief; the same verdict, met by repentance, is relenting; and neither encounter teaches God anything or costs his counsel anything. This is why Genesis 6 can make the regret the ground of the judgment (sec. 3.1): the grief is the verdict of 6:5 in its affective register, and the flood is that same verdict in its executive register, and a God whose grief were a change of mind could not ground a purposeful judgment in it without remainder.

Augustine's pair of chapters, which Calvin adopts at the center of this question, states the will-side of the same joint (Enchiridion 100-101, as quoted in Institutes 1.18.3; register Part V, entry 2: CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION, through Calvin's quotations): the things God rightly wills, he accomplishes by the evil wills of bad men; and, on Psalm 111:2, that is not done without his will which is done contrary to his will, "because it could not be done if he did not permit; nor does he permit it unwillingly, but willingly." Calvin quotes the chapters against the two-contrary-wills objection, and their bearing here is the affective corollary: God's willing engagement with a history containing evil is not unwilling endurance of it. The God who grieves is not a God overtaken by the history he grieves over; he wills the history, griefs included in its economy, and the grief is his genuine response inside a government that was never out of his hand. Section 2.2's discipline governs the word "permit" in Augustine's sentence exactly as in Calvin's: willingly, under counsel, or not at all.

### 3.4 The reconciliation stated, with its tag

The reconciliation the three deferred loci required can now be stated in one paragraph, each member carrying its basis.

**God's regret and grief are genuine affections of the immutable God toward the temporal states of his creatures: the affective expression, within the unfolding of his one eternal counsel, of a character that does not change, toward objects that do. They assert no ignorance, for their ground is a moral verdict on states God eternally knew and in part decreed; no error, for they sorrow over the creature's defection from a good appointment, not over the appointment; no caprice, for they follow the standing policy of his holiness and mercy; and no reversal of counsel, for they are themselves the counsel's stated motives in its execution (Genesis 6:7). The canon's denial of divine regret is comparative in form (not as man, Numbers 23:19; 1 Samuel 15:29) and excludes the human manner of regretting, ignorance, mistake, and instability, while the canon's affirmations, in the same chapter, predicate regret of God without embarrassment. Impassibility guards the joint: it denies that God's affections are involuntary and destabilizing passions suffered from without, not that they are genuine affections freely and constantly his own.**

**Tag: Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH.** The rating is argued, not assumed. The synthesis's load-bearing joint, the comparative form of the canonical denial, is textual and stands at HIGH; its frame, the one undivided will apprehended under different manners, is axiom I.3, HIGH within the treatise's confessional frame on Calvin read directly. The synthesis does not reach HIGH outright for two named reasons. First, the articulation of the eternal will's temporal manifestation (sec. 3.3) is the treatise's construction, an inference from the poles and the frame, not a finding any single study delivered. Second, the broader-corpus engagement study 02 assigned to this treatment is only partly discharged: Augustine is present through the chapters Calvin quotes (entry 2), and Aquinas on the divine will, providence, and mercy, named in the register for exactly this joint, stands at NOT YET CONSULTED (entry 3). The Part therefore performs the reconciliation the treatise's own premises support, at MEDIUM-HIGH, and records that its scholastic pressure-testing against Aquinas's account (God as cause of the act and not of the sin; mercy without passion) awaits the consultation the register schedules. It also declines the two resolutions the studies declined before it: open theism's mutable learner, refuted by the second pole (sec. 7.1), and the pure-metaphor evacuation, refuted by the first (sec. 7.2). A reconciliation that deletes a pole is not a reconciliation but a surrender, and this Part was assigned both poles as data.

## 4. The boundary of explanation

### 4.1 What Deuteronomy 29:29 establishes, and what it does not

The treatise's theodicy has a boundary stone, and this Part sets it where study 04's exegesis puts it, not where homiletic habit puts it. The verse, in its own grammar: "The hidden things [belong] to the LORD our God, and the revealed things to us and to our children forever, in order to do all the words of this law" (study 04, sec. 1). Neither participle carries an object: the verse specifies belonging, not content (finding 2) [Explicit text, HIGH]. Its primary contextual referents are more specific than a general doctrine of mystery: on the hidden side, the concealed covenant treachery of the man who blesses himself in his heart (29:18-21) and its judgment, which God reserves to himself; on the revealed side, the law, named by the verse's own purpose clause (finding 3). The extension to God's unrevealed counsel and future dealings is legitimate, because the chapter itself begins it (29:22-28, where the devastated land's observers are answered at the level of covenant cause and nothing more), but it remains an extension (finding 4) [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH]. Axiom PRO.1 carries the verse at Explicit text, HIGH: a real and permanent division between a domain reserved to the LORD and a domain revealed and entrusted forever, given for obedience.

From that structure, study 04 derives the epistemic rule the treatise operates (finding 6; axiom PRO.2): given that a real domain of things bearing on God's governance is legitimately withheld, the inference from "no morally sufficient reason for this evil is visible to us" to "there is no such reason" fails on the verse's own terms; and by the same silence about content, the verse supplies no positive evidence about any particular case. The rule is a valid consequence of the verse's structure, not a statement of the verse [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM]. Two disciplines, both load-bearing in this Part, follow immediately. First (finding 5; axiom X.3): Deuteronomy 29:29 does not address any particular evil, and Part V must not cite it as though it did. The verse is never this Part's answer to a sufferer's question about a named event; it marks where answers of that kind are not given, which is a different service. Second, the confessional use confirms the discipline: the Westminster Confession cites the verse at 3.8, with Romans 9:20 and 11:33, for the manner of handling predestination, "special prudence and care" by men attending to the revealed will, and not as supplying the locus's matter (study 04, sec. 6.3; register Part V, entry 1, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION). The verse governs how the decree is handled; it is not itself a doctrine of the decree, and a theodicy that quotes it as one has already misused it.

Study 04 registers one further precision this Part must state precisely, since its claim is a bounded and not an absolute hiddenness: the reserved domain is not impermeable to God's own disclosure. The same root galah that names the revealed things names God's self-revealing (1 Samuel 3:21), and Amos 3:7 states the prophetic qualification: the Lord GOD does nothing without revealing his secret (sod) to his servants the prophets. The boundary is therefore not a wall behind which God is unknowable in principle; it is an assignment of domains by the God who reveals what he reveals, and the hiddenness it protects is the hiddenness of what he has not revealed, which creatures may not annex by inference. Lament, section 4.2 will show, speaks to God from inside the revealed domain about the hidden one; it does not pretend to have crossed.

### 4.2 The guardrail carried where it belongs: lament

The working paper's rule, that the appeal to the boundary must never silence lament, is sustained by study 04 for action and expressly not sourced from Deuteronomy 29:29 for lament (finding 7; axiom V.6). Part V therefore carries the guardrail on its true texts, the Psalms of lament and Job, and states what those texts actually establish.

The Psalter institutionalizes protest inside faith. The lament psalms address God with the complaint the boundary verse is sometimes used to forbid: "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever?" (Psalm 13:1); "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1), words the crucified Lord takes as his own; the communal lament of Psalm 44 protests innocence while confessing the covenant ("All this has come upon us, though we have not forgotten you," 44:17) and demands, "Awake! Why are you sleeping, O Lord?" (44:23); and Psalm 88 ends in darkness without a turn to praise, and stands in the canon unrebuked. These are revealed things: God has given his people the words with which to accuse the hiddenness, which means the hiddenness may not be used to take the words away. A theodicy whose boundary silences the sufferer's address to God has made the boundary do the one thing its own canon forbids.

Job carries the guardrail to its sharpest point, because Job is the canon's own theodicy controversy and its verdict falls against the explainers. The friends offer exactly what a boundary-less system offers: a complete account in which every suffering is legible and every case decidable from the revealed principles as they apply them. Job refuses the account and insists on addressing God. At the end, God's verdict distinguishes the speeches: "My anger burns against you... for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7). The protester is vindicated; the tidy theodicy is rebuked. The treatise reads that verdict as canonical instruction for its own procedure: the boundary of section 4.1 exists to mark the place where explanation stops and address continues, and any deployment of it that ends the sufferer's speech rather than relocating it has inverted the book of Job. The pastoral half of axiom V.6 is therefore not decoration on the system. Lament is the revealed domain's own provision for living at the boundary, and Part IX's ethics will inherit the rule: in the presence of particular evil, the treatise's first speech is lament and action, not explanation.

The action half of the guardrail stands on Deuteronomy itself (finding 7) [Necessary inference, HIGH]: the revealed domain is entrusted "in order to do," and the chapter's order requires Israel to purge revealed evil from its midst; an appeal to the hidden things that excused known injustice would contradict the boundary's purpose clause. The boundary assigns responsibility rather than removing it. Between the two halves the whole of thesis T-V.3's guardrail claim stands: the boundary neither silences lament nor excuses inaction, and each half is carried on its own texts.

### 4.3 The dreadful decree, quoted in its paragraph

No text in this Part is more liable to misuse than Calvin's famous sentence at Institutes 3.23.7, and the misuse is always the same: the phrase torn from its paragraph and carried about as a freestanding label, usually as a label for reprobation alone. The research note's discipline governs the quotation here (Augustine and Calvin note, sec. 3.5; register Part V, entry 8, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION): the sentence is quoted with its paragraph, and the print-Battles check remains open.

The paragraph answers an objection. The objectors deny that Scripture anywhere says in distinct terms that God decreed Adam's fall: on their account God made his noblest creature with no special purpose for him beyond treating him according to his desert, leaving him architect of his own fortune; Calvin replies that predestination is manifest in Adam's posterity whether they allow it or not (context in Beveridge, read directly). Then the sentence itself, in the Battles translation as carried in the note, quoted here only up to the point the note verifies and deliberately not beyond it: "Again I ask: whence does it happen that Adam's fall irremediably involved so many peoples, together with their infant offspring, in eternal death unless because it so pleased God? Here their tongues, otherwise so loquacious, must become mute. The decree is dreadful indeed, I confess." The paragraph continues, in argument, that no one can deny God foreknew man's end before he created him, and that this foreknowledge itself hangs on his ordination; that complaint against foreknowledge is rash, and if any complaint stands it applies to predestination; and that it is not absurd to say God not only foresaw the fall and the ruin of Adam's descendants but meted it out according to his own decision. This Part paraphrases that continuation rather than quoting it, for the reason the note flags: one clause in one web reproduction of the Battles text is garbled at exactly this point, its syntax broken where the verified sense, confirmed by the agreeing reproduction, is that God foreknew because he so ordained by his decree; the treatise's rule is to repair the clause from a print Battles before quoting it as text (audit, sec. 4, item 12), and the garbled wording is not reproduced here. The argument of the continuation is used; its wording is not quoted.

Three things the paragraph, so quoted, establishes for the treatise. First, its referent: the decree Calvin calls dreadful is the decree as it encompasses Adam's fall and his posterity's ruin, within a chapter (3.23) refuting objections to election and reprobation; it is not a label for reprobation alone, and Part V never cites it as one. Second, its semantics: horribile is awe-full, not vile. The Latin stands confirmed in two sources: "Decretum quidem horribile, fateor"; the French of 1561 carries the same register, a decree that ought to make us tremble; and McNeill's note in the Battles edition glosses Calvin as "awestruck but unrelenting" in the declaration (fn 17, as quoted in Reymond; mediation labeled). Calvin is not confessing that the decree is monstrous; he is confessing that it is dreadful to contemplate and declining to soften it. Third, its location in the argument of this Part: 3.23.7 is the point at which the boundary of section 4.1 and the government of section 2 meet, and Calvin's paragraph models their joint handling. He asserts the ordination without supplying its reason ("why he so judged is hidden from us," 3.23.8), and he locates the fall's fault wholly in man (sec. 2.3 above). The dread is the honest affective register of holding both; a theodicy that feels no dread here has probably dropped one of them.

## 5. The measure of the claim

### 5.1 The governing premise and its limits

Romans 8:28-30 supplies the treatise's governing premise RP (axiom II.3), and study 05 fixed both its strength and its fences. The strength: RP does not depend on the textual variant. The shorter reading (without ho theos) is most probably original [Textual verdict, MEDIUM-HIGH], and on every attested reading the beneficiaries, the comprehensiveness of panta, and the telos are identical; only the grammatical location of the agency varies, and on the least explicit construal the agency is carried by the immediate context, where God is subject of every verb in 8:29-30, by kata prothesin, and by Ephesians 1:11's tou ta panta energountos (finding 5) [Necessary inference, HIGH]. The premise itself: for those who love God, those called according to his purpose, all things are worked together toward the good, the good defined forward and Christologically as conformity to the image of the Son and glorification (findings 1-3, 7) [Explicit text, HIGH]. The premise is spoken into groaning: its nearest contextual content is the sufferings and futility of 8:17-27, and the good is eschatological, not temporal relief.

The fences are as much a part of the premise as the premise. The beneficiaries are restricted to the called; the text supplies no warrant for universalizing its outcome (finding 1). Ephesians 1:11, RP's corroborant, supplies the domain of God's working and not its mode: it states no per-event benevolent telos, no eis agathon, and no reprobation (study 06, finding 5; axiom II.2). And finding 6 draws the fence this Part's central claim must respect: "On no reading does eis agathon assert that each particular evil was necessary as an instrument, or that each event is itself good; the working is toward an end, and the moral character of the events is untouched by it." Axiom II.4 states the negative as a standing rule: no text in the anchor set asserts event-by-event indispensability.

### 5.2 Apparent and actual gratuitousness; the gating argument

The working paper's definitions govern the terms. Apparently gratuitous suffering is suffering whose morally sufficient divine purpose is not discernible to a finite observer; actually gratuitous suffering is suffering that lacks any morally sufficient purpose. The treatise's claim distinguishes them absolutely: apparent gratuitousness is conceded, extensively and without embarrassment, since on the boundary of section 4 the reasons for particular evils are precisely what the hidden domain contains; actual gratuitousness is denied, and the denial is a synthesis with named premises and a named rating (axiom V.4), not a deliverance of Romans 8:28.

The gating argument is the working paper's derivation (6.13), restated here at its calibrations. **T1:** God is perfectly good, holy, just, and wise in his governance (revealed and synthesized; the archetype of Part I and the decree texts of Part II). **T2:** every event in the history falls within God's comprehensive providential governance (Ephesians 1:11's domain, axiom II.1; Genesis 50:20 as the pattern's instance, not as itself a universal quantification). **T3**, the explicit bridge premise: perfectly good and wise divine governance of an evil or suffering event entails a morally sufficient justification for its inclusion in the history. T3 is a moral-theological commitment of the model, not a definition of sovereignty smuggled in as one; the working paper flags it as the premise an interlocutor may contest, and Part X's register carries it as such. From T1-T3 it follows, for any evil event, that it is not actually gratuitous; RP (T4, in the working paper's numbering) then positively specifies the promised redemptive good for the called, and must not be expanded into an identical promise of glorification to every human being. The synthesis's confidence is therefore MEDIUM [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM], and the rating decomposes honestly: T2's domain claim stands at HIGH on study 06; T1 is the treatise's confessional frame; the MEDIUM is carried by T3, a bridge premise asserted on the archetype's logic (a perfectly good and wise governor does not include, in a history he wholly governs, suffering that serves nothing) rather than demonstrated from any single text, and by the step from RP's restricted beneficiaries to the universal scope of the denial. Romans 8:28 alone proves neither T3 nor the universal scope, and this Part does not cite it as though it did (study 05, finding 6).

What the synthesis does and does not buy must be stated with equal plainness, because the evidential problem of evil lives in the gap. It buys a framework claim: within the treatise's premises, there is no actually gratuitous suffering, and the inference from any case's apparent gratuitousness to its actual gratuitousness is blocked twice, by the boundary (PRO.2: invisibility of a reason does not entail its nonexistence) and by the synthesis (T1-T3: a reason exists). It does not buy a theodicy of instances: it does not identify the reason for any particular child's suffering, atrocity, or disaster; it does not prove that the reason could not have been achieved at less cost; it does not demonstrate that this history is uniquely optimal; and it is not, addressed to a sufferer, an answer of the kind lament asks for (sec. 4.2). The working paper's evidential section (6.4) accepts exactly this inventory as the Part's obligation, and section 7.3 below engages the evidential argument in its strongest form under it.

### 5.3 The ordained minimum and the modal distinctions

Between RP's denial of per-event indispensability and the decree's real inclusion of evil stands the working paper's refined thesis, which this Part adopts as the measure's center (working paper 6.7, with 6.5's modal analysis). Distinguish election, God's choice of persons and destination (Ephesians 1:4-5; Romans 8:29-30), from the content of the actual redemptive decree. The actual decree encompasses the Son's saving work in response to sin. Redemption from sin conceptually presupposes sin; righteous judgment of culpable rebellion presupposes rebellion; the ordained crucifixion involves suffering and culpable human action (Acts 2:23; 4:27-28). Some evil and suffering therefore belong necessarily to the fulfillment of this particular decree: not by a metaphysical law that glory requires sin, but by the decree's own content, since a history whose center is redemption-from-sin cannot be executed in a world without sin to redeem from. This is the ordained minimum, and its modal form is the working paper's first distinction (6.5): let D denote God's actual freely chosen eternal decree; if D includes the Fall as an ordained means in the actual history, then given D, the Fall occurs; this expresses the faithfulness of providence to the decree, not an independent proof that the Fall is logically unavoidable. The ontological qualification stands with it: creatureliness as such does not entail inevitable sin, and this Part does not assert that God could not have established a dependent creature immediately in confirmed righteousness; the necessity argued here rests on the content of the decree God actually made, not on an entailment from finitude to fall (6.7). Nor does the ordained minimum distribute downward: that some evil belongs to this decree's fulfillment does not justify each particular atrocity, prove each instance of natural suffering indispensable, or establish the actual decree as the only logically possible one (6.7, scope limitation). The minimum is a floor under the decree's content, not a warrant stamped on every event above the floor; above the floor, section 5.2's framework claim and section 4's boundary divide the labor between them.

### 5.4 The felix culpa boundary: the joint position

Thesis T-V.4 and axiom V.5 state the boundary; audit item (iii) fixed its three members, and all three are needed, since dropping any one of them produces a doctrine the treatise does not hold.

**(a) Decree-relative necessity, affirmed.** The fall actually occurred under the decree, and relative to the redemptive decree God actually made, the fall is presupposed by the redemption decreed: the decree's content (sec. 5.3) is redemption from sin, judgment of rebellion, and the Son's manifestation as Judge and Redeemer, and a history executing that content contains the fall as certainly as it contains the cross, which the canon states in the same breath (Acts 2:23; 4:28). The working paper's formula is retained with its qualifier load-bearing: the Fall is "regrettable but necessary," necessary relative to God's chosen decree. Given D, F: that is the whole of the necessity claimed, and it is real necessity, not a manner of speaking; a decree that might have failed at its presupposition would not be the counsel of sections 2 and 4.

**(b) Metaphysical necessity, denied.** The fall is not necessary in itself, not required by God's nature, by creatureliness, or by the logic of communion. Adam stood in posse peccare et posse non peccare and might have stood (axiom III.5; Institutes 1.15.8: his will was pliable in either direction, he had not received constancy to persevere, and he fell by his own will only). The necessity of member (a) is therefore located where the working paper locates it, in the free content of the actual decree, and nowhere else: not in Adam's constitution, which made the fall possible but not inevitable (mutability does not entail rebellion, 6.5, distinction 4), and not in God's goodness, which the fall contradicts rather than expresses. Strong felix culpa, the claim that God needed the fall in order to be or to show what he is, is not a treatise claim in any of its forms.

**(c) The consummation's silence, honored.** No text in the anchor set, and Revelation 21-22 expressly, claims that the consummation's glory required the fall as its precondition (study 11, finding 4(ii)). The closing chapters narrate the state that in fact follows redemption; they nowhere say the river, the tree, the throne, or the reign required sin, and the treatise may not cite them as though they did. This member is the boundary's exegetical teeth: the decree-relative necessity of member (a) is a synthesis from the decree's redemptive content, and it may not borrow the consummation's imagery to upgrade itself into a claim that the end, as God describes the end, presupposes the catastrophe. The end exceeds Eden on Eden's own terms (study 11, finding 5; axiom III.3) without Eden's fall being written into its definition.

The three members together are the treatise's whole felix culpa position: the fall was necessary, given what God in fact decreed to do; it need not have been, had God decreed otherwise; and the glory that follows it is nowhere said to have required it. O felix culpa may be sung as the church's astonishment at the redemption that answered the fall; it may not be printed as the treatise's metaphysics.

## 6. The reservation: 2 Peter 3:7 and the scope of the Noahic promise

The Noahic research note repaired Part VI's frame and flagged one text for this Part: the canon's own eschatological reservation, 2 Peter 3:7, where the heavens and earth that now exist are "stored up for fire," kept for the day of judgment and destruction of the ungodly. The question the note poses is whether the Noahic commitment is age-bound or absolute, and it matters here because Part V's account of this age's patience (secs. 4-5) must not quietly absolutize a promise the canon itself scopes.

The texts divide cleanly. The Noahic promise is specific in its instrument and its object: never again shall all flesh be cut off by the waters of a flood; never again a flood to destroy the earth (Genesis 9:11); God resolves never again to strike down every living creature as he has done (8:21-22). What the promise excludes is one instrument of universal destruction, forever; it is not a promise never to judge, never to prohibit, or never to end the age (Noahic note, Step 2(c)) [Textual analysis, HIGH]. Second Peter's reservation operates at a different joint: not another administration within the age but the age's termination, a judgment by fire at the day of the Lord (2 Peter 3:7, 10-12), followed, in Peter's own sentence, by the new heavens and new earth in which righteousness dwells (3:13). The reservation therefore does not breach the promise; it scopes it. Genesis 9:11 is absolute in its own terms: no flood will ever again destroy all flesh, and the fire of 2 Peter 3 is not a flood, nor is the judgment it names an act of moral government within the present order but the present order's end.

The disposition for the treatise is the note's: the Noahic material enters Part V as age-description, not as premise. The age between the flood and the fire is an age in which universal destruction is excluded as the ordinary instrument of moral government, in which judgment is discriminating rather than universal (local, partial, graded: Babel, Sodom, Nineveh repenting, per the note's candidate 4), and in which God's patience has the positive content Romans 2:4 and 2 Peter 3:9 themselves assign it: patience meant to lead to repentance, the Lord not willing that any should perish, which is why the day has not yet come. The reservation confirms from the eschatological side what section 4's boundary states from the epistemic side: the patience of this age is policy within a history that ends in judgment, not a metaphysical fact about God that evil could hide behind forever. Part VI will draw the accommodation consequences under the repaired frame (axiom VI.1) on its own criteria; nothing in Part VI may borrow this section's age-description as a premise, and the retired binary (no Option C; accommodation-or-destruction) is not revived by being quoted here: the note's verdict stands, that the binary fails as stated and the repair does the work [HIGH].

## 7. Counter-positions in their strongest registered form

The register's disciplines govern this section: each position is stated in its strongest form from its registered source; positions whose sources stand at NOT YET CONSULTED are stated only in their standard registered form and flagged as such, so that no reply here pretends to a direct reading the treatise has not done (Plantinga, Adams, Wykstra, Bergmann, Hick, process theism, Aquinas). Replies are kept distinguishable from statements: the treatise's answer follows each statement, separately marked.

### 7.1 Open theism on Genesis 6

**Statement.** Study 02 registers and tests the reading in its own terms: it "takes the grief language at full face value, resolves the tension by reading the future as partly open, and accepts the consequence that God's experiential engagement with history involves genuine learning and risk" (study 02, sec. 4.1). On this reading, God's regret over man's making and over Saul's appointment is the report of an outcome God had not settled and now sorrows over, and the denial texts are read as denying caprice while the affirmations carry the metaphysics. Study 02 records that the specific texts underlying the reading were VERIFIED (the specialist articles on nacham and atsav, Chisholm 1995 and Parunak 1975, stand at CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION through the NET notes), while the position's named representatives remain unnamed in the Phase 1 sources; the statement above is therefore the reading as study 02 formulated and tested it, which is the form the register approves (register Part V, entry 18; the specialist mediation behind it at entries 14-15).

**Reply.** The reading is declined on the study's own grounds, restated: it requires nacham to bear the semantic weight of error and revision, which the verb does not bear anywhere of God (not shuv; never confession of error), and it cannot accommodate Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29 inside the same semantic field without remainder, where 15:29 stands in the same chapter as the regret it would need to reinterpret (study 02, sec. 4.1). The reconciliation of section 3 takes the grief at full seriousness without the metaphysical price: the face value of the text includes the face value of its denials, and an account that reads one pole literally and the other evasively has not taken the text's face value but selected half of it.

### 7.2 Process theism

**Statement.** In its standard registered form (representative unnamed in the Phase 1 sources; NOT YET CONSULTED, register Part V, entry 25): God is dipolar, in his consequent nature genuinely affected by and learning from the world; he cannot unilaterally prevent evil, since creaturely power is not his to override, and he works instead by persuasion toward the good; evil is therefore not a problem of permission at all, because prevention was never within God's power, and divine goodness is preserved at the cost of divine omnipotence and of the decree itself.

**Reply.** The treatise declines the position on study 06's domain finding, which is the ground the position would need to surrender: Ephesians 1:11 predicates of God that he works ta panta, all things without exception within the created totality, according to the counsel of his will [HIGH]; a God who cannot govern the totality is not the God of the anchor texts, and the position solves the problem of evil by removing one of its terms, the governor, which the treatise's whole architecture refuses to do (Part II, axiom II.1). The position's pastoral intuition, that God is genuinely affected by creaturely suffering, is the one element the reconciliation already honors at section 3 without the metaphysical loss: genuine affection without mutability. What is declined is the exchange of the decree for the affection, when the texts present both.

### 7.3 The evidential argument from evil

**Statement.** The working paper registers the argument at its full strength and this Part repeats it so (6.4), because the treatise's reply is worthless against a weakened version: many instances of suffering appear, to informed and sympathetic observers, to serve no outweighing good; they are extreme in scale, apparently gratuitous in distribution, and prolonged in duration; hidden reasons are possible in principle, but the sheer pattern lowers confidence that every instance has a God-justifying purpose; on an evidential rather than a deductive construal, the existence of such apparently pointless suffering counts as evidence against the God of classical theism. The logical argument, by contrast, the treatise takes as answered at the level of consistency (6.3): if it is possible that God has a morally sufficient reason to ordain a history containing evil, the set of propositions (God is perfectly good; God is omnipotent; evil exists) is not contradictory, and the treatise's theses do not entail that evil is good, that God requires evil for his goodness, or that every evil is individually necessary.

**Reply.** The evidential argument's force is conceded at exactly one joint and refused at the inference. Conceded: apparent gratuitousness is real and extensive; the treatise's boundary (sec. 4) predicts it, since the reasons for particular evils lie in the domain the verse reserves, and the Part offers no instance-level explanations to set against the catalogue (sec. 5.2). The inference from the pattern of apparent gratuitousness to probable actual gratuitousness is refused on the boundary's epistemic rule (PRO.2): where a domain of reasons is legitimately withheld, the noseeum step, from "we see no reason" to "probably there is none," is invalid for agents in our position, and its invalidity does not shrink as the catalogue grows, because the catalogue's size measures our ignorance of the reserved domain, not the domain's emptiness. The positive denial of actual gratuitousness is then carried not by the boundary (which supplies no positive evidence, PRO.2) but by the synthesis of section 5.2 on T1-T3, at MEDIUM and with its gating named; the treatise does not pretend the MEDIUM is a demonstration. The evidential dispute is therefore relocated, honestly, to the bridge premise T3 and to the archetype behind it: an interlocutor who grants a perfectly good and wise governor of the whole history, and denies that such governance entails sufficient justification for each governed evil, has located the real disagreement, and Part X takes up the premises there. What the treatise will not do is meet the evidential argument with per-event explanations it does not have, nor with the boundary wielded as a demonstration it is not.

### 7.4 Skeptical theism, and the critique from both sides

**Statement.** The skeptical-theist position, as the register carries it (Wykstra on the noseeum inference and the CORNEA condition, entry 21; Bergmann's defense, entry 22; both NOT YET CONSULTED, stated in standard registered form): our cognitive position relative to God's reasons is such that the inference from apparent to actual gratuitousness is unwarranted, because we have no good reason to think the goods and entailments God might have in view would be visible to us. The critique of the position, from the other side, is also registered in its strength: thoroughgoing skepticism about our access to God's reasons threatens skepticism about our moral knowledge itself, since if we cannot judge that apparently pointless suffering probably lacks a justifying reason, our confidence in moral judgments God might override is equally unsettled, and the position purchases theodicy at the price of the moral realism the theist also needs.

**Reply.** The treatise's relation to skeptical theism is convergent in conclusion and divergent in ground, and the divergence is the guard against the critique. Convergence: the noseeum inference is invalid, and this Part's boundary yields that conclusion (secs. 4.1, 7.3). Divergence: the treatise's boundary is not a general skepticism about our cognitive access to value or to God's mind, but a revealed assignment of domains with a purpose clause. The revealed domain is generous and is given for doing: the moral law, the character of God as the archetype (Part I), the RP promise to the called, the whole apparatus by which the treatise judges evil as evil, are revealed things, known and usable; the hidden domain is specified by belonging, not by content, and its hiddenness is God's stated policy, not an inference from human cognitive frailty in general. The critique's target, skepticism that bleeds across the boundary into moral knowledge, is therefore answered by the boundary's own structure: the same verse that reserves the hidden things entrusts the revealed things forever, for obedience, and the treatise's moral judgments are made from the revealed side with full confidence. PRO.2's second half is the Part's internal guard, and it cuts against the treatise's own defenders as readily as against its critics: the boundary supplies no positive evidence in any particular case, so it may not be used to silence the evidential question, to assure a sufferer that a reason certainly exists for this event on the basis of the verse alone, or to convert the synthesis's MEDIUM into a pretense of knowledge. The treatise's positive claim stands on T1-T3 as a theological commitment argued from the archetype and the decree texts, and the boundary keeps it honest about what kind of claim it is.

### 7.5 Two further engagements the register schedules

**Hick's soul-making theodicy** (Evil and the God of Love; NOT YET CONSULTED, entry 24) is registered here because its surface resemblance to Part III is the treatise's most likely misreading in the literature, and the difference controls the felix culpa boundary: Hick's Irenaean two-stage account makes epistemic distance and a soul-making environment, including a real fall into an immature state, the designed path itself, such that the first stage's imperfection is a feature of the good design and not a defection from an upright state. The treatise's two states differ at the root: the first state is very good, upright, and sufficient (study 01; Part III), the fall is a culpable defection from it, regrettable even within the decree (sec. 5.4), and the second state's glory is nowhere said to require the defection (5.4(c)). Where Hick's account tends to dissolve the fall into the design, the treatise's decree-relative necessity is precisely the doctrine that keeps the fall a fall while keeping it inside the government; the resemblance is structural (two stages) and the substance is opposed (designed immaturity versus decreed redemption of a culpable fall).

**Adams on horrendous evils** (Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God; NOT YET CONSULTED, entry 23) is registered as the interlocutor this Part's framework claim most needs and has not yet taken up: the demand that God defeat evil not merely globally but within the sufferer's own life, so that participation in horrendous evil is itself woven into a life that is, for that person, a great good. Section 5.2's inventory honestly records that RP's specified good runs to the called as a body in the golden chain, and that the treatise has not yet worked out, at Part V's level, the relation between the universal no-gratuitousness denial and the individual's defeat of the evil he suffered; the consummation texts (study 11: God dwelling with them, tears wiped away, death and pain no more) supply materials, and Part VIII takes the question up with Adams's demand on the table.
