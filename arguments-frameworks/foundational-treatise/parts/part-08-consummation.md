## 1. The question of this Part and its theses

Every preceding Part has been, in one way or another, a promissory note drawn on this one. Part II's governing premise ends in glorification (Romans 8:30), and Part V's theodicy ends by reserving its hardest question, the defeat of evil inside the sufferer's own life, to the consummation texts. Part III stated the creation arc's first state and read the second back under discipline; Part IV's four states of created freedom have a fourth member that no earlier Part could exhibit. This Part exhibits it. The question is: what is the final state of the created order and of the saints within it, and how secure is it?

The Part's theses, as the derivation map states them, are four.

**T-VIII.1.** The consummated state is permanent: it has no fall left to undergo. Depends on axioms VIII.1, VIII.2.

**T-VIII.2.** The saints in glory are confirmed, non posse peccare, carried as theological synthesis at MEDIUM-HIGH with its joining premises named. Depends on axioms VIII.3, VIII.7.

**T-VIII.3.** The end is renewal of the totality under a new order, not replacement without remainder and not mere repair. Depends on axiom VIII.4.

**T-VIII.4.** The consummated life is communion in act: priestly service and royal reign together, entered by gift. Depends on axioms VIII.5, VIII.6, I.2.

Four limits govern the argument and are stated at the outset, because each one caps what a section below may claim. First, the textual base is thinner here than anywhere else in the treatise: no papyrus reaches Revelation 21-22 (P47, the oldest substantial witness, covers 9:10 to 17:2 only), and the earliest continuous witnesses for these chapters are Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Alexandrinus (register entry VIII-12, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION; study 11, sec. 6). Within the witnesses consulted, no variant in the load-bearing verses alters study 11's findings; but BDAG and the major commentaries on Revelation 21-22 (Aune, Beale, Koester) stand at NOT YET CONSULTED (entry VIII-13), and no lexical verdict in this Part is cited as apparatus-complete. Second, no Reformed confessional formulation of the saints' confirmed state in glory has been consulted for this locus (entry VIII-1, NOT YET CONSULTED): the Westminster texts opened in this project treat Christology, not glory, and this Part states its confirmation doctrine from study 11 and the Augustinian scheme, claiming no confessional formulation. Third, Augustine's City of God XXII.30, the standard additional locus for the confirmed will, was not opened and is not cited anywhere in this Part (entry VIII-5); the Augustinian scheme below rests on De correptione et gratia 12.33 and Enchiridion 104-105 only. Fourth, the audit's two-ratings discipline binds every page: the permanence of the state (axiom VIII.2) is Necessary inference, HIGH; the label for the saints' interior condition, confirmed and non posse peccare (axiom VIII.3), is Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH; and no argument in this Part spends the first rating on the second claim (audit item (ii); Prolegomena, sec. 4.6, discipline 2).

## 2. The text of the end: the negation chain

Study 11's first finding is that the consummated state is described, before it is described any other way, by a chain of absolute negations. [Explicit text, HIGH; study 11, finding 1; axiom VIII.1.] The chain has five links, and each link abolishes a named feature of the fallen order.

**Death, mourning, crying, pain (21:4).** "Kai ho thanatos ouk estai eti, oute penthos oute krauge oute ponos ouk estai eti": death shall be no more, neither mourning nor crying nor pain shall be any more. The four nouns name the fall's whole economy of sorrow, and each has its profile in the book. Thanatos, death, is in Revelation nearly a person: it rides (6:8), holds the dead in Hades (20:13), and is thrown into the lake of fire (20:14) before it is said to be no more; the abolition of 21:4 is therefore the narrative completion of a destruction already narrated, not a free-standing symbol. Penthos, mourning, falls elsewhere in the book only on Babylon (18:7-8): what Babylon boasts of escaping and then suffers in a day, the bride simply does not have. Krauge, crying, the cry of distress, occurs in the critical text only here in this book; its Septuagintal anchor is Isaiah 65:19, where the voice of crying is no longer heard, and John strengthens Isaiah: not only is the sound unheard, the crying itself shall not be. Ponos covers both pain and toil, and is the Greek-side counterpart, at the thematic level, to the toil of Genesis 3:17-19 (the Septuagint's word there is lype, not ponos; the answer is thematic, not lexical). The clause that grounds the four abolitions is causal: "hoti ta prota apelthan," because the former things passed away. The abolition is not arbitrary comfort; it follows from the passing of the order whose conditions these nouns were. And the verse opens with an act before it states the abolitions: "he will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (21:4a), a clause whose personal grammar section 8 will need.

**Every cursed thing (22:3).** "Kai pan katathema ouk estai eti": every cursed thing shall be no more. The word katathema is a New Testament hapax legomenon, the strengthened compound of anathema, carrying the Septuagint's cherem sense: a thing devoted to destruction without hope of redemption. Three features of the clause carry weight. First, pan: with the universal negation it amounts to "nothing at all"; no corner of the renewed order is contemplated where a cursed thing persists. Second, the clause is unlocalized: John does not say the curse is absent only within the walls; he says every cursed thing shall not be, and then, as a further statement, that the throne of God and of the Lamb shall be in the city. The curse's abolition is a fact about the new order as such, and the throne's presence is given as its ruling evidence. [Explicit text for the scope, HIGH; Necessary inference for the grounding in the throne's presence, MEDIUM-HIGH; study 11, finding 8.] Third, the clause's frame is Zechariah's: Zechariah 14:11 LXX closes its Jerusalem vision with "kai anathema ouk estai eti," and John takes up the clause frame and the anathema root, adding pan and the strengthened compound. The lexical bridge between the curse of Genesis 3 and its removal therefore runs through Zechariah and the cherem tradition, not through Genesis's own vocabulary: Genesis 3:17 LXX curses the ground with the adjective epikataratos, a different word from a different line. The reversal of the Genesis curse in 22:3 is canonical and conceptual, not a word-for-word quotation, and this Part argues it at exactly that tier: the removal of the cursed condition as such, pan, every cursed thing. [Canonical synthesis; study 11, secs. 2 and 3.5.]

**Night and sea (22:5; 21:1).** "Kai nux ouk estai eti": night shall be no more, and the inhabitants need no light of lamp or sun, for the Lord God shines on them. And at the vision's opening, "he thalassa ouk estin eti": the sea is no more. In this book's own usage the sea is the beast's place of origin (13:1) and the reservoir of the dead (20:13); its absence is the removal of the chaos-source. The new earth is not waterless; it has the river of the water of life (22:1). What is gone is the sea as threat. [Symbolic freight within the book's own usage, HIGH for the distribution; study 11, sec. 2.]

**Nothing common (21:27).** "Kai ou me eiselthe eis auten pan koinon": there shall by no means enter into the city anything common. The negation is the strongest the language offers, ou me with the subjunctive: by no means, never. And the word requires the precision the Prolegomena's conventions promise (sec. 8.1 there): the adjective at 21:27 is koinon, "common," not akathartos, "unclean." The English versions mostly smooth the distinction away (ESV and NASB "unclean," NIV "impure"; HCSB's "profane" stays closest to the lexeme). But Peter's protest at Acts 10:14 holds the two words side by side as distinguishable, "anything common or unclean (koinon e akatharton)," and in Revelation itself the akathartos vocabulary is spent elsewhere: on the unclean spirits (16:13) and on Babylon (17:4; 18:2). At the gate of the city John reaches instead for the common/holy antithesis: the city is hagia, holy (21:2, 10), and what is merely common, un-sanctified, does not enter it. The exclusion is then stated in three registers together: cultic (nothing common), moral (no one who does an abomination, bdelygma, or a lie, pseudos), and by the book (only those written in the Lamb's book of life). The consummated exclusion is therefore not only that the impure are shut out, but that nothing un-sanctified is inside: the city's purity is consecration, not merely cleansing. [Lexical, HIGH; study 11, sec. 3.4.]

Running through the chain is one grammatical frame: eti, "any longer," with the verb of being. The sea is no more (present, 21:1); death shall be no more (21:4, twice); every cursed thing shall be no more (22:3); night shall be no more (22:5). The negatives are absolute within their subjects: not diminished, not intermittent, but no longer existing at all in the described state. No version consulted softens them. This is the text on which the next section's inference stands.

## 3. Permanence: the inference and its ground

Study 11's second finding delivers its required verdict in two halves, and this Part keeps the halves separate because the audit requires it.

**The first half: the state's permanence.** As an explicit proposition, "a further fall is impossible" is nowhere stated in Revelation 21-22: the text does not discuss possibility at all. As an inference, it is as strong as the passage can make it. The state described excludes, first, every consequence of the fall: death, mourning, crying, and pain (21:4), and every cursed thing (22:3). It excludes, second, every agent of corruption: nothing common enters (21:27); the practitioners of abomination and falsehood are outside (21:27; 22:15); the second death has disposed of the finally impenitent (21:8); and death itself, the last agent, has been destroyed before the state is described (20:14). It excludes, third, every occasion of a renewed fall: no night in which deeds hide, no sea from which a beast rises. And it measures the inhabitants' reign by the book's maximum duration, eis tous aionas ton aionon, "forever and ever" (22:5), the same formula that measures the life of God himself (4:9-10), under the immediate presence of God and the Lamb: God tabernacles with his peoples (21:3), and the throne is in the city (22:3). The study's sentence is the Part's thesis T-VIII.1: a state so defined has no fall left to undergo. [Necessary inference, HIGH; study 11, finding 2; axiom VIII.2.]

**The ground of the permanence.** The inference states what the state excludes; it does not yet say on what the state's holding rests. Revelation's own answer is positional rather than mechanical: the curse is absent because the throne is present (sec. 2), and the dwelling of God is with men permanently and unmediatedly (21:3; the verb skenoo is the incarnation's verb, John 1:14, here made everlasting). The treatise's fuller answer comes from the decree chain of Part II: foreknowledge, predestination, calling, justification, glorification form one sequence of divine acts with no human co-agent named as a link, and glorification stands in the aorist, as accomplished (axiom II.8; study 05, finding 7). The consummation's security rests on that chain: glorification is a link in God's act, not a contingency awaiting the saints' unaided stability. [Theological synthesis, HIGH, for the security direction; axiom VIII.7.] The verse that closes study 05's chain and the verses that open this Part's vision describe one end from two sides: the chain that ends in glorification (Romans 8:30) ends here, in a state where death's non-existence is predicated outright (study 11, sec. 3.3).

**What the inference is not.** The HIGH rating belongs to the state's described permanence: the order of things in which a fall could occur no longer exists. It does not belong, and may not be transferred, to any claim about the inhabitants' interior condition, their wills considered in themselves. Revelation 21-22 speaks of the interior only through the city's boundaries and its occupants' service and reign. The claim about the wills is section 5's, at its own lower rating, and the distance between the two ratings is not an embarrassment to be closed by rhetoric; it is the exact measure of what this text does and does not say.

## 4. The three silences

Before the label can be stated, the Part must print what the anchor text withholds, because study 11 registers three silences as load-bearing and the Prolegomena's method (sec. 4.2 there) treats warranted non-assertion as a positive rule. Each silence is an explicit absence in a fully inventoried text. [Explicit absence, HIGH for each; study 11, finding 4.]

**(i) No reason is given why the first creation was mutable.** No clause in the two chapters explains Eden's capacity to fall, and Genesis's own silence on the point (study 01) is matched here, not repaired. Part III argued the first state's mutability from the lexical limit, the canonical controls, and the tradition's loci; Revelation adds the end from which the arc is visible, but it does not add an explanation of the beginning. Why God made a world whose head could fall is a question this text does not take up. Accounts that answer it from the consummation's side (the unconfirmed state as fitting for a creation ordered through probation to glory, for instance) go beyond anything Revelation 21-22 states and would be, at best, philosophical conjecture; this Part advances none of them as doctrine, and registers the question among its open items (sec. 11).

**(ii) No claim is made that the fall was necessary to reach this state.** The chapters narrate the state that in fact follows redemption. They nowhere say that the river, the tree, the throne, or the reign required sin as their precondition. This silence is the exegetical teeth of the treatise's felix culpa boundary (axiom V.5; Part V, sec. 5.4, member (c)): the fall is necessary relative to the redemptive decree God actually made, a synthesis argued from the decree's content; it is not metaphysically necessary; and the strongest eschatological text in the canon, describing the end at greatest length and in greatest detail, refuses to write the catastrophe into the end's definition. A felix culpa of the strong form, the fall as instrumentally necessary to the consummation's glory, cannot be cited from these chapters, and this Part does not cite it. The end exceeds Eden on Eden's own terms (sec. 6) without Eden's fall being among the terms.

**(iii) No mechanism of confirmation is supplied.** The text describes the consummated state's boundaries, occupants, and duration, and grounds the whole in God's dwelling and throne; it does not state whether the state's permanence rests on the saints' transformed wills, on God's preserving presence, or on both. Section 5 argues the mechanism from the wider canon and prints it as synthesis. The discipline is axiom VIII.7's: any mechanism argument is synthesis and must be presented as such, never as Revelation's teaching. A reader who finishes this Part believing that Revelation explains how the saints are kept has been misled by the Part, and the Part writes its mechanism section under this sentence to prevent exactly that.

## 5. The confirmation label: the Augustinian scheme and the mechanism discipline

### 5.1 The label stated, with its joining premises

The saints in glory are confirmed in righteousness, non posse peccare. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH; axiom VIII.3; thesis T-VIII.2.] The claim joins three premises, and the Part names them because the rating is the rating of the join. (1) Revelation's exclusions (secs. 2-3): the state has no fall left to undergo, stated at Necessary inference, HIGH, as a claim about the state's order. (2) The Augustinian four-state scheme's last member: the tradition's account of the glorified will, in which the inability to sin is not a restriction laid on freedom but its greatest form. (3) The canon's account of the saints' perseverance, gathered in the decree chain (axiom II.8): the security of the end rests on God's act, so that the inhabitants' confirmation is the interior side of a state whose exterior God has already sealed. What the join adds beyond its premises is the application to the saints' interior condition: that the wills of the glorified are themselves so fixed in righteousness that sin is not possible for them. Revelation speaks to that interior only indirectly, and the MEDIUM-HIGH rating is the price of the indirectness, paid openly.

### 5.2 Augustine: the first liberty and the last

The scheme's primary locus is De correptione et gratia, ch. 12, sec. 33, read in the Latin and in Wallis's translation (NPNF First Series, vol. 5), CONSULTED DIRECTLY (entry VIII-2). Augustine's verified verbatim collocations are the pairs posse non peccare and non posse peccare, with the parallel pairs posse non mori / non posse mori and bonum posse non deserere / bonum non posse deserere. Wallis: "Therefore the first liberty of the will was to be able not to sin, the last will be much greater, not to be able to sin; the first immortality was to be able not to die, the last will be much greater, not to be able to die; the first was the power of perseverance, to be able not to forsake good - the last will be the felicity of perseverance, not to be able to forsake good." Axiom VIII.3 takes the sentence for its definition of the label: "the last liberty is much greater than the first, not to be able to sin, not to be able to die, not to be able to forsake the good."

Two precisions govern the treatise's use of Augustine here, and both are printed because the register's honesty flags require them. First, the label this Part uses, non posse peccare, is Augustine's own wording for state (d): the last liberty's name stands in his Latin. The neighboring tag for the fallen state, non posse non peccare, is different: it was not found verbatim in any Augustinian text opened, and it is the later tradition's label for the condition Augustine describes in prose (the Bavinck editor's note is a source for the tag, so labeled; entry VIII-11, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION). Part IV carries the fallen state under that flag; Part VIII's label needs no such flag, and the difference between the two tags' provenance is itself a small exhibit of the treatise's method. Second, Augustine's other fourfold, at Enchiridion ch. 31, sec. 118 (ante legem, sub lege, sub gratia, in pace), is a salvation-historical scheme and must not be conflated with the posse scheme of De correptione et gratia 12.33 (entry VIII-4, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION). The scheme Part VIII uses is the posse scheme only.

The Enchiridion corroborates the last member directly (ch. 28, secs. 104-107; Outler translation, Library of Christian Classics, CONSULTED DIRECTLY, entry VIII-3). Of the future life Augustine writes that man "will not have the power to will evil," his will being "much freer, because he will then have no power whatever to serve sin" (sec. 105). The comparative is the doctrine's center: the confirmed will is not less free than Adam's pliable will but much freer, freedom being measured by the will's unhindered adherence to the good rather than by its openness to opposites. Part III set Calvin's Edenic pole against this: Adam upright, "his will was pliable in either direction and he had not received constancy to persevere" (Institutes 1.15.8, Beveridge, CONSULTED DIRECTLY, entry VIII-8). The two loci are the arc's two ends in the tradition's own grammar: integrity without confirmation at the beginning; confirmation as the increase of liberty at the end.

The Reformed orthodox reception stands where the register places it. Turretin, in the locus on the state of glory, carries the modal vocabulary of the states: paradise knew posse non peccare; the glorified state, the condition of the elect angels, is non posse peccare (Institutes, vol. 2, Giger/Dennison, CONSULTED DIRECTLY, entry VIII-10). The Part adopts Turretin as the reception of the Augustinian scheme at synthesis tier: the label the treatise prints is the tradition's label, received through its own doctors, not a coinage of this treatise.

### 5.3 The mechanism, argued as synthesis

Section 4's third silence bars presenting any mechanism as Revelation's teaching. What the wider canon and the system's own axioms support, argued openly as construction, is this. Augustine's own scheme carries a mechanism distinction, and it is the one the treatise adopts. Adam's aid was an adjutorium sine quo non, an aid without which he could not persevere but which he could forsake (Part III, sec. 7.1, from De correptione et gratia chs. 11-12, DIRECT). The grace given in Christ is of the other kind: an aid by which perseverance is effected, effecting the willing itself. And Augustine supplies a standing precedent inside the created order: the angels who stood received, as their reward, such a fullness of blessing that by it they might have the fullest certainty of always abiding in it (ch. 11, sec. 32, as carried in Part III). The treatise's mechanism claim is that the saints' confirmation runs on those two rails together: wills healed and fixed by efficacious grace of the second kind, the adjutorium quo applied eschatologically; and God's preserving presence immediate and unmediated, the throne in the city and his name on their foreheads (22:3-4), so that the external and internal securities coincide. The pattern of a human will fixed in non-contrariety to God's will without being suppressed is not foreign to the system: it is the pattern of Christ's own human will under the conciliar confession (axioms VII.1-VII.3), and the saints' confirmation is its created echo, at the distance between the Mediator and the mediated. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM: the component premises are strong (the aid distinction and the angels' precedent are Augustine's, DIRECT; the decree chain is axiom II.8), but the assembly into a mechanism for the glorified saints is this treatise's construction, and Revelation 21-22, the anchor text, is silent on it, as sec. 4 (iii) records.]

Two boundary notes close the section. First, the grace state under this scheme (axiom IV.4) is never sinless in fact in via: the saints in this life receive perseverance itself bestowed and still fight sin. Confirmation belongs to patria, not to via prolonged; the scheme's distinction of states, not any contradiction, carries both claims, and Part IV's state (c) must not be quietly promoted into state (d) anywhere in the treatise. Second, no confessional formulation of this doctrine is claimed (sec. 1, second limit): the Westminster standards as consulted in this project do not treat the saints' confirmed state, and the Part's doctrine stands on the exegetical and Augustinian basis stated, awaiting the confessional register check registered as an open item.

## 6. Continuity and discontinuity: one totality, a new order

Thesis T-VIII.3 states the Part's verdict on the relation between the first creation and the new: the consummation is discontinuity of the order and continuity of the totality. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH, on grammatical and lexical observations rated HIGH; study 11, finding 3; axiom VIII.4. The audit holds the verdict consistent with the two-states thesis of axiom III.3: the two states are states of the same created totality under different orders (audit item (xi)).]

The verdict rests on the passage's two verb families, and each family forbids one of the one-sided readings. The passing verb is aperchomai in the aorist: apelthan, "they passed away, departed," said of the first heaven and the first earth (21:1) and of the former things, ta prota, as a whole (21:4). It is a departure verb, not an annihilation verb: John does not write that the first creation was destroyed or unmade, but that it went away, as a departed order. The making declaration runs the other way: at 21:5 the enthroned One says, with the adjective fronted as predicate, "kaina poio panta": not "I make all new things," but "I make all things new." The panta that is made new is the same totality the passage has been describing, heaven, earth, city, nations, now stated without remainder. A pure replacement cannot carry poio panta; a merely cosmetic renewal cannot carry ouk estai eti. The text forces the two-sided verdict on its own grammar, before any wider canon is consulted.

The vocabulary of newness agrees. John uses kainos for heaven (21:1), earth (21:1), Jerusalem (21:2), and the totality in God's own speech (21:5), and earlier in the book for the new name and the new song; he never uses neos anywhere in Revelation. The kainos/neos distinction (new in quality and freshness against new in time and sequence) is a tendency of the lexemes, not an invariable law, and cannot by itself decide the question; but the distribution in this passage is one-sided, and the consummation's newness is uniformly kainos-newness. [Lexical, HIGH for the distribution; MEDIUM for the weight the quality-sense bears in any single occurrence; study 11, sec. 3.1.]

The book's dramatic movements draw the same picture. The city descends to the renewed earth; the redeemed do not ascend away from a discarded world (21:2). Eden's river and tree reappear, but inside a city (22:1-2): the garden is taken up into the garden-city, not restored as a garden. The nations walk by the city's light, and the kings of the earth bring their glory into it (21:24, 26): human culture and rule, absent as such from Eden's description, belong to the consummated picture, arriving with their history rather than deleted by it. The gates are never shut (21:25, with the emphatic ou me), because there is no night there and no enemy to shut them against. And the discontinuities are discontinuities of the order, total within their kind: no sea (21:1), no temple building, for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple (21:22), no night (22:5), no curse (22:3), no death (21:4). What passes is the former order in its fallenness; what is made new is all things.

Two corroborations are registered at their proper strength. Romans 8:19-22, creation groaning to be set free from its bondage to corruption, converges with this verdict; it is study 05's text, not this study's, and it is cited as convergence, not as this Part's evidence (study 11, finding 3). And Calvin, at Institutes 3.23.8, runs Genesis 1:31 as an argument that depravity is not from creation (Battles text known through verbatim secondary quotation, Beveridge wording consulted directly; entry VIII-9, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION as stated there), from which the register draws the Part VIII consequence: the consummation's incorruption is an increase over a genuinely good but mutable beginning, not the repair of a defective one. That consequence joins Part III's defense of Eden (sec. 8 there) to this Part's account of the end: the arc rises because the beginning was unconfirmed, not because it was flawed.

With this section the two-states thesis (axiom III.3) stands complete in both its members, at the joint calibration Part III printed: Theological synthesis, MEDIUM from study 01's side, MEDIUM-HIGH as the joint assessment with study 11. The end exceeds the beginning on Eden's own terms: where Genesis 1:31's tov meod asserted fitting excellence without the vocabulary of incorruption, Revelation 21-22 describes the end with exactly the vocabulary Genesis withheld: death abolished, curse abolished, entrance sealed against everything un-sanctified, reign measured by forever and ever (study 11, finding 5). The trajectory is an increase in security and glory, not a restoration to a lost maximum and nothing more. This Part raises no earlier rating: Part III's MEDIUM from the Genesis side stands as printed there, and the joint MEDIUM-HIGH is the same joint assessment, now argued from both ends.

## 7. The consummated vocation, and entrance by gift

The negation chain says what the final state is not. Verses 3-5 of chapter 22 say what its inhabitants do, and the two verbs together correct any picture of the consummation as either idle rest or autonomous rule. The inhabitants are God's douloi, his servants, literally his slaves: the book's standing name for God's own (1:1; 7:3; 19:2, 5; 22:6), applied to the consummated community without embarrassment, so that perfected freedom is confessed in the language of ownership. They latreusousin auto, render him priestly service (22:3): latreuo is the cultic service verb, which the old order localized in priests and temple; at 7:15 the redeemed serve him day and night in his temple, and at 22:3 there is no temple building, because God and the Lamb are the temple (21:22), and the service continues without night to interrupt it. They see his face, and his name is on their foreheads (22:4), the consummation of what no priest of the old order could have, bearing the Name openly as the high priest bore the plate inscribed "Holy to the LORD" (Exodus 28:36-38, thematic). And they basileusousin eis tous aionas ton aionon, reign forever and ever (22:5), the reign promised at 5:10 and shared with Christ at 20:4, 6, here stated in its final, unending form. Service and reign are not two destinies distributed among two classes; the same subjects bear both verbs in consecutive verses. [Explicit text, HIGH, for the simultaneity of service and reign; study 11, finding 6; axiom VIII.5.] The Adamic commission (rule, Genesis 1:26-28) and the priestly commission (serve and keep, Genesis 2:15) are both fulfilled, permanently, by the same people at the same time. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH, for the commission linkage; study 11, sec. 3.6.]

The framing of the whole is the treatise's archetype, at its own calibration: rest in the consummation is not inactivity, and rule is not autonomy; both verbs describe a communion in which being God's own and exercising dominion coincide permanently, the created image of the triune life (axiom I.2) confirmed in act. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH, for the communion framing on study 11's basis; the archetype axiom itself stands at MEDIUM as carried by Part I, and this Part does not raise it.] Augustine's two cities give the framing its civic form (City of God XIV.28, Dods translation, CONSULTED DIRECTLY, entry VIII-6): two loves made two cities, the earthly city by love of self to the contempt of God, amor sui usque ad contemptum Dei, and the heavenly city by love of God to the contempt of self. The consummated city of Revelation 21-22 is the second of those cities brought to its confirmed state: the city formed by the love of God, now with nothing common at its gates and the practitioners of the other love outside them (22:15). (The Latin sentence's wording is confirmed verbatim by independent academic citations; the CCL 48 page is reported inconsistently in the sources and is not cited here; see sec. 11.)

Against the reign stands the entrance, and the entrance is gift. Inscription in the Lamb's book of life is the sole stated ground of entry (21:27): the verse that excludes everything common admits, in its third member, only "those written in the Lamb's book of life." And the right, exousia, to the tree of life belongs to "those who wash their robes" (22:14): access to the tree that was barred by cherubim and flaming sword (Genesis 3:22-24) is now a granted right, and the gates through which the blessed enter are never shut (21:25). The consummated state is populated by gift and cleansing, not by attainment, and Part VIII must not let the reign of 22:5 be misread as the reward-structure of an achievement. [Explicit text, HIGH, subject to the textual note on 22:14; study 11, finding 7; axiom VIII.6.] The textual note is printed, not buried: the critical text reads hoi plynontes tas stolas auton, "those who wash their robes," attested by Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, and the Vulgate; the Received Text reads hoi poiountes tas entolas autou, "those who do his commandments." The Part follows the critical reading, which also coheres with the book's own washed-robes figure at 7:14. Were the Received reading original, this section's gift framing would need restating in terms of the commandments kept; the inscription ground of 21:27, on which the gift structure chiefly rests, would still stand.

Thesis T-VIII.4 is thereby stated in full: the consummated life is communion in act, priestly service and royal reign together, entered by gift.

## 8. Adams's demand taken up: the defeat of evil in the sufferer's own life

Part V closed its counter-positions section with an engagement it expressly did not complete. Marilyn McCord Adams (Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God; register, Part V, entry 23, NOT YET CONSULTED) is registered there as "the interlocutor this Part's framework claim most needs and has not yet taken up: the demand that God defeat evil not merely globally but within the sufferer's own life, so that participation in horrendous evil is itself woven into a life that is, for that person, a great good." Part V recorded the gap honestly: its governing premise specifies the good promised to the called as a body in the decree chain, and its denial of actually gratuitous suffering (axiom V.4, synthesis at MEDIUM) operates at the level of the whole history. Neither, as Part V left them, yet says what God does about the horror inside the one life that suffered it. Adams's demand is stated here in the registered form, since her text has not been opened; the Part presses the engagement exactly as far as the consummation texts carry it, and no further.

What the texts supply is, first, that the defeat they describe is personal in its grammar, not merely global in its bookkeeping. The abolitions of 21:4 are predicated of the inhabitants' own experience: death, mourning, crying, and pain are the sufferer's own states, and each is abolished in the life of the people who had them, not averaged away across a cosmos. The verse's first clause is an act of God's own hand directed at the sufferer's own face: "he will wipe away every tear from their eyes." The dwelling is likewise personal in its form: "the dwelling of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his peoples (laoi), and God himself shall be with them" (21:3), the covenant formula of Leviticus 26:11-12 and Ezekiel 37:27 in its consummated form, universalized to the redeemed nations. The inhabitants see his face and bear his name on their foreheads (22:4): the defeat's positive content is unmediated communion with God himself, which on the treatise's archetype (axiom I.2) is not one good among others but the good from which the moral order is read. And the leaves of the tree are "for the healing of the nations," eis therapeian ton ethnon (22:2): read alongside 21:4, the leaves cannot signify residual sickness inside the consummation, and the study takes them as the standing sign of the nations' wholeness, drawn from Ezekiel 47:12's form. [Synthesis, MEDIUM: the sign-reading is an inference from the tension with 21:4, stated as an inference; study 11, sec. 3.7.] A defeat stated as presence, tears personally wiped, and wholeness signified is not the merely global defeat Adams's demand refuses: her objection is to a theodicy in which the individual's horror is outweighed by goods in other lives or in the whole, and Revelation's grammar of defeat runs in the opposite direction, from God's presence to this people's own abolished sorrows.

What the texts do not supply must be stated with the same plainness. Adams's demand in its strongest form is not only that suffering cease and be healed, but that the horrendous evil itself be defeated within the sufferer's own life-story: taken up and woven in, so that the life, including its participation in the horror, is a great good for the one who lived it. Revelation 21-22 does not describe that retrospective integration. No clause in the two chapters speaks of the redeemed remembering their horrors transfigured, or valuing their lives partly because of what was suffered and overcome within them; the chapters promise abolition of sorrow's causes, the wiping of tears, and the presence of God, and on the specific question of memory's eschatological healing they are silent, as they are silent on confirmation's mechanism (sec. 4). Nor is the scope Adams's: the defeat described is the defeat of the inscribed inhabitants, the called of the governing premise; her demand addressed to every sufferer as such exceeds what these chapters promise, and Part V's universal denial of actual gratuitousness is a different claim at a different level (the level rule, axiom X.1), which this Part does not conflate with it.

The engagement therefore advances one argued step and stops where the evidence stops. Advanced: the consummation's defeat of evil is personal and experiential in its stated form, with God himself as its content, which answers the global-balancing form of the objection from the registered statement. Open: the integration form of the demand, whether God defeats horrendous evil by weaving it into the sufferer's own good life rather than by abolishing its sorrows and healing its wounds past it, is not answered by these chapters and awaits both Adams's own text (still unopened) and any wider-canonical argument Part X can mount. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM, for the advance as stated; the demand is recorded as an open engagement, narrowed and located, not closed.]

## 9. Counter-positions in strongest form

### 9.1 Full discontinuity: the first creation annihilated and replaced

*Strongest form.* The first heaven and earth "passed away" (21:1) as the heavens and earth of 2 Peter 3:10-13 are dissolved in fire; the sea, a major feature of the present earth, is simply gone; the repeated "no more" pattern describes a world with no material continuity with this one; and "new," even kainos, can denote a fresh creation, as a new garment replaces an old. The reading protects the passage's absolute negations from being diluted into renovation. *Adjudication (study 11, reading 1).* Accepted: the discontinuity of conditions is real and total; death, curse, mourning, sea, and night do not survive the transition in any measure. Declined: annihilation of the totality is not what the text's verbs say, for the reasons section 6 gives (apelthan is a departure verb; kaina poio panta takes the same totality as its object). Second Peter's dissolution language describes the same event from the side of the old order's perishable elements; it is not part of this unit's text. [Declined as an account of this text's own verbs, HIGH for the grammatical points; study 11, sec. 4.]

### 9.2 The purely symbolic-ecclesial reading

*Strongest form.* The city is expressly identified as people, "the bride, the wife of the Lamb" (21:9-10); Hebrews 12:22 tells present believers "you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem"; Galatians 4:26 calls the Jerusalem above "our mother"; the gates never shut and the nations stream in read naturally as the church's present mission. On this reading chapters 21-22 are the church's self-portrait under the figure of a city, and "no more death" is proleptic language. *Adjudication (study 11, reading 3).* Accepted: the city's identity as the people of God, and the inaugurated presence of the reality; Hebrews 12:22 is a genuine already. Declined: the evacuation of the future and cosmic referent. The passage's own sequence forbids it: the vision follows the final judgment and the destruction of death (20:11-15), and its central negation, "death shall be no more," is falsified by every churchyard if it is predicated of the present age. Inauguration and consummation are compatible; only the second is this text's subject. [Partially accepted, HIGH on the sequence point; study 11, sec. 4.]

### 9.3 The conditionalist reading of the exclusion texts

*Strongest form.* The exclusion texts state a boundary, not a metaphysics of endless duration. The second death (21:8) is death: the finally excluded perish, and their exclusion from the city (21:27; 22:15, "outside") is exclusion from life itself, so that the consummated state's permanence is the permanence of life for its inhabitants in a universe from which evil has been removed by the destruction of its agents, not preserved forever alongside the blessed. On this reading the negation chain is, if anything, strengthened: the cursed thing is no more because its bearers are no more. It must be recorded that no conditionalist primary text stands in this Part's register; the position is stated in its standard form, and nothing in this adjudication pretends to a direct reading the project has not done. *Adjudication.* This Part's theses do not decide the question, and the Part says so rather than borrowing strength it has not earned. T-VIII.1 concerns the state's permanence and T-VIII.2 the inhabitants' confirmation; both stand on either eschatology of the lost, since both are claims about the city and its people, argued from the negation chain, the duration formula of 22:5, and the decree chain. Conceded to the reading: the exclusion texts are boundary statements, and 21:27 and 22:15 by themselves are not duration treatises. Registered in the other direction, without building on it: the duration formula eis tous aionas ton aionon is used elsewhere in this book of the torment of the beast's party (20:10; cf. 14:11), a canonical observation any conditionalist account of Revelation must also carry; its adjudication belongs to Part X's objections register, where the duration-of-punishment question properly sits, and no thesis of this Part depends on the outcome.

### 9.4 The universalist reading of the nations and the kings

*Strongest form.* The vision's movement is centripetal and, on its face, unfinished: the nations walk by the city's light and the kings of the earth, the same class that made war on the Lamb (19:19), bring their glory into it (21:24, 26); the gates are never shut (21:25); the tree's leaves are for the healing of the nations (22:2), and healing implies patients, nations whose wounding under the curse is being undone after the judgment the vision has already narrated. On this reading the boundary texts mark the city's present holiness, while the open gates and the healing leaves hold out the vision's final word as restoration without remainder. *Adjudication.* The observation is partly this Part's own evidence: the glory of the nations arriving in the city is a member of the continuity thesis (sec. 6), and the healing of the nations is a genuinely restorative image whose force the sign-reading of section 8 preserves rather than denies. But the boundary statements stand in the same vision and are absolute in form: ou me eiselthe, by no means shall enter (21:27); the practitioners of abomination and falsehood are outside (22:15); the second death is the portion of the unbelieving (21:8); and the sole stated ground of entrance is inscription in the Lamb's book of life (21:27). The universalist reading must treat the inscription as finally universal, a claim the text nowhere states; the text's kings who enter are the inscribed, and the text does not narrate a second, post-judgment inscription. The gates stand open, on the passage's own causal frame, because there is no night there (21:25): the openness signifies security, the absence of any threat to shut against, not porosity in the boundary that the same chapter states with the language's strongest negation. What the reading rightly protects, the genuine universality of the vision's horizon (peoples, nations, kings, the whole totality made new), this Part protects inside its continuity thesis; what it adds beyond the text, a finally empty outside, the text's own boundary refuses.

### 9.5 The soul-making completion

*Strongest form, as registered in Part V (sec. 7.5 there).* Hick's Irenaean two-stage account, carried eschatologically, makes the consummation the guaranteed completion of a soul-making process extended beyond death until all are perfected: the end is universal because the process does not stop at the grave, and a God of love does not abandon the project at any creature's final refusal. Hick's text is NOT YET CONSULTED (register, Part V, entry 24), and the statement here is the registered form only. *Adjudication.* Two differences control, one from each end of the treatise. From the beginning: the first state on this treatise's reading is very good, upright, and sufficient, and the fall a culpable defection from it (Part III; Part V, sec. 7.5), whereas the soul-making scheme requires the first stage's imperfection as a designed feature; the resemblance between the two accounts is structural (two stages) and the substance is opposed. From the end: Revelation's consummation follows the judgment and is described with a fixed boundary and a completed negation chain (secs. 2-3); inside the city there is fruitfulness without season (22:2), but no probation, and across the boundary the text narrates no continued process, only the outside of 22:15 and the second death of 21:8. A completion that continues until none are left outside is a claim the anchor text does not make, and the Part declines to make it for the text.
