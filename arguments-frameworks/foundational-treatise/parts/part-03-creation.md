## 1. The question of this Part and its theses

Part II stated God's one good counsel. This Part states what that counsel first produced: what did God make, and in what state did he make it? The question is narrower than it sounds, and more consequential. The treatise's theodicy (Part V) requires that evil's origin not be located in the creation itself; its account of the Fall (Part IV) requires that Adam's fall be his own act and not the unfolding of a built-in flaw; its account of the consummation (Part VIII) requires that the end be measured against the beginning without collapsing the two into one state described twice. All three requirements are decided here, by what Genesis 1:31 does and does not say about the state in which creation began.

The Part's theses, as the derivation map states them, are two.

**T-III.1.** The canon's creation arc runs from a first state very good and mutable to a final state confirmed and incorruptible, and the end exceeds the beginning on Eden's own terms. Depends on axioms III.1, III.2, III.3, VIII.2.

**T-III.2.** Evil's origin is not in creation: the first state was upright and non-defective, and its mutability was a capacity, not a flaw. Depends on axioms III.4, III.5.

Three limits govern the argument and are stated at the outset. First, the lexical claims rest on BDB-level work as reproduced in standard tools at one remove; HALOT and TWOT are registered but not yet consulted (register, Part III, entries 7-9), and no lexical claim in this Part is cited as lexicon-complete. Second, the two-states thesis itself stands at **Theological synthesis, MEDIUM** on study 01's basis, rising to **MEDIUM-HIGH as the joint assessment** when study 11's end of the arc is joined to it (axiom III.3); this Part prints both ratings where each applies and argues the thesis as convergence, never as a lexical entailment of Genesis 1:31 alone. Third, the two broad-corpus positions nearest this Part's thesis, Irenaeus on Adam's created immaturity and Vos on Eden's pre-redemptive eschatology, stand at NOT YET CONSULTED (register entries 2 and 12); the Part therefore makes no originality claim for the two-states account, and its contribution statement (sec. 10) is sized accordingly.

## 2. The verdict in its place: Genesis 1:31

"And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31, ESV). The Hebrew clause is *we-hinneh tov meod*, "and behold, good exceedingly."

Study 01 fixes the clause's position in its chapter, and the position carries weight. The clause is the seventh and climactic divine evaluation in Genesis 1, after the evaluations of light (1:4), earth and seas (1:10), vegetation (1:12), the luminaries (1:18), the water and sky creatures (1:21), and the land creatures (1:25). Two features distinguish it from the previous six. Its scope is comprehensive: "everything that he had made," the whole taken together, not one domain at a time. And it alone carries the intensifier *meod*. No significant textual variant affects the clause.

The timing matters as much as the scope. Genesis 1 evaluates each work as it is made, before the narrative introduces any human act, obedience, or failure. The verdict is God's own, pronounced over a finished creative act, inside a chapter whose verbs are speaking, separating, making, setting, ruling, blessing, and sanctifying. Nothing in the chapter speaks of testing, probation, growth, or consummation; those categories enter the narrative in Genesis 2, with the command, the "not good" of 2:18, and the garden given to be worked and kept, and they are consummated only in Revelation 21-22. The exegetical question of this Part is therefore precise: what does *tov meod* itself claim about the state it approves, and what does it leave unclaimed? The answer comes in three movements: the word's own sense (sec. 3), the vocabulary the chapter does not use (sec. 4), and the canonical controls that locate the verdict inside the whole canon (sec. 5).

## 3. What tov meod says

### 3.1 The senses of tov in Genesis 1

*Tov* (Strong's H2896, adjective; root verb H2895) is among the most frequent adjectives in the Hebrew Bible, about 740 occurrences counting verb and adjective together (Barscevski 2023, as cited in study 01). BDB's sense structure for the adjective runs: good, pleasant, agreeable; pleasant to the senses; pleasant to the higher nature; good, excellent of its kind; good, rich, valuable in estimation; good, appropriate, becoming; kind, benign; good, right (ethical). As a noun: a good thing, benefit, welfare, prosperity.

The word's distribution inside Genesis itself displays the range and selects the operative senses. In Genesis 1 the word falls on light, dry land, vegetation, luminaries, and creatures, and finally on the totality: entities most of which are not moral agents and cannot be ethically right or wrong. Elsewhere in the book it marks fitness for a purpose (2:9, trees good for food; 2:12, the gold of the land is good, that is, of high quality); the ethical register (2:9, 17, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; 3:5-6); beneficial outcome in providence (50:20, "God meant it for good," study 03's text); and, negated, a created state lacking something proper to it (2:18, sec. 5.1 below).

Study 01's finding is that in Genesis 1 the operative senses are *excellent of its kind*, *appropriate and fitting*, and *approved by God*, applied to non-moral entities as much as to the whole; the ethical sense the word can bear elsewhere is not the sense the chapter's usage selects. [Lexical, HIGH; study 01, finding 2; axiom III.1.] The verdict is therefore real and strong: the finished creation, in every part and in the whole, is what its Maker approves, excellent in the kind to which each thing belongs. What the verdict is not, lexically, is a verdict on the creation's permanence, its confirmability, or its finality. Those are different claims, and Hebrew had different words for them (sec. 4).

### 3.2 Meod: degree, not category

*Meod* (Strong's H3966) is an adverbial intensifier: "exceedingly, very." In Genesis 1:31 it raises the comprehensive evaluation above the six preceding ones: the whole is better approved than any part severally, because the whole is the parts in their ordered togetherness, and the order itself is approved. But the intensifier intensifies degree; it does not change category. *Tov meod* claims that the whole is very good, not that the whole belongs to a different order of goodness than the parts had severally. [Lexical, HIGH; study 01, finding 3.]

This small finding excludes a large reading. If *meod* changed the category of the goodness asserted, from fitting excellence to maximal and unsurpassable perfection, then any later state could at most restore Eden and never exceed it, and the canon's own end (sec. 6.2) would be capped by its beginning. The lexicon gives no warrant for loading the intensifier with that freight. The word says the verdict is emphatic; it does not say the state is final.

### 3.3 The lexical limit

The negative finding is stated as an axiom because so much is built on it:

**III.2 (The lexical limit).** The goodness vocabulary of Genesis 1 does not lexically encode incorruptible, immutable, or consummated; the available wholeness vocabulary (tamim, shalem) is not used of creation in Genesis 1-2, and shalem in all its uses describes completion reached, not a beginning. [Lexical and Lexical distribution, HIGH; study 01, findings 2, 4.]

The claim is carefully bounded. It does not say Genesis 1:31 teaches mutability; a verse's silence about confirmation is not a doctrine of unconfirmation. It says the verse does not lexically teach *incorruptibility*, and that any account of the first state, whether it affirms or denies the two-states reading, must get its doctrine of Adam's mutability or immutability from somewhere else: from the narrative of Genesis 2-3, from the canonical controls, from 1 Corinthians 15, or from the tradition's loci. Section 4 now shows why the silence is a datum and not merely an absence: the words for the stronger claim existed, were used elsewhere in the Torah for exactly such claims, and were not used here.

## 4. The vocabulary Genesis does not use

### 4.1 Tamim: wholeness without defect

*Tamim* (Strong's H8549, from tamam, to be complete) is BDB's "complete, sound," about 91 occurrences. Its usage clusters in study 01 are these. The largest cluster is sacrificial and cultic: "without blemish, without defect" (Exodus 12:5; Leviticus and Ezekiel passim). A second cluster concerns persons: Noah, "righteous, tamim in his generations" (Genesis 6:9), where the NET textual note observes that of men the word denotes blamelessness as integrity and non-violation in context, not sinless perfection; the command to Abraham, "walk before me, and be tamim" (Genesis 17:1); and Israel, "you shall be tamim with the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 18:13). A third cluster is God and his works: "his work is tamim" (Deuteronomy 32:4); God's way (Psalm 18:30); God's law (Psalm 19:7). A fourth is time and measure: a whole day (Joshua 10:13), complete Sabbaths and years (Leviticus 23:15; 25:30).

Hebrew therefore possesses, and the Torah uses, a word for wholeness-without-defect, applied to sacrificial animals, to men in their generations, and supremely, without qualification, to God's own work. That word is never applied to Adam, to Eden, or to the created order in Genesis 1-2. [Lexical distribution, HIGH; study 01, finding 4.]

### 4.2 Shalem: completion reached

*Shalem* (Strong's H8003, from shalam, to be complete) is BDB's "complete, safe, at peace," about 28 occurrences. Its clusters are: fullness of measure ("the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet shalem," Genesis 15:16; a full weight and full measure, Deuteronomy 25:15; a full reward, Ruth 2:12); finished work (stones finished at the quarry, 1 Kings 6:7; the temple finished, 2 Chronicles 8:16); wholeness of state (whole, unhewn stones, Deuteronomy 27:6; Joshua 8:31; a person arriving shalem, safe and whole, Genesis 33:18); and the undivided heart ("let your heart be shalem with the LORD your God," 1 Kings 8:61, the standing royal test at 1 Kings 11:4; 15:3).

Study 01's finding is that *shalem* is the vocabulary of completion reached: a measure that has filled up, a work that is finished, a heart that is undivided. None of its uses describes a beginning. Genesis 1:31, at the beginning, does not use it. [Lexical, HIGH; study 01, finding 4.]

### 4.3 The weight of a distribution

An argument from vocabulary selection is an argument from silence at one remove, and this Part weights it exactly as study 01 weights it. The distribution is a fact, and a strong one: across Genesis 1-2, over against a chapter that evaluates seven times, the wholeness words never appear. The inference from the distribution, that Genesis 1:31 does not assert defect-free consummation, is corroboration, not proof: it "corroborates, and does not by itself prove, that Genesis 1:31 does not assert defect-free consummation" (study 01, sec. 3.3), and the two-states synthesis built partly on it is rated MEDIUM from this side for that reason among others (study 01, sec. 3.5). What the distribution does decisively is shift the burden. A reading that finds consummation inside *tov meod* must explain why the text, possessing *tamim* and *shalem* and using *tamim* absolutely of God's own work at Deuteronomy 32:4, chose neither; a reading that finds only fitting excellence in the verse needs no such explanation, because it claims only what the chosen word says. Burden-shifting is not proof, and this Part does not present it as proof. It is the first line of a convergence whose remaining lines are canonical (sec. 5) and eschatological (sec. 6.2), and the convergence, not any single line, carries the thesis.

## 5. The canonical controls

The lexical work fixes what Genesis 1:31 says and leaves unsaid. It does not by itself describe the first state positively. That description comes from the canon's own controls, each of which study 01 assembles and each of which contributes a distinct feature to the portrait: Genesis 2:18 contributes incompletion-without-defect; Ecclesiastes 7:29 contributes uprightness; the image texts contribute the state's relational constitution.

### 5.1 Genesis 2:18: a "not good" inside the good creation

Within the good creation, before any sin, God pronounces one state "not good": "It is not good that the man should be alone" (Genesis 2:18). The lack is real: something proper to the man's created design is not yet present. And the lack is supplied inside the creation order itself (2:21-24), so that the comprehensive *tov meod* of 1:31, which in the narrative's canonical order frames the whole, is spoken over a creation in which the supply belongs. Study 01's control finding: *tov* evaluation in Genesis operates relative to completeness of design; a thing can be genuinely of God's making, and the narrative can still register that something proper to it is not yet present. [Explicit text, HIGH; study 01, sec. 4.]

Two disciplines follow. First, the control is not read backward into 1:31 as though the verse were spoken over the solitary man; in canonical sequence the verdict of 1:31 follows the woman's creation (1:27), and the "not good" of 2:18 is the narrative's own flashback exposition of what the verdict's world contains. The point is about the semantics of *tov* in Genesis, not about a defect in the finished whole. Second, the control establishes the conceptual space this Part needs: in Genesis's own usage, "not yet complete" and "defective" are not synonyms. A created state can be genuinely good, genuinely God's, and genuinely awaiting a supply proper to it, all at once. The two-states thesis will ask for exactly this space at the scale of the whole creation: a first state that is very good and unconfirmed, awaiting a confirmation proper to its end, is not thereby a defective state. Genesis 2:18 does not prove that the whole creation stood in that posture; it proves that the posture is coherent within Genesis's own evaluative vocabulary, which is what a control is for.

### 5.2 Ecclesiastes 7:29: upright

"See, this alone I found, that God made man upright (yashar), but they have sought out many schemes" (Ecclesiastes 7:29). Study 01 is explicit about the division of labor: the treatise's claim of original uprightness rests here and on WCF 4.2, not on *tov*. [Explicit text, HIGH; study 01, sec. 4.] The verse contributes what the lexical study of Genesis 1 could not: a direct canonical predicate for man's original moral posture. *Yashar* is straightness, the opposite of crookedness; man was made straight and himself devised the bend. The verse also assigns the direction of causation that Part IV will develop: the seeking out of schemes is man's act, subsequent to a making that was upright. For Part III the contribution is the predicate itself. The first state was not merely approved as excellent of kind; its human head was upright, and the uprightness is attested by a text whose whole point is to contrast the making with the seeking.

### 5.3 The image and the relational constitution

The work package assigns Part III the imago Dei and the relational constitution of humanity alongside the states of creation, and the Part takes them at the level the sources support. Man is made in God's image and likeness (Genesis 1:26-27), male and female, blessed and commissioned to fruitfulness and rule (1:28). [Explicit text.] The image's moral content, knowledge, righteousness, and holiness, is specified where the canon describes the image renewed: "created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness" (Ephesians 4:24) and "created in knowledge after the image of its creator" (Colossians 3:10). This is the traditional Reformed reading carried at WCF 4.2, received by study 01 in substance (sec. 7.3 below), with the study's narrowing kept: those attributes rest on the wider canon, not on *tov*, and the Part cites them on those texts. The relational constitution is Genesis 2's own exposition of the image: the man is formed for a communion he does not supply himself (2:18), receives the woman as "bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh" (2:23), and the two become one flesh (2:24), the whole set inside the garden given "to work it and keep it" (2:15), the priestly pair of verbs whose consummated form study 11 finds in Revelation 22:3-5. That humanity images the triune archetype of Part I in its relational constitution is the treatise's framing synthesis (axiom I.2, Theological synthesis, MEDIUM), and Part III presupposes it at that tag rather than re-arguing it; what Part III adds is only that the constitution belongs to the first state as created: relationality is not a remedy introduced after the Fall but a feature of the state God called very good.

## 6. The two states stated

### 6.1 The first state assembled

The elements of the first state can now be assembled, each on its own basis and at its own tag.

- **Very good.** The finished creation, in every part and in the whole, is fitting and excellent of its kind, approved by God (Genesis 1:31). [Explicit text and Lexical, HIGH; axiom III.1.]
- **Upright.** Man was made yashar, upright (Ecclesiastes 7:29), in knowledge, righteousness, and holiness after God's image (Ephesians 4:24; Colossians 3:10; WCF 4.2 received in substance). [Explicit text, HIGH; axiom III.5's rectitude member.]
- **Mutable.** The goodness vocabulary of Genesis 1 asserts no incorruptibility or immutability (sec. 3.3), the wholeness vocabulary is withheld (sec. 4), and the tradition's form of the state (sec. 7) is an upright will pliable in either direction, with no constancy to persevere received. Mutability here names the state's real openness in both directions: Adam could stand and could fall. [The openness is Theological synthesis as assembled, with its tradition loci at HIGH as reports (axiom III.5).]
- **Unconfirmed.** The first state carried no confirmation: no securing of the will in righteousness such that it could not fall, and no securing of life such that it could not be lost. Confirmation is the property of the second state (sec. 6.2), and its absence from the first is the lexical and traditional burden of sections 3, 4, and 7 jointly, not of any one text.

Assembled: **creation's first state is very good, upright, and mutable** (axiom III.3, first member). The three adjectives are not three praises of the same kind. The first is God's verdict on the work; the second is the canon's predicate for man's posture; the third is the state's modal character, the feature that makes the narrative of Genesis 3 possible without making it necessary.

### 6.2 The second state, read back under discipline

Part VIII carries the consummation's argument in full. Part III needs only the arc's far end, stated at study 11's calibrations and under the audit's disciplines, because thesis T-III.1 is a claim about the arc and not about either end alone.

Study 11's finding 5 states the joint verdict: "The consummation exceeds Eden on Eden's own terms. Where Genesis 1:31's tov meod asserted fitting excellence without the vocabulary of incorruption (unit 01, finding 2), Revelation 21-22 describes the end with exactly the vocabulary Genesis withheld: death abolished, curse abolished, entrance sealed against everything un-sanctified, reign measured by 'forever and ever.' The canonical trajectory from tov meod to the consummation is an increase in security and glory, not a restoration to a lost maximum and nothing more." [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH as the joint assessment; study 11, finding 5; axiom III.3.] The study's supporting findings are the negation chain (21:4: death, mourning, crying, and pain no more; 22:3: every cursed thing, pan katathema, no more; 21:1: the sea no more; 22:5: night no more) [Explicit text, HIGH; study 11, finding 1], and the permanence inference: a state that excludes every consequence of the Fall, every agent of corruption, and every occasion of a renewed fall, under the immediate presence of God and the Lamb and for the maximum duration, "has no fall left to undergo" [Necessary inference, HIGH; study 11, finding 2; axiom VIII.2].

Two disciplines bind this reading-back, and the Part observes both in the sentence where it uses study 11. First, the permanence of the state (axiom VIII.2, Necessary inference, HIGH) and the label for the saints' interior condition, confirmed and non posse peccare (axiom VIII.3, Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH), are distinct claims at distinct ratings, and no argument in this Part spends the first rating on the second; the confirmation label and its mechanism belong to Part VIII, and Revelation itself supplies no mechanism (study 11, finding 4(iii); axiom VIII.7). Second, the two states are states of the same created totality under different orders (audit item (xi)): study 11's verdict on continuity is discontinuity of the order and continuity of the totality, on the strength of both its verb families, the departure verb apelthan and God's own summary, kaina poio panta, "I make all things new" (21:5) [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH on grammatical observations rated HIGH; study 11, finding 3; axiom VIII.4]. The arc is therefore not replacement of one world by an unrelated second, nor the first world's mere repair: it is one creation, first unconfirmed and then confirmed, and the difference between its states is a difference of order within a single history.

### 6.3 Convergence, not proof-texting

The two-states thesis (axiom III.3) is now statable in its final form for this Part: **the canon's creation arc runs from a first state very good, upright, and mutable to a final state confirmed and incorruptible, and the end exceeds the beginning on Eden's own terms** (T-III.1). Its support is a convergence of five lines, no one of which is presented as bearing the thesis alone:

1. The lexical limit: *tov meod* asserts fitting excellence and does not lexically encode incorruptible, immutable, or consummated (study 01, findings 2-3). [HIGH]
2. The withheld vocabulary: *tamim* and *shalem*, available and used elsewhere for wholeness and completion, are not used of creation in Genesis 1-2 (study 01, finding 4). [Distribution HIGH; its inferential use corroborative]
3. Incompletion without defect inside the good creation: Genesis 2:18's "not good," supplied within the order (study 01, sec. 4). [HIGH]
4. Uprightness without confirmation in the controls: Ecclesiastes 7:29's yashar, whose contrast is with man's own later seeking, not with a defect in the making (study 01, sec. 4). [HIGH]
5. The two Adams and the two bodies: 1 Corinthians 15:42-49 sets the perishable sowing against the imperishable raising (phthora against aphtharsia), the natural body (soma psychikon) against the spiritual body (soma pneumatikon), the man of dust against the man from heaven, and concludes that the image of the man of heaven is borne after, and beyond, the image of the man of dust; and Revelation 21-22 then describes the end in the incorruption vocabulary Genesis withheld (studies 01, sec. 4; 11, finding 5). [The trajectory as Theological synthesis, MEDIUM from study 01's side]

The thesis is tagged, in full: **Theological synthesis, MEDIUM** on the Genesis-side evidence (study 01, finding 5), **MEDIUM-HIGH as the joint assessment** with study 11 (axiom III.3). It is offered as a synthesis because that is what it is: the canon nowhere states "creation had two states" in a single sentence, and the treatise does not pretend otherwise. What the canon does, on the showing above, is describe a beginning in the vocabulary of fitting excellence and an end in the vocabulary of incorruption, with a narrative between them in which the beginning's head falls by his own will; the two-states account is the treatise's name for that described arc, and its claim is that the name clarifies, and does not add to, what the texts severally say.

## 7. Adam's state in the tradition's form

The exegetical assembly of section 6 is not the treatise's invention; it is the form in which Augustine stated the first state and Calvin restated it, and the Part now sets the two loci side by side with the exegesis, under the edition discipline of the research note on Augustine and Calvin. The claim made for the loci is a report claim: that the tradition's two most load-bearing witnesses state Adam's state as upright, able in both directions, aided but not confirmed. [Theological synthesis, HIGH as a report of the loci; axiom III.5.]

### 7.1 Augustine: the first liberty and the leavable aid

Augustine's primary locus is De correptione et gratia (On Rebuke and Grace), ch. 12, sec. 33, read in the Latin (Migne text, catholiclibrary.org reproduction) and in Wallis's translation (NPNF First Series, vol. 5), both DIRECT in the research note. His verified verbatim collocations are the pairs: "Prima ergo libertas voluntatis erat, posse non peccare; novissima erit multo major, non posse peccare: prima immortalitas erat, posse non mori; novissima erit multo major, non posse mori: prima erat perseverantiae potestas, bonum posse non deserere; novissima erit felicitas perseverantiae, bonum non posse deserere." Wallis: "Therefore the first liberty of the will was to be able not to sin, the last will be much greater, not to be able to sin; the first immortality was to be able not to die, the last will be much greater, not to be able to die; the first was the power of perseverance, to be able not to forsake good - the last will be the felicity of perseverance, not to be able to forsake good." The treatise's tag for state (a) is the tradition's fuller form of these pairs, posse peccare et posse non peccare (Prolegomena, sec. 6): Adam was able to sin and able not to sin. Augustine's own sentence carries the same content in the posse non peccare member, the posse peccare being the presupposition of the aid's being leavable at all.

The mechanism is the aid, and Augustine distinguishes its kinds with care (De correptione et gratia chs. 11-12). Wallis, ch. 12, DIRECT: "The aid without which a thing does not come to pass is one thing, and the aid by which a thing comes to pass is another. ... Therefore to the first man, who, in that good in which he had been made upright, had received the ability not to sin, the ability not to die, the ability not to forsake that good itself, was given the aid of perseverance, not that by which it should be brought about that he should persevere, but that without which he could not of free will persevere." Adam's aid was an adjutorium sine quo non, an aid without which he could not persevere, but not an aid by which his perseverance was effected: he could forsake it, and the forsaking would be his. The contrast Augustine draws is with the second Adam's gift, by which it is "even effected that he will" (ch. 11, DIRECT), and with the angels who stood and received as reward "such a fulness of blessing that by it they might have the fullest certainty of always abiding in it," under the maxim, "For what shall be more free than free will, when it shall not be able to serve sin?" (ch. 11, sec. 32, DIRECT). Adam received no such certainty-bestowing fullness. His state was the first liberty, not the last.

The Enchiridion corroborates in Outler's translation (Library of Christian Classics, at CCEL), DIRECT. Sec. 107: "Man was, therefore, made upright, and in such a fashion that he could either continue in that uprightness - though not without divine aid - or become perverted by his own choice." Sec. 105 states the arc's two ends together: "in the future life he will not have the power to will evil; and yet this will not thereby restrict his free will. Indeed, his will will be much freer, because he will then have no power whatever to serve sin." Two of this Part's theses are already Augustine's sentences: the making was upright (sec. 107, T-III.2), and the end exceeds the beginning in liberty itself (sec. 105, T-III.1). The four-state scheme of which state (a) is the first member is printed in the Prolegomena (sec. 6) with its tag discipline; this Part adds only the standing caution that the tag for state (b), non posse non peccare, was not found verbatim in any Augustinian text opened and is the later tradition's label for his prose (research note, sec. 1.2) - a caution that does not touch state (a), whose posse vocabulary is Augustine's own in the Latin quoted above.

### 7.2 Calvin: highest rectitude, no constancy

Calvin's Institutes 1.15.8 is, in the research note's judgment, his exact counterpart to Augustine's state (a). Beveridge translation (1845), DIRECT (sermonindex.net and worthychristianbooks.com reproductions of the same text): "It were here unseasonable to introduce the question concerning the secret predestination of God, because we are not considering what might or might not happen, but what the nature of man truly was. Adam, therefore, might have stood if he chose, since it was only by his own will that he fell; but it was because his will was pliable in either direction and he had not received constancy to persevere, that he so easily fell. Still he had a free choice of good and evil; and not only so, but in the mind and will there was the highest rectitude..." The sentence continues, in Strong's quotation of the same section (SECONDARY mediation, flagged here as the register flags it): "...and all his organic parts were rightly disposed to obedience, till, destroying himself, he corrupted all his excellencies."

Every member of axiom III.5 is in this paragraph. Uprightness: "the highest rectitude" of mind and will. Real alternative: "might have stood if he chose"; "free choice of good and evil." Mutability: the will "pliable in either direction" (flexibilis, in the note's Latin gloss). Unconfirmation: "he had not received constancy to persevere" (constantia not received). Sole fault: "it was only by his own will that he fell." Calvin's "not received" is the exact negative this Part needs: confirmation is a gift-category in his account, and it is a gift Adam, upright as he was, had not received. What Calvin does not say, and the Part does not make him say, is anything about Genesis 1:31's vocabulary; his own gloss on tov meod in the Commentary on Genesis stands at NOT YET CONSULTED (register entry 6), and no Genesis-specific reading is attributed to him beyond 1.15.8.

### 7.3 The confessional reception, with the study's narrowing

The Westminster Confession's treatment (4.2, on man's creation in knowledge, righteousness, and holiness; 6.1, on the Fall) reaches this Part through study 01's citation (register entry 1, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION), and the Part receives it as study 01 receives it: "Accepted in substance. The study's point is narrower: those attributes rest on the wider canon (Ecclesiastes 7:29; Ephesians 4:24; Colossians 3:10), and conditional immortality itself confirms that Edenic goodness was mutable, which is the treatise's thesis. No conflict." The narrowing is worth printing in the Part's own voice, because it is the point at which the confessional reading and the two-states thesis are seen to be the same claim in two idioms. A confession that makes Adam's immortality conditional on obedience has already denied that the first state was confirmed: what obedience could have retained, disobedience could and did lose, and a state losable by its head's act is a mutable state by definition. The two-states account does not correct the confession here; it names the modal structure the confession's own conditionality presupposes. This is a claim of consistency and consequence from the standards, in the Prolegomena's hierarchy (sec. 3), not a claim that the standards formulate the two-states account, which they do not.

## 8. Eden was not defective

Thesis T-III.2 remains to be defended against the reading it most often provokes. If the first state was mutable and unconfirmed, was it not, on the treatise's own showing, a deficient piece of work: a goodness with a crack in it, a beginning whose failure was a matter of time? The treatise answers no, on four grounds, and prints the answer as a section because the defective-Eden reading, study 01's competing reading 4, is the one misreading of this Part that would poison Parts IV and V if it survived: if Eden was defective, evil's origin is creation after all, and the Fall is the design working as designed.

### 8.1 The verdict forbids the word

The first ground is the verdict itself. *Tov meod* is God's own evaluation of the finished whole, comprehensive in scope and emphatic in degree (secs. 2-3). [Explicit text, HIGH; study 01, finding 6; axiom III.4.] To call the state defective is to enter a contrary evaluation against the only evaluator whose verdict the text records, on evidence the text does not supply: nothing in Genesis 1-2 predicates a flaw, a lack in the workmanship, or a divine reservation about the work. Study 01's finding 6 is stated without qualification: "Nothing in the text or the lexicon supports calling Eden defective." The defective-Eden reading is not an alternative exegesis of Genesis 1:31; it is a refusal of the verse, usually mounted from the fact of the Fall read backward (sec. 8.4). Calvin makes the argument in exactly this form at Institutes 3.23.8, running Genesis 1:31 against the supposition that depravity came from creation (Beveridge, DIRECT, biblestudytools.com reproduction): "The Lord had a little before declared that all the things which he had made were very good (Gen. 1:31). Whence then the depravity of man, which made him revolt from God? Lest it should be supposed that it was from his creation, God had expressly approved what proceeded from himself. Therefore man's own wickedness corrupted the pure nature which he had received from God." The approval is God's express act, and in Calvin's logic it functions as a barrier: what proceeded from God stands approved, and the corruption is assigned to man's own wickedness, not to the approved nature.

### 8.2 Mutability is a capacity, not a flaw

The second ground distinguishes the two things the defective reading conflates. A defect is a privation: the absence, in a thing, of a good proper to its nature and state. Mutability is not an absence; it is a positive capacity, the will's being genuinely able in more than one direction, which is the form creaturely freedom takes before confirmation. The distinction is visible in the tradition's own grammar. Augustine does not describe Adam's posse peccare as a wound in the making; he describes it as the first liberty (libertas), an inferior liberty to glory's, but a liberty and a good of its state (sec. 7.1). Calvin does not describe the pliable will as a flaw in the workmanship; in the same paragraph he calls the mind and will's rectitude "the highest" (sec. 7.2). Both witnesses hold mutability and excellence together without embarrassment, because for both the comparison class is right: Adam is measured against what a creature in the first state ought to be, not against what a confirmed creature in the last state is. To call unconfirmation a defect is to measure a beginning by an end and then blame the beginning for the distance, which is the error section 8.4 names from Genesis 2:18's side. Only God is immutable by nature; that a creature is not God is the definition of creaturehood, not a defect in it. [Theological synthesis, on the loci reported in sec. 7 and the verdict of sec. 8.1.]

### 8.3 Not a setup: the aid was real and the standing was possible

The third ground answers the sharper form of the charge: that an unconfirmed Adam, placed under a command whose breach carried death, was set up to fall, the probation a formality over a foregone conclusion. The tradition's loci refuse the premise. The aid Adam received was a real aid: the adjutorium sine quo non was that "without which he could not of free will persevere," which is to say, with which he could (sec. 7.1). The standing was a real alternative: "Adam, therefore, might have stood if he chose" (Calvin, sec. 7.2), and Augustine's Enchiridion has him able to "continue in that uprightness - though not without divine aid" (sec. 107). What Adam had not received was a gift that would have made his perseverance the gift's effect rather than his act; its absence is the definition of an unconfirmed state, not evidence of a rigged one. The audit holds the two sides of this in one item and this Part prints them together: decree-relative necessity of the Fall (axiom V.5) against Adam's real capacity to stand (axiom III.5), with Calvin held on both sides, since Calvin states both ("might have stood if he chose," 1.15.8; "man falls according as God's providence ordains, but he falls by his own fault," 3.23.8, Battles as quoted in the research note, SECONDARY) (audit item (xvi)). The systematic working-out belongs to Part V; Part III's contribution is the creation-side member in its Calvinian and Augustinian form: the state was such that the Fall, when it came, was the head's own act in a state that did not require it.

### 8.4 "Not yet" is not "defective"

The fourth ground is Genesis's own pattern, from section 5.1. When something proper to a created thing is not yet present, Genesis's word for the situation, from God's own mouth, is "not good" in the sense of not yet complete, and his act is supply, not apology (Genesis 2:18, 21-24). Beginnings in this canon are not judged by their ends and found wanting; they are judged by their Maker's verdict on what they are, and where they are ordered toward more, the more comes as gift in its season. The vocabulary confirms the pattern from the other side: shalem, completion reached, is never used of a beginning anywhere in its distribution (sec. 4.2), as though the lexicon itself declined to measure first things by last things. The first state lacked confirmation the way the solitary man lacked a helper: as a not-yet proper to its place in the order, not as a flaw in its making. The Fall is not the not-yet ripening; it is the head's own turning, and Part IV takes it up on those terms.

## 9. Counter-positions in strongest form

### 9.1 "Very good" as maximal perfection

*Strongest form.* Genesis 1:31 is the climax of the creation account, spoken by God over the completed whole with the canon's strongest intensifier available to the sentence, immediately before the Sabbath rest in which God ceases because the work is finished. On this reading, *tov meod* is a superlative of totality: the creation lacked nothing a creation could have, including security; the fall is therefore an unqualified catastrophe against a maximal state; and the consummation can only restore what was lost, its glories being Eden's glories recovered, not exceeded. The reading's strength is its protection of Eden's honor: it will not let the first state be talked down, and it takes the intensifier with full seriousness. Study 01 declines it "while granting that the reading correctly protects Eden from being called defective" (study 01, sec. 5.1).

*Answer.* The reading loads *tov meod* with the combined content of tamim-absolute and shalem, and the lexical comparison does not support the load (secs. 3-4): the intensifier intensifies degree within the category the adjective supplies (sec. 3.2), the wholeness vocabulary was available and unused (sec. 4), and 1 Corinthians 15 describes the last Adam's state in terms the first Adam's never carried - imperishable, spiritual, heavenly (sec. 6.3, line 5). A restoration-only eschatology must flatten that contrast. What the reading rightly protects, Eden's non-defectiveness, this Part protects on other grounds (sec. 8), so nothing it exists to defend is lost by declining it.

### 9.2 The functional reading: "good" as cosmic-temple function

*Strongest form.* In Genesis 1's ancient Near Eastern frame, "good" evaluates a thing's functioning within an ordered whole, and the chapter's whole is a cosmic temple: the luminaries set to rule, the creatures blessed to fill, mankind installed as the image in the sanctuary. "Very good" then means: everything in its place, functioning as intended, the temple operational. The reading accounts for the verdict's falling on non-moral entities with an economy the ethical senses cannot match, and it anchors the evaluation in the chapter's own architecture (register entry 11, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION through study 01's formulation).

*Answer.* Study 01 accepts the reading "as a component of the sense, not the whole of it: Genesis 50:20 and the ethical uses show tov exceeds bare function" (study 01, sec. 5.3). This Part adopts that adjudication unchanged, and adds only its consequence for the states question: even on the functional sense at full strength, the verdict evaluates operation, not permanence; a temple functioning perfectly on its first day is not thereby a temple that cannot be profaned. The reading is therefore compatible with, and on its own terms neutral toward, the two-states thesis. Its named representatives were not identified in study 01 and stand unopened; the Part engages the position only in the study's formulation and claims no more against it than that formulation supports.

### 9.3 Mutability itself as the problem

*Strongest form.* The Fall was not an accident of biography but the predictable outcome of the design: a being who can fall, placed under a prohibition whose violation ruins everything, in a state admittedly short of confirmation, will fall, and a designer who knows it has built the ruin in. On this reading the locus of the problem of evil moves from man's act to God's specification; Eden's mutability is the defect, whatever Genesis calls it, and the kindest version of the reading adds that Genesis 1:31's verdict is God's approval of a first draft he always intended to supersede. Open and process forms of the reading generalize it: the creation's goodness is provisional because its future is genuinely unwritten, God's own experience of the creation is an adventure into outcomes he does not determine, and the first state is neither confirmed nor confirmable by any divine purpose standing behind it.

*Answer.* In its design-charge form, the reading must explain away three things it cannot digest: God's verdict over the whole, which it reclassifies as approval of a draft against the text's own comprehensive scope (sec. 8.1); the tradition's unanimous grammar in which the pliable-but-upright will is a liberty and an excellence, not a flaw (secs. 7, 8.2); and the real alternative attested by both Augustine's aid and Calvin's "might have stood" (sec. 8.3). The charge that a possible fall, foreknown, is a designed fall is the modal conflation Part V answers on decree ground (axiom V.5; audit item (xvi)); Part III's answer is the creation-side one, that possibility is not defect and foreknowledge is not flaw. In its open-ended form, the reading is answered where this Part can answer it: the canon does narrate the end, in the vocabulary Genesis withheld and under God's own first-person declaration, kaina poio panta (sec. 6.2), so the first state is not the opening of an undecided story but the first state of a two-state arc whose second state the same canon describes. The metaphysical premise of the open reading, that God's counsel does not determine the creation's outcome, is Part II's and Part V's contested ground, registered in their registers; this Part notes the dependence and does not pretend its lexical and canonical work settles it.

### 9.4 The unopened frontier

The register places four positions in this Part's field that this Part does not answer, because they have not been opened, and honesty requires naming them as frontier rather than treating the field as cleared. **Irenaeus** (register entry 2, NOT YET CONSULTED): his picture of Adam as created immature and growing toward a perfection he did not yet possess is the patristic position nearest the two-states account, and the Part's originality for that account first depends on the check. The anticipated point of difference, the distinction between created immaturity and this Part's upright-but-unconfirmed maturity, cannot be argued until Adversus Haereses is read; the Part registers the expectation as an expectation only. **Athanasius** (entry 3, NOT YET CONSULTED): created nature's inherent instability apart from participation in the Word would, if as the register describes it, supply patristic depth for the mutability claim from the side of ontology; unchecked, it is cited for nothing. **Aquinas** (entry 4, NOT YET CONSULTED): his account of man's original rectitude and of whether Adam's state included confirmation is the medieval control on this Part's uprightness-confirmation distinction; the distinction is argued here from Augustine and Calvin, and Aquinas may sharpen or complicate it when opened. **Vos and Bavinck** (entries 12-13, NOT YET CONSULTED): Vos's pre-redemptive eschatology of Eden is the modern Reformed position nearest the two-states account, and Bavinck's treatment of the image and Adam's state is the Dutch Reformed control; both are required register checks on the contribution statement that follows.
