## 1. The question and the theses

Part VII is the treatise's Christological locus, and its question is narrower than Christology at large: how does the one Mediator will, and what follows from the manner of his willing for the restoration of the communion the treatise traces from creation to consummation? Three claims carry the Part, each stated at the calibration the Phase 2 architecture fixed for it.

**T-VII.1.** Christ wills in two natural wills, the human following and subject to the divine without opposition and without suppression, and without a gnomic will. (Axioms VII.1, VII.2, VII.3, VII.4.)

**T-VII.2.** Christ is impeccable, non posse peccare, printed at the calibration of the Phase 2 consistency audit, item (i): HIGH as theological synthesis on the treatise's full premise set, MEDIUM as an inference from Hebrews 4:15 alone, and in neither case a conciliar definition or a confessional article. (Axioms VII.5, VII.6, VII.7; disciplines X.1, X.2.)

**T-VII.3.** In Christ's obedience the decree is executed and communion restored: the will he came down to do is the definite saving purpose of John 6:39-40, to lose nothing of all the Father has given him and to raise it at the last day. (Axiom VII.8; axiom II.7.)

The Part's evidence is organized as the tradition itself organized it. First, the control-text triad of the working paper's section 6.24 (Luke 22:42; John 6:38; Hebrews 4:15), exegeted in Phase 1 as studies 07, 08, and 09 and read here as one dossier (sec. 2). Second, the conciliar settlement of the seventh century, which turned the dossier's data into dogma about the wills while expressly leaving the modal question open (sec. 3), and Maximus the Confessor's analytical apparatus, which the tradition received from that settlement without the council's having defined it (sec. 4). Third, the confessional boundary: what the Westminster standards actually confess at this locus, and what they do not (sec. 5). Fourth, the impeccability inference itself, drawn at its printed calibration, with its strongest historical dissent stated and answered (sec. 6), the remaining counter-positions (sec. 7), and the Part's contribution statement (sec. 8). Open items are collected in sec. 9, including the result of the check this draft was commissioned to run on working paper sections 6.19 and 6.23.

A word on method, because this Part is where the treatise's tagging discipline is most severely tested. The claims in play sit at three different dogmatic tiers, and the whole argument fails if the tiers are blurred: what a council defined (two wills), what a confession confesses (two natures, one person, yet without sin), and what theology infers (impeccability) are three different kinds of claim with three different warrants. The working paper's section 6.24 already observed the distinction; the Phase 2 research note on the two wills verified it against the primary texts; the audit fixed the printed calibrations. Part VII's task is to argue each claim at its own tier and to let the reader see, at every step, which tier is bearing the weight.

## 2. The control triad as one dossier

The three texts were not chosen arbitrarily. Luke 22:42 and John 6:38 are the texts the church's own settlement used: John 6:38 stands in Constantinople III's Definition itself, and the Gethsemane prayer enters the conciliar record through Agatho's dogmatic letter and its florilegium (sec. 3.3). Hebrews 4:15 is the text the Westminster standards themselves cite for "yet without sin" (WCF 8.2), and it is the text on which the sympathy objection to impeccability has always been pressed. Read together, the triad supplies the distinction of wills (Luke, John), the constancy of the alignment (John), the cost of the obedience (Luke; Hebrews 5:7-9), and the sinlessness confessed as an abiding state (Hebrews). No one of the three carries the Part's conclusions alone, and the draft says at each point which member is carrying what.

### 2.1 Luke 22:42: distinction, recoil, and free alignment

Luke's text: "Pater, ei boulei parenegkein touto to poterion ap' emou; plen me to thelema mou alla to son ginestho": "Father, if you are willing, carry away this cup from me; nevertheless, not my will but yours be done" (study 07, sec. 1). Three findings govern the Part's use of the verse.

First, the distinction is explicit text. Jesus predicates thelema of himself in real distinction from the Father's: "to thelema mou" over against "to son," the noun carried over under the second possessive (study 07, sec. 3.4, finding 1; axiom VII.2). The sentence is intelligible only if "my will" names something genuinely his and genuinely distinguishable. A reading on which the human will of Jesus is absent, overridden, or merely apparent must explain why the prayer's central sentence is built on a contrast that, on that reading, does not exist. [Explicit text, HIGH]

Second, the recoil is from the cup, and it is sinless (study 07, finding 2; axiom VII.3). Poterion is the Old Testament's cup of wrath (Jeremiah 25:15; Isaiah 51:17, 22; Psalm 75:8): what Jesus recoils from is the passion as the drinking of the Father's appointed judgment, not the Father's authority and not obedience as such. The conditional submits the request before it is finished being spoken ("ei boulei," if you are willing), and the recoil is presented without any mark of fault, correction, or repentance; Jesus rises from the prayer and goes to meet the arrest (22:47). Natural human aversion to suffering and death is here exhibited as innocent. Aversion to the cup is not a will to disobey. [Necessary inference, HIGH]

Third, the alignment is free and unforced (study 07, finding 3; axiom VII.3). The adversative plen subordinates the request without withdrawing it: the grammar does not say "I did not mean it" but "not this, if it comes to the choice, but yours." Nothing in the scene depicts the human will as bypassed, coerced, or replaced; the submission is itself the human will's own act. Matthew's repetition pattern is the narrative's own commentary: by the second prayer the "my will" clause is gone, and only "genetheto to thelema sou" remains, the Lord's Prayer unadorned (Matthew 26:42; 6:10). The prayer therefore exhibits, before any council formulates it, a human will following and subject to the divine will, not opposed to it: the exegesis supplies the exhibition, the council supplies the formula, and the treatise keeps the two in that order.

Two disciplines attach to this text. The textual question of verses 43-44 (the angel, the sweat like drops of blood) is genuinely open: the verses are absent from weighty early witnesses, printed in double brackets in NA28 and UBS5, and rated {C} by the UBS committee; study 07's verdict, following the NET textual note, is that they are probably not literarily original and probably authentic tradition, at MEDIUM. No finding of this Part rests on them (study 07, finding 5); they would illustrate the prayer's cost and carry none of its logic. And the verse by itself does not settle impeccability: what it shows is a human will that articulates a contrary preference at the level of natural recoil and does not, at the level of choice, entertain refusal; whether that entails that Jesus could not have willed refusal is a dogmatic inference from the whole dossier, not a deliverance of this verse alone (study 07, finding 4).

### 2.2 John 6:38: the constancy pole, and the will's content

John's text: "hoti katabebeka apo tou ouranou, ouch hina poio to thelema to emon alla to thelema tou pempsantos me": "For I have come down from heaven, not in order that I might do my own will, but the will of the one who sent me" (study 08, sec. 1; the critical text reads apo tou ouranou; the working paper's transliteration prints ek, the Byzantine reading, agreeing in substance). The verse's contribution to the dossier is distinct from Luke's, and study 08 fixes it in four findings.

First, the datum: one speaker, one "I," predicates two referentially distinct wills in a single sentence (study 08, finding 1; axiom VII.2). The two articular phrases, to thelema to emon and to thelema tou pempsantos me, are distinguished by ownership inside one purpose clause. Whatever systematics is built on the verse must start there: John presents one speaker who speaks of "my will" as a real possession and of the sender's will as another, and subordinates the first to the second at the level of purpose. [Explicit text, HIGH]

Second, the relation stated is non-competition by constitution (study 08, finding 2). The negation falls on purpose (ouch hina): the descent was never aimed at his own will. John gives no scene in which the Son's will resists and is then overridden; the alignment is the mission's definition from the outset: his food (4:34), the ground of his just judgment (5:30, "I seek not my own will but the will of him who sent me"), his abiding experience (8:29, "I always do what is pleasing to him"). The cost and recoil that belong to the human will's obedience appear in John at 12:27 ("Now my soul is troubled; and what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour? No, for this purpose I have come to this hour") and 13:21 (troubled in spirit), where the recoil is real and is answered from within the mission in the same breath. John's is the constancy pole of the dossier: a human will genuinely present and genuinely troubled, whose course is unbroken alignment. The framing of the two Gospels as a constancy pole (John) and a petitionary-tension pole (Luke) is study 08's own, tagged there Theological synthesis, MEDIUM (axiom VII.3 as corrected), and this Part adopts it at that tag: Part VII needs both poles and must not import Luke's form into John or flatten John's constancy into insensibility.

Third, the will's content is specified (study 08, finding 3; axiom VII.8). The sentence's own continuation defines the thelema being done: "This is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up at the last day" (6:39); "this is the will of my Father, that everyone who looks on the Son and believes in him should have eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day" (6:40). By the time John uses thelema absolutely in this discourse, it denotes the purpose end of the word's range, not the inclination end: a definite saving purpose with specified objects and a specified outcome. [Explicit text, HIGH]

Fourth, the consequence (study 08, finding 4): 6:38 is soteriological as well as Christological, and this Part uses it as both (T-VII.3). The Son's obedience is the execution of the saving decree; the resurrection of the given ones at the last day is that execution's terminus, and Part II has already carried the resurrection of the Son himself as the covenant's first executed consequence (axiom II.7, from Hebrews 13:20). A treatment that used 6:38 only to count wills would underread its own control text.

### 2.3 Hebrews 4:15: sympathy on a standing state, sin excluded throughout

Hebrews' text: "ou gar echomen archierea me dynamenon sympathesai tais astheneiais hemon, pepeirasmenon de kata panta kath' homoioteta choris hamartias": "For we do not have a high priest unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who has been tempted in all things according to likeness, apart from sin" (study 09, sec. 1). Study 09's findings, on which this Part leans most heavily, are these.

The participle pepeirasmenon is perfect passive, not aorist (study 09, sec. 3.1). The temptation is finished as episode and unfinished as qualification: he remains, now, in the exalted state of 4:14, the one who has been through it. Hebrews itself sets the contrast at 2:18, where the same verb appears as an aorist participle (peirastheis) for the event beside a perfect of suffering (peponthen): the epistle can narrate the tempting as event when it wants to, and at 4:15 it chooses instead to confess it as abiding condition. His sympathy is therefore grounded in a standing state, not a memory, and the verse's own logic runs from completed experience to present help: "for because he himself has suffered when he was tempted, he is able to help those who are being tempted" (2:18). The ground of the readers' approach in 4:16 is fully discharged by the completed experience; ability to sin is not named, implied, or needed anywhere in the argument (study 09, findings 1, 3; axiom VII.5). [Explicit text, HIGH; the exclusion of peccability from the grounding, Necessary inference, HIGH]

The scope of the likeness is total across kinds (study 09, sec. 3.2, finding 2). Kata panta distributes over respects, not over individual episodes: no category of human temptation lay outside his experience, and the likeness term itself (homoiotes, resemblance, not tautotes, identity) builds the discipline into the grammar. And the distribution of astheneia in the epistle is itself an argument (study 09, sec. 3.3): weakness is predicated of us (4:15), of the Levitical priests (5:2; 7:28), and of the heroes of faith (11:34), and never of Christ. He sympathizes with weaknesses whose conditions he underwent, hunger, exhaustion, pain, mortality, without the word's ever being applied to his own constitution.

The closing phrase governs the whole complex (study 09, sec. 3.4, finding 4; axiom VII.5). Choris hamartias stands last, closing the entire participial complex (pepeirasmenon kata panta kath' homoioteta choris hamartias), and choris marks separation, not mere absence: Tittmann's observation, carried in Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, is that aneu would have presented sin as an object merely absent, while choris presents the subject as separated from sin across an interval; Alford's consequence follows, that throughout his temptations "in their origin, process, and result, sin had nothing in Him." The phrase's only other New Testament occurrence, at 9:28, states Christ's objective apartness from sin as such, and the chora-root cognate at 7:26 confesses the state in a participle: kechorismenos apo ton hamartolon, "separated from sinners," perfect passive, a settled condition exactly parallel in aspect to 4:15's pepeirasmenon. The two perfects belong together: the priest who has been tempted (4:15) is the priest who stands apart from sinners (7:26), and 7:27-28 builds the cultic consequence (no sins of his own to offer for; the once-for-all offering; the Son "perfected forever" over against priests "having weakness"). What no scope of the phrase permits is a temptation partly constituted by sin and merely failing to end in it: on every grammatical option, sin is denied to something that includes the process (study 09, sec. 3.4). One tagging caution the study itself adds is carried here: choris names sin, not desire as such; the specification that the exclusion reaches inward sinful desire (James 1:14's epithymia mechanism) is secured by the phrase together with the wider canon (James 1:13-15; 2 Corinthians 5:21; 1 John 3:5), and is tagged necessary inference and synthesis accordingly, not read off the preposition alone (study 09, findings 4, 5).

Finally, the limit, stated by the study as a limit on its own control text (study 09, finding 6): 4:15 contains no modal term. It confesses that he did not sin and that sin had no place in his tempting; it does not, in so many words, state that he could not sin. Section 6 builds the modal claim on its proper basis and at its proper calibration; nothing in this section borrows it in advance.

Above the three texts stands the epistle's own portrait of the temptation's cost, Hebrews 5:7-9: prayers and supplications "with loud cries and tears," obedience learned through what he suffered, perfection reached through the suffering. It is the letter's commentary on Gethsemane, and it is the canon's internal answer, written before the objection was framed, to the charge that a sinless temptation must be an unreal one. Part VII returns to it in sec. 6.4.

## 3. The conciliar settlement: Constantinople III

### 3.1 Setting and Definition

The Sixth Ecumenical Council was convened by the emperor Constantine IV and sat in eighteen sessions from 7 November 680 to 16 September 681. It received as consonant with Chalcedon the dogmatic letter of Pope Agatho and the synodal letter of the Roman synod of 125 bishops held under him; its Definition of Faith stands in the acts of Session XVIII. The council was read for this treatise in the Schaff translation (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, vol. 14); the critical edition of the acts (Acta Conciliorum Oecumenicorum) was not consulted, an open item recorded in sec. 9.

The Definition confesses: "two natural wills and two natural operations indivisibly, inconvertibly, inseparably, inconfusedly, according to the teaching of the holy Fathers." The four Chalcedonian adverbs are carried over from natures to wills and operations, so that the completeness of each nature is protected at the level of volition and activity, not merely at the level of essence (research note, Finding 1.1; axiom VII.1). A nature without its natural will would be an incomplete nature; a Christ without a human natural will would not be fully man. The confession is therefore not an addition to Chalcedon but Chalcedon's own logic extended to the faculty Chalcedon left unexamined.

### 3.2 Non-contrariety, subjection, and preservation

The Definition immediately guards the confession against its obvious misreading: "And these two natural wills are not contrary the one to the other (God forbid!) as the impious heretics assert, but his human will follows and that not as resisting and reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and omnipotent will." After Athanasius, "it was right that the flesh should be moved but subject to the divine will"; and the human will, "although deified, was not suppressed, but was rather preserved," the Definition quoting Gregory the Theologian: "His will is not contrary to God but altogether deified" (Finding 1.2). The three predicates together are the dogma's shape: distinction (two wills), harmony (not contrary; the human follows and is subject), and preservation (deified, not suppressed). Luke 22:42 is the exhibition of all three in a single sentence: a real "my will," a real subordination freely spoken, and a will that is neither resisted into silence nor reluctant in its following (sec. 2.1).

### 3.3 The proof-text distribution, kept exact

Precision about which conciliar document cites which text is part of this Part's discipline, because the distribution is itself evidence of how the tradition read the dossier. In the Schaff translation, the Definition proper anchors the wills in John 6:38, reading Christ's word as the will of his flesh speaking: "as he himself says: I came down from heaven, not that I might do my own will but the will of the Father which sent me! where he calls his own will the will of his flesh, inasmuch as his flesh was also his own" (Finding 1.3). The Gethsemane word (Matthew 26:39; Luke 22:42) is expounded at length not in the Definition but in the materials the council received and approved, Agatho's dogmatic letter and its patristic florilegium, where Athanasius against Apollinaris is quoted on the prayer: "he shows that there are two wills, the one human which is the will of the flesh, but the other divine" (Finding 1.3). This Part therefore cites John 6:38 as the Definition's own anchor and Luke 22:42 as the council's anchor through Agatho and Athanasius, and it does not trade the one citation for the other.

### 3.4 Two operations, and the sentence on monothelitism

The second operation is proved from Leo's Tome, in the Definition's quotation: "For each form does in communion with the other what pertains properly to it, the Word, namely, doing that which pertains to the Word, and the flesh that which pertains to the flesh" (Finding 1.4; register entry VII-3, consulted via mediation, the Definition itself). The Definition refuses "one natural operation in God and in the creature," on the ground that this would either exalt the created into the divine essence or degrade the divine to the creature's place. The Tome's sentence matters beyond the operations question: each nature doing what is proper to itself, in communion with the other, is the closest conciliar analogue to the Westminster standards' later proper-works language (sec. 5), and it is the structural reason the two-wills confession protects rather than threatens the unity of the person: the acts are the person's, performed through each nature's proper operation.

The Definition names the monothelite teachers and anathematizes them: Theodore of Pharan; Sergius, Pyrrhus, Paul, and Peter of Constantinople; Honorius of Rome; Cyrus of Alexandria; Macarius of Antioch; and Stephen (Finding 1.5). The council's address to the emperor (the Prosphoneticus) states the sentence in terms against "all who have taught, or do teach, or shall presume to teach one will and one operation in the incarnate Christ," and a formal sentence also stands at Session XIII. Monothelitism is thus the historically condemned counter-position at this locus, and sec. 7 treats it as such: not a live option within the treatise's frame, but the error whose refutation produced the confession.

### 3.5 What the council did not define

The Definition's silence is as load-bearing for this Part as its speech. The council confesses Christ "in all things like us, sin only excepted," the Chalcedonian predicate restated (register entry VII-2, via mediation, the Definition restating Chalcedon). Sinlessness is therefore inside the conciliar confession as a fact. But the council nowhere uses a modal formula of inability to sin, and it nowhere defines impeccability (Finding 1.6). Its decree settles the number, naturality, and harmony of the wills; the modal question, could Christ have sinned, is left to theological inference. Part VII states this plainly because its own sec. 6 occupies exactly the ground the council left open: the treatise's impeccability claim cannot borrow conciliar authority, and does not.

## 4. Maximus: the natural will and the gnomic will

The council defined that there are two natural wills; it did not supply the analysis of what a will is, or of why the human will's alignment in Christ is neither opposition nor compulsion. That analysis is Maximus the Confessor's, a generation before the council, in the Disputation with Pyrrhus (645) and the Opuscula; the tradition ratified it in effect by the council's outcome, and the council itself did not define it (research note, Findings 2.1-2.5; register entries VII-8, VII-9). This Part presents the apparatus at that tier, Tier C, theological construction, and with its access flag visible: Maximus was reached at excerpt level only in this project (Opusculum 3 in an unattributed web translation whose translator and edition could not be verified, consulted directly under that caveat per the Phase 2 V&V correction C-3; the Disputation through a peer-reviewed article's citations of PG 91, via mediation; Farrell's translation reported only; the Greek text and critical editions not consulted). Nothing in this section quotes Maximus beyond the excerpts the research note verified.

The natural will (thelesis physike) is a faculty of nature: "the power that longs for what is natural," containing "all the properties that are essentially attached to the nature"; without it "the human nature cannot be." When the Fathers speak of two wills in Christ, Maximus writes, "they do not speak of quantity in relation to gnomic wills, but only in relation to natural wills, rightly calling the essential and natural laws and principles of what has been united wills" (Opusculum 3, Finding 2.1). Will belongs to nature as such; the count of wills follows the count of natures, not of persons, which is the same distribution Turretin later states as an axiom (sec. 5).

The gnomic will (thelema gnomikon, from gnome) is not a second faculty but a mode of using the natural will: the deliberative inclination of a particular person who must weigh alternatives under partial ignorance, implying "hesitation and deliberation since a person does not know what he/she wants," and in fallen persons the seat of wavering and of sin (Finding 2.2, via mediation, the Vox Patrum article on the Disputation and Opuscula). Gnomic willing is a tropos of fallen hypostases, not a logos of human nature. Christ therefore has the natural human will and does not have a gnomic will (Finding 2.3; axiom VII.4, Theological synthesis, MEDIUM): "Christ did not possess gnomic will. He was never in a state of ignorance concerning what he wanted. His human will was perfect and could not oppose the divine will or lead to sin. It was a deified will." The absence is not a defect of his humanity, because what is not assumed is not healed applies to nature and its faculties, not to a fallen mode of exercising them; and Maximus's reductio, as the scholarship quotes it, shows the cost of the alternative: had Christ possessed a gnomic will, he would have been "double-minded and double-willed, and fighting against himself... and therefore double-personed," since "it was only this difference of gnomic wills that introduced into our lives sin and our separation from God" (Finding 2.3, via mediation).

On Luke 22:42, Maximus reads the agony as the human natural will doing what human nature does in the face of death, and then being freely conformed: the Saviour "possesses as a human being the natural disposition to will, and this is moved and shaped by his divine will"; he wills "in a fleshly way the shrinking in the face of death together with the rest of the passions," and the conclusion is that "the Saviour therefore possesses as a human being a natural will, which is shaped, but not opposed, by his divine will" (Opusculum 3, Finding 2.4). Bieler's study confirms the reading and its polemical target: the deprecation of the chalice "was a natural act of his truly human will" and "in no way did this natural act of deprecation contradict the will of the Father, who created nature and its natural fear of death"; against Sergius, the prayer is not evidence of a human will contradicting the divine (Finding 2.4; register entry VII-10, consulted directly). Following Cyril, Maximus adds the soteriological point this Part's thesis T-VII.3 requires: in the agony Christ assumes our shrinking from death in order to remove it from our nature; the recoil is itself part of the economy of restoration, not an embarrassment to it.

The critical caveat is recorded, not suppressed (Finding 2.5): not all modern readers find the construction costless, since on general anthropological grounds Maximus attributes both a natural and a gnomic will to ordinary human beings, and the critic argues he was driven by the Christological case to deny the gnomic will to Christ and to treat gnome as bound up with sin. The treatise accordingly presents the natural/gnomic distinction as Maximus's theological construction, received by the tradition and ratified in effect by the council's outcome, not as though Constantinople III had defined the gnome apparatus. It did not (sec. 3.5).

## 5. The confessional boundary: Westminster, and the Reformed reception

### 5.1 What the standards confess

The Westminster standards were read directly for this locus (research note, sec. 3.2; register entry VII-5). Their confession at this point has four members. (1) The natures and the person: the Son took "man's nature, with all the essential properties and common infirmities thereof, yet without sin," so that "two whole, perfect, and distinct natures, the Godhead and the manhood, were inseparably joined together in one person, without conversion, composition, or confusion" (WCF 8.2); Christ "was and continues to be God and man, in two entire distinct natures, and one person, forever" (WLC 36), having taken "a true body, and a reasonable soul... yet without sin" (WLC 37). (2) The sinlessness is confessed with its ground stated: he was thereby "holy, harmless, undefiled, and full of grace and truth" (WCF 8.3); and the proof the Confession cites for "yet without sin" is Hebrews 2:14, 16-17 and 4:15, which is why sec. 2.3's text is this Part's confessional anchor as well as its exegetical one. (3) The operations: "Christ, in the work of mediation, acteth according to both natures, by each nature doing that which is proper to itself" (WCF 8.7); the Mediator had to be God and man in one person "that the proper works of each nature might be accepted of God for us, and relied on by us, as the works of the whole person" (WLC 40). The proper-works language of 8.7 and WLC 40 is the standards' nearest approach to the two-wills doctrine, and WLC 40's whole-person premise is a load-bearing member of the impeccability premise set (sec. 6.3). (4) The sympathy: among the reasons he had to be man, WLC 39 gives that he might "have a fellow-feeling of our infirmities," receiving Hebrews 4:15's stated function into the confession itself.

### 5.2 What the standards do not say

Neither WCF 8 nor WLC 36-40 contains the word "will" or "wills" as a predicate of Christ's natures. There is no two-wills formulary, no statement that the human will follows and is subject to the divine will, and no statement of impeccability, no non posse peccare (Finding 3.2.2; axiom VII.7). Dyothelitism is entirely consistent with, and arguably presupposed by, "two whole, perfect, and distinct natures" together with 8.7; but as a matter of confessional text it is not formulated there. This Part therefore never says that the Westminster standards confess two wills: the precision flag raised in the preparation of working paper section 6.24 was confirmed by direct inspection of the texts, and the standards function in this treatise as the boundary within which the conciliar doctrine is received, not as a second formulation of it. The treatise claims consistency and consequence from Westminster for both the two wills and impeccability, never confessional formulation (axiom VII.7). The distinction is not pedantry: a treatise whose method is good and necessary consequence must be the first to distinguish what its own confession states from what it infers.

### 5.3 Turretin: the Reformed reception in use

Turretin's Institutes, Thirteenth Topic ("The Person and State of Christ"), was read directly in the Giger/Dennison translation, vol. 2 (research note, sec. 3.1; register entry VII-12). Two disciplines govern citation. First, locus discipline: the Thirteenth Topic has no standalone question on the two wills or on impeccability; the material lives inside Q. V (the nature assumed), Q. VII (the hypostatic union), Q. XI (the conception), Q. XII (the graces bestowed on the human nature), and Q. XIII (the knowledge of Christ's soul), and this Part cites those loci rather than implying a dedicated question. Second, the reception's shape: dyothelitism is presupposed and deployed, with the nature/person distribution doing the analytical work.

The two-wills locus is Q. VII, section XIV (vol. 2, pp. 320-321), where Turretin, arguing against Eutychian confusion, gives as his second reason: "(2) two wills are ascribed to him ('not my will, but thine, be done,' Lk. 22:42)." He immediately blocks the Nestorian inference: it does not follow that there are two willing persons, "because the will belongs to the nature, while willing belongs to the person"; and the number of wills does not track the number of persons, since "in God there are three persons, but only one will." Maximus's distribution (sec. 4) here becomes the Reformed tradition's working axiom. The sinlessness loci supply the modal framework sec. 6 builds with: Q. V (pp. 306-310), the assumed nature is like ours in all things, "sin excepted"; Q. XII (pp. 347-348), the human nature received habitual graces in a "fulness of abundance" beyond all angels and men, the Spirit given "without measure" (John 3:34), the gifts not transient acts but "permanent and fixed" possessions; Q. XIII, section II (p. 349), of the ignorance ascribed to Christ, it cannot be the ignorance of a depraved disposition, "not the least shadow of [sin] do we acknowledge to have been in him." The same volume's locus on the state of glory supplies the modal vocabulary: paradise knew posse non peccare; the glorified state, "the state and condition of the elect angels," is non posse peccare. That Turretin holds impeccability, and argues the compatibility of necessity with free and meritorious obedience in Christ, is the received scholarly reading (the Themelios article summarizing Paul Helm; register entry VII-17, via mediation): Turretin and Edwards as Chalcedonian adherents affirming dyothelitism with "volitional non-contrariety" and "Christ's impeccability," rejecting freedom as indifference. The article's location of Turretin's free-will discussion in vol. 1 (Tenth Topic, 1:501-508) is reported through the article only; vol. 1 and the Latin were not consulted (sec. 9).

## 6. Impeccability: the inference at its printed calibration

### 6.1 The printed calibration

The Phase 2 consistency audit fixed one calibration for this doctrine, printed in the Prolegomena (sec. 4.6, discipline 1) and carried verbatim by this Part wherever the doctrine is stated:

"Impeccability is theological synthesis. From Hebrews 4:15 alone it stands at MEDIUM. On the treatise's full premise set (Hebrews 4:15 with 7:26-28, 1:12, 13:8; James 1:13; 1 John 3:5; 2 Corinthians 5:21; John 8:46; and the one-person premise of WLC 40) it stands at HIGH. It is not a conciliar definition and not a confessional article, and it is contested within the Reformed tradition (Hodge)."

Neither half may be printed without the other (Prolegomena, sec. 4.3), and this section exists to show why the calibration has two halves: the two source calibrations it reconciles measure different things, and printing either figure alone would misstate one of them.

### 6.2 The basis that stands at MEDIUM: Hebrews 4:15 alone

Study 09, finding 6, is dispositive and this Part does not soften it: the verse contains no modal term. It confesses the fact of sinlessness, the totality of the likeness, the exclusion of sin from the whole temptation complex, and the sympathy grounded in completed experience (sec. 2.3). It does not state that he could not sin. As an inference from this verse alone, therefore, impeccability remains what working paper section 6.24 tagged it: a theological inference, MEDIUM. Any draft sentence of the form "Hebrews 4:15 teaches that Christ could not sin" overclaims the verse, and the working paper's own modal qualification is confirmed at exactly this point: sinlessness alone does not establish impeccability (WP 6.24); the stronger claim "is a further doctrinal inference, not a proposition explicitly phrased in Hebrews 4:15" (WP 6.19; likewise 6.23).

### 6.3 The basis that stands at HIGH: the full premise set

As the treatise actually constructs the claim, the premise set is larger, and study 09, finding 8(b), rates the inference on that set as good and necessary consequence at full strength. The premises, each at its own tag:

(1) The fact of 4:15, restated within the same epistle as an abiding state: kechorismenos, separated from sinners as a settled condition (7:26), cashed out in the offering that required no sins of his own (7:27) and in the Son "perfected forever" (7:28) (study 09, finding 7; Explicit text, HIGH).

(2) The same epistle's unchanging Son: "you are the same" (1:12, applying Psalm 102 to the Son) and "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever" (13:8), predicated of the same subject to whom 4:15's tempting is predicated (study 09, finding 7; Explicit text, HIGH). Shedd's first scriptural member runs here: a mutable holiness could not be described in terms proper to unchangeable divine holiness (research note, Finding 3.3.2(a)).

(3) The canonical frame: God cannot be tempted by evil (James 1:13, apeirastos); in Christ sin is not merely uncommitted but absent (1 John 3:5, present tense: "in him there is no sin"); he "knew no sin" (2 Corinthians 5:21); and his own unanswered challenge stands in the record: "Which of you convicts me of sin?" (John 8:46).

(4) The completion, the one-person premise: the willing and acting subject is the one divine Son, and the works of each nature are the works of the whole person (WLC 40; sec. 5.1). On this premise, a peccable Christ would require either that the unchanging, sin-separated Son be capable of acting against his own confessed state and nature, or that there be a second, independent center of willing alongside the person, which the two-wills confession itself excludes (study 09, finding 8(b)). Turretin's modal framework belongs to this member: the human nature endowed with habitual grace in fulness of abundance, the Spirit without measure, the gifts permanent and fixed (XIII.xii), so that the nature's confirmation is not a bare decree about it but a constituted plenitude in it (sec. 5.3).

On that set, the inference stands at HIGH as theological synthesis (axiom VII.6). Two features of the rating must be kept visible. It is synthesis, not exegesis of any single text: no premise in the set is dispensable, and the set's modal conclusion is drawn, not quoted. And its HIGH is a rating of the inference's strength on the stated basis, not of the doctrine's dogmatic rank, which is the subject of sec. 6.5.

### 6.4 Shedd's construction, used as synthesis

W. G. T. Shedd's full-dress defense (Dogmatic Theology, vol. 2, Christology ch. 5; read directly in full-text reproduction; register entry VII-13) supplies the construction's classical form, and this Part adopts it under study 09's discipline: it is accepted as theological synthesis, and it is never stated as a lexical claim on Hebrews 4:15. Its members:

(a) The person measured by his mightiest nature. Sin is an act of a person, and this person is the God-man; "the divine determines and controls the human, not the human the divine," so the person's power of resistance "must be measured not by the human nature but by the divine." Hence the careful distribution: "Christ while having a peccable human nature in his constitution, was an impeccable person." The human nature considered in abstraction from the union is the kind of nature that fell in Adam; the person who wills and acts in it is not peccable. The guilt of a sinful act would attach to the whole theanthropic person, and the Logos could consent to suffer in the human nature but not to sin in it.

(b) The two-wills proof. "Again, the impeccability of Christ is proved by the relation of the two wills in his person to each other. Each nature, in order to be complete, entire, and wanting nothing, has its own will; but the finite will never antagonizes the infinite will, but obeys it invariably and perfectly." Shedd here draws the modal conclusion from the conciliar confession itself (sec. 3.2): non-contrariety, confessed without temporal qualification, is the dogmatic ground on which the inference stands most naturally.

(c) Temptability without peccability. Against the objection that one who cannot sin cannot be tempted: "any more than it would be correct to say that because an army cannot be conquered, it cannot be attacked. Temptability depends upon the constitutional susceptibility, while impeccability depends upon the will." Christ was open to every temptation that works through natural susceptibility, "excepting those that spring out of lust or corruption of nature," which he did not have, the very exception study 09, finding 5, establishes exegetically. The analogy of confirmed creatures completes the member: the elect angels, impeccable now by the power of God bestowed on them, "might be tempted still"; redeemed men in heaven are impeccable through grace "yet they are still temptable, though not exposed to temptation"; the only absolutely intemptable being is God (James 1:13). Impeccability in the God-man according to his humanity is therefore compatible with genuine assault, genuine suffering under assault, and genuine sympathy.

(d) The sympathy inversion. Because he never yielded, Christ bore the full and cumulative force of every temptation to its end, which those who yield early never do; his sympathy is therefore greater, not less. Section 6.5's answer to Hodge leans on this member, and Hebrews itself supplies its exegetical form: the one who does not succumb feels temptation's whole weight, and 5:7-9 (loud cries, tears, obedience learned through suffering) is the record of that weight (study 09, sec. 5.2).

### 6.5 The standing dissent, and the audit's reconciliation stated honestly

The calibration's second half exists because the doctrine is contested within the tradition, by a theologian of weight. Charles Hodge denied impeccability outright (Systematic Theology, vol. 2, p. 457), in the strongest form in which the objection has been stated. Hodge was not read directly for this project; his words enter through two named mediations and are labeled accordingly wherever used: verbatim in John F. Walvoord's survey of the question, and in Shedd's own footnote quotation, with the citation independently confirmed by the Clearly Reformed primer (register entry VII-15). In Walvoord's quotation:

"This sinlessness of our Lord, however, does not amount to absolute impeccability. It was not a non potest peccare. If He was a true man, He must have been capable of sinning. That He did not sin under the greatest provocations; that when He was reviled He blessed; when He suffered He threatened not; that He was dumb as a sheep before its shearers, is held up to us as an example. Temptation implies the possibility of sin. If from the constitution of his person it was impossible for Christ to sin, then his temptation was unreal and without effect and He cannot sympathize with his people."

Shedd's footnote preserves the objection's compressed form: "Temptation implies the possibility of sin. If from the constitution of his person it was impossible for Christ to sin, then his temptation was unreal and without effect, and he cannot sympathize with his people" (research note, Finding 3.3.3). The objection has two prongs: (i) a definition, that temptation analytically implies the possibility of succumbing; and (ii) a pastoral consequence, that an impeccable Christ cannot sympathize, against Hebrews 4:15's own stated function.

The answer proceeds at the objection's own levels, as the treatise's level rule requires (axiom X.1; study 09, finding 9): the premise "temptation implies the possibility of sin" is a philosophical premise, not an exegetical finding, and it may not be cited as a deliverance of Hebrews 4:15. On the exegetical level, it fails twice. Peirazo encodes nothing about the openness of the outcome: the word names an assault, a trial, a solicitation directed at a subject, and a test that cannot be failed by the one tested is not, lexically, any less a test (study 09, sec. 3.1). And the verse's sympathy logic nowhere includes a capacity to fall: the double negative of 4:15, the tense network of 2:18, and the pastoral conclusion of 4:16 all ground sympathy and help in undergone and completed experience (sec. 2.3; study 09, finding 3). On the philosophical level, where the premise is contestable and contested, Shedd's reply stands as this Part's synthesis answer (sec. 6.4(c), (d)): the reality of a temptation resides in the assault's force and the cost of resistance, both of which the canon maximizes for Christ rather than minimizing; the one who yields early never feels the whole of it; and the confirmed angels show the category, temptable yet impeccable, already instantiated in creatures. What is retained from Hodge is not nothing: the temptations were real, the cost was real, the human nature in abstraction is the kind of nature that fell in Adam (Shedd concedes exactly this), and the sympathy is the verse's point, not a decoration on it (study 09, sec. 5.1). What is declined is the inference from those truths to peccability, because its load-bearing premise is in neither the lexicon nor the verse.

There remains the reconciliation itself, and this Part states its provenance exactly. The two-wills research note holds impeccability at MEDIUM as a tradition-status claim, and grounds that tag in the claim's being an inference "neither defined nor uncontested," stating that "the standing dissent of a theologian of Hodge's weight inside the tradition is itself sufficient reason to keep the tag at MEDIUM," with raise conditions: a tradition-wide good-and-necessary-consequence demonstration, plus a field-commanding answer to Hodge's definition of temptation (note, sec. 4, Tier C1). Study 09 splits the tag by basis (sec. 6.2, 6.3 above). The distinction drawn in the printed calibration between the doctrine's tradition-status and the strength of the treatise's inference on its stated premise set is the Phase 2 audit's reconciliation, audit item (i), not the two-wills note's own statement; the note distinguishes no such measures, and its stricter ground is preserved here in the record rather than harmonized away (audit correction C-2, applied to the axioms document and honored in this draft). The calibration is adopted with that difference recorded. It preserves the note's substance by printing the contested qualifier wherever the HIGH synthesis tag is used, and the note's raise-condition still governs any future claim that the doctrine itself stands above MEDIUM in the tradition. This Part claims the inference at HIGH; it does not claim the tradition at HIGH, and the difference is the audit's construction, openly owned.

## 7. Counter-positions in strongest form

### 7.1 The mode-limiting reading of choris hamartias (accepted as refined)

Strongest form: Alford, that sin had nothing in Christ "in their origin, process, and result"; Tittmann's lexical ground, choris as separation against aneu as mere absence; and the modern summary, that the preposition extends both to the outcome of the temptations and to their nature, or as John Owen put it (the dictum entering this project via the Clearly Reformed primer, via mediation; register entry VII-14), Christ faced the suffering part of temptation while we also face the sinning part. Adjudication (study 09, reading 3): accepted in substance and refined in statement. The reading is right that the phrase reaches the temptation itself and not only its score; it is imprecise only when phrased as "rather than" the outcome, since its own best statements include both. Its desire-specific component (no solicitation from indwelling epithymia) is a necessary inference from the phrase together with James 1:13-15 and the wider canon, tagged so, not read off the preposition alone (sec. 2.3).

### 7.2 The outcome-only reading of choris hamartias (partially accepted, declined as exhaustive)

Strongest form: it is the reading embodied in the NIV's rendering ("yet he did not sin"), converting the phrase into a finite clause; it keeps the verse's doctrine to the minimum the words most plainly state, and it warns, sincerely, against building a psychology of Christ's inner life on a two-word prepositional phrase at the end of a sentence whose argumentative purpose is pastoral, not speculative. Adjudication (study 09, reading 4): the outcome is certainly included in the phrase's coverage, and the pastoral purpose is real. Declined as an exhaustive account: it requires restricting a trailing modifier, whose word denotes separation, to the one member of the complex (the result) that its position least singles out, while the same phrase at 9:28 carries total apartness and the cognate participle at 7:26 confesses the state. A minimum-reading discipline is a virtue in exegesis; here it is purchased by assigning the author's words a narrower sense in 4:15 than he gives them in 9:28, which is not discipline but inconsistency.

### 7.3 Monothelitism (the historical counter-position)

Strongest form, as the conciliar record frames it: one will and one operation in the incarnate Christ, taught by Theodore of Pharan, Sergius and his successors at Constantinople, Cyrus of Alexandria, and, in the Definition's judgment, Honorius of Rome; its motive was the unity of the person, the fear that two wills must mean two willing subjects at war. Adjudication: the council's answer is this Part's (sec. 3): the fear confuses the count of faculties with the count of persons; the will belongs to the nature, willing to the person (Turretin's form of Maximus's distribution), and the Definition confesses distinction with non-contrariety rather than purchasing unity at the price of an incomplete humanity. A Christ without a human natural will would lack a constituent of the nature he assumed, and the obedience that saves would not be a human obedience. The position stands condemned by name in the acts (Session XVIII; the Prosphoneticus; Session XIII), and this Part registers it as the historical counter-position whose refutation produced the confession, not as a live option.

### 7.4 The genuinely divided will (from the Luke dossier)

Strongest form (study 07, reading 2): prayer that cannot fail is not prayer; freedom that cannot choose otherwise is not freedom; Hebrews 5:7's loud cries and tears are not the sounds of a settled outcome; the honesty of the recoil demands the real possibility of the refusal it recoils toward. Adjudication: the valid core is retained (the recoil is real, the asking is real, the freedom is real), and the stronger claim is declined. The text nowhere represents refusal as a live option entertained and narrowly defeated; the plen clause is the prayer's own grammar from its first utterance in all three Synoptic accounts, and Matthew's repetitions move away from the contrast, not deeper into it. The theological cost of the stronger claim must also be stated: if the human will might have settled against the Father, the cross's certainty rests on a creaturely decision that could have gone the other way, and the "will that might refuse" begins to function as an independent center of self-determination alongside the person of the Son, the Nestorian pressure the two-wills doctrine exists to refuse. Freedom in the prayer is spontaneous alignment, not indifference between obedience and refusal; Turretin and Edwards, on Helm's summary, reject freedom-as-indifference at exactly this point (sec. 5.3).

### 7.5 The reductionist reading of John 6:38 (declined)

Strongest form (study 08, readings 2 and 3, taken together): "my will" is agency idiom, the sent one's fidelity to commission, or a manner of speaking with no real human volition behind it; two wills in one person would risk the opposition that is impious to imagine, and John's lack of a Gethsemane struggle supports the idiom reading. Adjudication: the register claim is right (the verse's direct assertion is mission-functional, and its content is the commission's content, 6:39-40), and the closure does not follow. John does not lack the human interiority the reduction needs him to lack: the soul troubled at the hour (12:27), the spirit troubled at the betrayal (13:21), the groaning and tears at Bethany (11:33-35). The Fourth Gospel protects the unity by constancy of alignment, not by evacuation of affect or volition; and a possessive construction identical to Luke's, where the tradition took the human will as real, cannot be evacuated in John alone without special pleading. The subordinationist conversion of the same data (distinct wills prove unequal natures) is likewise declined on the discourse's own evidence (study 08, reading 1): the same chapters predicate of the Son life in himself (5:26), all judgment (5:22), and the raising of the dead by his own voice (5:25-29), and the descent perfect of 6:38 stakes the speaker's heavenly origin in the verse's first clause. The doctrine of this Part stands: the distinction of wills is the text's datum; what it does not prove is a distinction of worth or nature.

## 8. Contribution statement

Checked against the Appendix C register for Part VII (19 entries, VII-1 to VII-19), this Part claims no new primary-text discovery: the conciliar acts were read in the Schaff translation, Maximus at excerpt level, Hodge only in mediation, and the register's statuses are reproduced in this draft wherever they bind. What the Part adds beyond its sources is synthesis, calibration, and integration, in four members.

(1) **The dossier as one argument.** The working paper's section 6.24 named Luke 22:42, John 6:38, and Hebrews 4:15 as control texts; Phase 1 exegeted each in its own idiom, and this Part is, to the register's knowledge, the first place in the corpus where the three are read as a single dossier under one discipline: Luke's distinction and free alignment, John's constancy pole and the soteriological specification of thelema, Hebrews' sympathy on a standing state with sin excluded from the whole complex, each member forbidden to borrow the others' forms (the two-poles framing carried at its own MEDIUM tag).

(2) **The proof-text distribution as method.** The observation that the Definition proper anchors the wills in John 6:38 while Luke 22:42 enters through Agatho's letter and its Athanasian florilegium is present in the research note; this Part makes it a standing citation discipline and an exhibit of the treatise's larger claim, that the tradition's own anchoring, recovered exactly, is stronger than a flattened appeal to "the council's texts."

(3) **The dual-basis calibration, with its provenance disclosed.** The printed calibration of sec. 6.1, distinguishing a doctrine's tradition-status from the strength of the treatise's inference on its stated premise set, and sec. 6.5's open recording that the distinction is the Phase 2 audit's construction rather than the research note's own statement, together constitute the Part's sharpest contribution: not a new doctrine of impeccability, but a defensible way to print a contested one at full inferential strength without misrepresenting its dogmatic rank. The register contains no source that prints the claim this way; the calibration is the treatise's own, offered to the field as a practice.

(4) **Integration with the decree.** Thesis T-VII.3, the Son's obedience as the execution of the definite saving purpose of John 6:39-40, joins this Part to Part II's covenant chain (the pactum at synthesis MEDIUM; Hebrews 13:20 as chain member) and to Part VIII's consummation (the given ones raised at the last day), so that Christology in this treatise is not a self-contained locus but the point where the good decree becomes enacted communion. The working paper stated the two-wills doctrine inside a theodicy; the treatise's Part VII gives it its own locus and its connective tissue at once.
