## 1. The question of this Part and its theses

Every later Part of this treatise measures something against a standard it does not itself supply. Part IV defines sin as the privation and opposite of communion; Part VIII describes the consummation as communion perfected; Part IX reads the whole moral order off a single pattern and dissolves the Euthyphro dilemma by naming it. All three derivations presuppose an answer to the question this Part exists to answer: what is God eternally, such that his own life can serve as the moral archetype of the created order? If the archetype claim fails, the treatise loses not an ornament but its load-bearing member, and Parts IV, VIII, and IX must be rebuilt on three separate foundations. This Part therefore argues the archetype claim directly, from its textual bases, at its audited calibration, and under the disciplines the audit and the Appendix C register impose on it.

The Part's theses, as the derivation map states them, are two.

**T-I.1.** God's eternal triune life is loving communion, and that life is the moral archetype of the created order. Depends on axiom I.2; supported by axioms I.1 and VIII.5.

**T-I.2.** Divine impassibility, rightly stated, denies involuntary and destabilizing passion, not genuine affection: God's grief and regret are real without any mutability of character or counsel. Depends on axiom I.1. The metaphysical reconciliation of the two poles is argued in Part V; this Part states the poles as data, and does not pretend the reconciliation has been performed.

Three limits govern everything that follows, stated here rather than discovered later. First, the archetype claim stands at **Theological synthesis, MEDIUM** (axiom I.2), and this Part does not argue it past that calibration: it is the internal corpus's governing thesis, its textual bases are real, and its broader-corpus novelty check has not been performed (secs. 5, 10). Second, the tradition's chief witnesses for the doctrine of God in this Part's register (Plato's Euthyphro, Athanasius, Augustine's De Trinitate, Aquinas on God and the Trinity, Westminster Confession ch. 2, Edwards, Bavinck, Vos, Van Til) stand at NOT YET CONSULTED; they are this Part's stated research frontier, not its silent authorities, and nothing here rests on them. Third, the Part distinguishes three levels the audit forbids it to merge (audit item (vi)): the eternal triune life itself (Level 1), the eternal decree (Level 2), and the pactum as the decree's covenantal form toward the Mediator (Level 3). This Part treats Level 1; where it touches the decree or the covenant, it marks the boundary, and the arguments belong to Part II.

## 2. The datum: God is love, eternally

The archetype claim begins from a datum, not from a construction. The internal corpus states it in its compact form: "John 17:24 places the Father's love for the Son before creation; 1 John 4:8 identifies love with God's character. Matthew 28:19 and 2 Corinthians 13:14 contribute to the biblical basis of triune revelation. The formulation of one divine essence and three distinct persons is a doctrinal synthesis. God's love requires no created object to complete it" (Triune Loving Communion systematic theology, v0.1, sec. 1). Each member of that statement carries its own weight, and the Part keeps them distinct.

That the Father loved the Son "before the foundation of the world" (John 17:24, as carried in the internal corpus) locates love inside God's eternal life, prior to any created object. Love in God is therefore not a relation God entered when creatures gave him something to love; it is a relation he eternally is. [Theological synthesis from the cited texts, as the corpus itself tags its formulation; the pretemporal location of the love is the text's own.]

That "God is love" (1 John 4:8, as carried in the internal corpus) predicates love of God's character, not merely of his acts. The distinction matters for everything the archetype claim does later. A God who performs loving acts toward creatures might have a character otherwise; a God who is love has love as what he is, and his acts toward creatures are the overflow of an eternal fullness rather than the acquisition of a new property. [Explicit text for the predication; the overflow construction is synthesis.]

That this one God is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (Matthew 28:19; 2 Corinthians 13:14, as carried in the internal corpus) gives the eternal love personal terms: not God's regard for an abstraction, nor for himself undifferentiated, but the mutual love of distinct persons in one essence. The formulation "one divine essence and three distinct persons" is, as the corpus says, a doctrinal synthesis from these texts, and this Part receives it as such, stated inside the boundary the creeds fix (register, Part I, entries 2-3: the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed and the Chalcedonian Definition are this Part's registered confessional boundary; their texts in fixed editions are not yet opened, and the Part cites no conciliar wording for its archetype claim).

The corpus draws the immediate consequence: "God's love requires no created object to complete it." The consequence is load-bearing. If God's love needed creation to be actual, creation would be necessary to God, the archetype would be partly constituted by its image, and the treatise's direction would reverse: the created order would complete the pattern instead of imaging it. The texts run the other way: the love is eternal (John 17:24); creation is its free overflow and image, not its missing term. [Theological synthesis, HIGH as a consequence of the corpus's texts read together; its archetype use is section 5.]

## 3. The two poles of divine affection (T-I.2)

An archetype of love drawn only from God's eternal self-sufficiency would risk picturing a God whose love never touches the world he made with anything but calm benevolence at a distance. The canon forbids that picture in the same breath with which it forbids the opposite one, and Part I receives both poles as data (axiom I.1).

The anchor study (study 02, on Genesis 6:5-7) established the first pole. God's regret (nacham) over human wickedness is real and moral in object: it "expresses sorrow and regret over what humanity had become, grounded in the moral verdict of 6:5," and the construction is repeated by the canon itself at 1 Samuel 15 for Saul's appointment, so it is not a narrator's loose figure awaiting correction (study 02, finding 1). [Explicit text, HIGH.] His grief (atsav, in the Hithpael, "to his heart") is likewise real felt pain, "in a pattern Scripture repeats (Psalm 78:40; Isaiah 63:10)"; a reading that evacuates the affection entirely "must explain away a settled biblical pattern, not one verse" (study 02, finding 2). [Explicit text, HIGH.]

The second pole stands in the same canon and, at 1 Samuel 15, in the same chapter: neither verb asserts ignorance, error, or reversal of character. Nacham's range includes relenting toward the penitent and pity toward sufferers, neither of which implies prior mistake; and the canon's own qualification (Numbers 23:19; 1 Samuel 15:29) "denies man-like relenting, lying, and caprice, while affirming regret in the same breath" (study 02, finding 3). The study's summary is the axiom's: "The text fixes two poles: genuine divine affection in time, and no instability of character or counsel." [Canonical synthesis, HIGH for the two poles as textual data.]

Two readings of the first pole are declined, on the study's grounds, because the archetype claim depends on the pole surviving both. The open-theist reading (God learns, is surprised, revises) requires an ignorance premise the text does not supply: the regret responds to wickedness evaluated, in a narrative where judgment's trajectory is already announced (study 02, sec. 5, reading 1). The pure-metaphor reading (the verbs describe effects in creatures only, with no affection in God) is declined because the Hithpael with el-libbo and the Psalm 78 / Isaiah 63 pattern resist evacuation (study 02, sec. 5, reading 2). Against the second, the study states the classical doctrine correctly, and Part I adopts the statement as T-I.2: "The classical doctrine of impassibility, properly stated, does not require this evacuation; it denies involuntary, destabilizing passion, not genuine affection."

What Part I does not do with the poles is reconcile them metaphysically. Study 02 assigns that work to Part V, "with the broader corpus (Augustine, Aquinas on divine will and mercy) in view," and the audit repeats the assignment: the impassibility account "is named as Part V work in study 02, sec. 5, and remains unwritten" (audit item (viii)); both interlocutors stand at NOT YET CONSULTED in this Part's register in any case (entries 5 and 7). The archetype claim proceeds with the poles held as canonical data: the God whose life is the archetype genuinely grieves over evil and genuinely does not change. A treatise that resolved the tension by deleting either pole would have a simpler archetype and a false one.

One further datum belongs to the archetype's portrait, because it shows affection and government in one act rather than in competition: "Regret and decree are not set against each other in this text. Verse 7 makes the regret itself the stated ground of an act of judgment that the wider canon presents as purposeful" (study 02, finding 5). The grief is morally serious, grounded in a verdict on evil (finding 1), and it issues in purposeful judgment. Part V builds the theodicy on that joint; Part I registers it so the archetype is not misread as sentiment.

## 4. One God, one will

The archetype is the life of one God, not the negotiated outcome of three. Axiom I.3 states the discipline: "God's will is one and undivided; the distinction between his secret counsel and his revealed precept is a distinction of manner and of our apprehension, not two contrary wills in God." Its basis is Calvin's framing in Institutes 1.18.3, as carried in the research note on Augustine and Calvin (sec. 3.3; Beveridge, read directly for that note): Calvin's sec. 3 framing is one undivided will, manifold to us, not two contrary wills. [Theological synthesis, HIGH within the treatise's confessional frame.]

The axiom does three kinds of work for the archetype. First, it protects the archetype's unity: the pattern creatures image is one life and one will, so the good it grounds is one good, not a compromise among divine interests. Second, it protects the decree against a misreading Part V will need to avoid: when Scripture speaks of what God does contrary to his precept (the hardening and governance material of Institutes 1.18), the resolution is not two wills at war in God but one will apprehended under different aspects, secret counsel and revealed precept. Third, it fixes the level of Part VII's two-wills doctrine, so the Parts cannot be played against each other: the will belongs to the nature, while willing belongs to the person; in God, three persons and one will, while in the incarnate Christ there are two natural wills because there are two natures in the one person (audit item (xiv), carrying Turretin, Thirteenth Topic, VII.xiv, as reported in the two-wills note, sec. 3.1). Part I states the distinction here because its archetype language will otherwise be misheard at the wrong level: the persons' communion is not three wills in concord, but one undivided life and will, personally distinct.

## 5. The archetype stated (T-I.1)

With the datum (sec. 2), the poles (sec. 3), and the unity (sec. 4) in place, the archetype claim can be stated exactly, in the axiom's own terms.

**Axiom I.2.** "Triune loving communion, God's own eternal life of love, is the moral archetype of the created order: the pattern from which the treatise draws its account of the good, its definition of sin, and the shape of the consummated state." Basis: the work package (secs. 1, 2A), as the governing thesis of the internal corpus; study 11, finding 6 (the consummation as communion); study 02, findings 1-2 (divine love and grief are real). **Tag: Theological synthesis, MEDIUM.** The axiom itself records why the tag is not higher: "The broader-corpus novelty check for this claim is a Phase 2 register task and has not been performed; the axiom is carried as the corpus's governing thesis, not as a verified originality claim."

Four clarifications fix what "archetype" means in the treatise's use, each excluding a misreading.

**Pattern, not copy by essence.** To call God's life the archetype of created morality is to say that created goodness is goodness by imaging that life, not by sharing its substance. The corpus states the guard in its anthropology: the imago Dei account "does not entail that creatures share God's essence" (Triune Loving Communion systematic theology, v0.1, sec. 3). The archetype relation is analogical: creaturely love is true love, really like God's, at the creature's measure; it is not a portion of the divine essence distributed to creatures. This guard is what keeps the archetype claim inside the creator-creature distinction while still letting it do normative work: the image is measured by the archetype precisely because it is not the archetype.

**Communicated, not merely contemplated.** The archetype is the good as God's own life communicated: the created order does not merely resemble the pattern at a distance; it is made for participation in it. The corpus's word for the created side is communion, covenantal relational access to God, granted unilaterally by God (Covenant and Communion, Definition 4), and its core thesis holds that human beings are born as image-bearers "with actual communion, not merely capacity" (Covenant and Communion, Part V, Core Thesis, item 1). The archetype thus grounds a morality of participation: creaturely goods are good as they belong to, image, or serve communion with the God whose life communion is. Part IX draws the ethical consequences; Part I states the ground.

**One principle, three derivations.** The candidate contribution the work package names is that the archetype is a single principle doing three jobs the fields usually source separately: "triune loving communion as the moral archetype that grounds the Euthyphro resolution, the definition of sin, and the shape of consummation in a single principle" (work package, sec. 1). Section 6 argues the first derivation (the good). The second is Part IV's: sin is the archetype's privation and opposite, the self closed in on itself against the communion it was made for (axiom IV.1). The third is Part VIII's: the consummated state is the archetype's created image confirmed, communion in act (axiom VIII.5; sec. 7.3 below). The claim is not that the derivations are obvious; it is that they are one derivation, from one archetype, and that the treatise's coherence stands or falls with that unity.

**Revealed, not constructed.** The archetype is identified from God's self-revelation, not assembled from religious ideals and then attributed to God. The corpus's Axiom 2 disciplines its Axiom 3 in exactly this direction: "We know God's self-revealed nature through Scripture alone. We do not construct God philosophically and then read Scripture through that lens" (Covenant and Communion, Axiom Interactions). The Part's argument therefore runs from texts to the archetype and only then to the derivations; the philosophical material (secs. 6, 8) tests and illustrates the revealed archetype, never generates it. Section 8's dyad discipline is the standing example of that order enforced.

## 6. The Euthyphro resolution

The dilemma the resolution answers is the one the register names as this Part's classical counter-position (register, Part I, entry 1: Plato, Euthyphro, NOT YET CONSULTED; no edition of record is yet fixed, and the Part accordingly quotes no line of the dialogue and rests nothing on its text). In the form in which the question has come down in the discipline, it asks whether the good is good because God wills it, or God wills it because it is good. The treatise contests both horns as exhaustive (register, Part I, entry 1, take/contest). This section states each horn at its strength, then the resolution, then the refinement the archetype adds to the standard resolution.

**The first horn: a standard above God.** If God wills the good because it is good, and the good's goodness is independent of God, then there is a standard to which God conforms: goodness is a reality God consults, not one he is. The horn's strength is real: it protects the meaningfulness of "God is good" and protects morality from caprice. Its cost is decisive within the treatise's premises: it divides the ultimate. Something other than God would be uncreated, eternal, and normative over him, and his aseity would be qualified by a co-eternal norm. The corpus makes the parallel point about logic, in an axiom this Part adopts as its model: "The law of non-contradiction is grounded in God's nature, not in abstract logic floating above Him" (Covenant and Communion, Axiom 1). A moral order floating above God is the first horn under another name.

**The second horn: bare will.** If the good is good only because God wills it, with his character out of account, then willing is the whole story: had God willed cruelty for its own sake, cruelty would be good, and "God is good" reduces to "God wills what God wills." The horn's strength is also real: it protects divine sovereignty against any norm binding God from outside, and it takes seriously that there is no court above his will. Its cost is that it severs will from nature. The corpus's axiom excludes the result by name: Axiom 1's exclusions bar "voluntarism (God's will as arbitrary)," on the ground that God "is the ground of coherence itself," cannot lie (Titus 1:2, as carried in the corpus axiom), and cannot deny himself (2 Timothy 2:13, as carried in the corpus axiom). A will that could deny God's character would not be freer than his; it would be a different god's.

**The resolution: the good is who God is.** The treatise's resolution (axiom IX.2) refuses the dilemma's shared assumption, that God's will and the good are two things needing an order of priority between them. "The good is neither a standard external to God, which he conforms to, nor an arbitrary product of bare will: God's will is itself the highest rule of perfection, the law of all laws, and it is the will of the triune communion whose character is love (axiom I.2), so that what God wills is good because of who God is." The second clause carries Calvin's formulation: God's will is "the highest rule of perfection... the law of all laws" (Institutes 3.23.2; Battles as quoted in the research note on Augustine and Calvin, sec. 3.6; CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION, and cited as such). Calvin's point, on the note's showing, is not that will alone makes right; it is that God's will needs no rule above it because it is God's. The treatise's axiom completes the sentence with the character clause: the will that is the highest rule is the will of the triune communion whose character is love. Priority questions dissolve because the relata are one life: God does not consult a standard (first horn refused), and his willing is not characterless (second horn refused); his willing is his loving nature in act. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM, per axiom IX.2; the MEDIUM is inherited from the archetype premise, axiom I.2, and is printed wherever the resolution is stated.]

**The refinement: the good has a shape.** A standard divine-command resolution stops at identity: the good is God's nature, and God's commands express it. The archetype claim adds content to the identity. God's nature is not goodness in the abstract; it is, specifically, triune loving communion (sec. 2). The good therefore has a discernible shape before any command is given: whatever conforms to, images, or serves communion with God and neighbor is good in the pattern's terms, and whatever closes the self against that communion is evil in the pattern's terms. This is what lets one principle ground both the resolution and the definition of sin (Part IV) instead of merely adjoining them, and it is the precise sense in which the archetype is load-bearing rather than decorative: remove the communion specification, and the resolution still stands as an identity claim, but the treatise's definition of sin loses its positive term and its derivation of the consummation loses its pattern. [Theological synthesis; the refinement is the treatise's own construction from axioms I.2 and IX.2, and carries their MEDIUM.]

Two honest limits close the section. First, the resolution addresses the dilemma's metaphysical form (where goodness resides), not the epistemic question (how creatures know the good), which the treatise answers through revelation and the revealed law in Parts II and VI, under the boundary of axiom PRO.1. Second, the dialogue that names the dilemma is unopened in this project (sec. 6, above): when the Part is revised after the Euthyphro is consulted in a fixed edition, the statement of the horns will be checked against the dialogue's own text. The resolution's premises are the treatise's axioms and the Calvin locus, not the dialogue, so the argument does not wait on that consultation to be stated; it waits on it only to be quoted precisely.

## 7. The communion structure in the anchor texts

The archetype is a Level 1 claim (sec. 1), and its scriptural footing is assembled, not quoted from a single locus: no anchor text says "God's triune life is the moral archetype." What the studies supply is the structure the synthesis assembles: where communion is located, how God acts toward the Son within it, how it appears when perfected, and what its intentions look like in a single event. Each study's contribution is set out at its own tag, so the reader can see how much of the archetype is text, how much inference, and how much synthesis.

### 7.1 Ephesians 1:3-14: communion's location and grounds

Study 06's sentence is the treatise's fullest single picture of the archetype at work toward creatures, and its findings fix three structural facts.

First, every blessing is located in Christ. The berakah "speaks in plurals, locates every blessing en Christo," and its verbs take persons as objects (exelexato hemas; proorisas hemas) before the foundation of the world (study 06, finding 7). The sentence's form is "corporate in form, personal in object, Christological in location." [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH.] For the archetype, the location is the point: created communion is not a generic God-ward relation but life inside the Son's own relation to the Father, extended to persons. The corpus's Christological text says the same from the other side: "John 17:20-23 connects believers' unity to the communion of Father and Son" (Triune Loving Communion systematic theology, v0.1, sec. 6). The archetype's image is therefore not a copy of triune life produced at a distance; it is an incorporation into it, on the terms the incorporation texts state.

Second, the grounds of the whole movement are exclusively on God's side. Choosing, predestining, purposing, and working are all God's acts, and "the grounds named are his pleasure (eudokia, 1:5, 9), his purpose (prothesis, 1:11), the counsel of his will (bouleh tou thelematos, 1:11). No human quality, act, or foreseen response is named anywhere in the sentence as a ground or condition" (study 06, finding 1); and the acts are pretemporal, dated pro kataboles kosmou (finding 2). [Explicit text, HIGH, both findings.] Communion with creatures thus originates where the archetype says it must: in the eternal God's own determination, not in a creaturely initiative God then blessed. Part II argues the decree from this sentence; Part I takes the direction of flow: communion descends from God's life; it is not assembled upward.

Third, the summing up displays the archetype's scope. The purpose is "to sum up all things in Christ" (anakephalaiosasthai ta panta en to Christo, 1:10), glossed by the merism "things in heaven and things on earth," and 1:11's God works that same totality according to his counsel (study 06, findings 3-4). [Explicit text, HIGH for the corroborant proposition; Necessary inference, HIGH for the scope.] Study 06, finding 5, binds the use: 1:11 specifies domain, not mode; it states no per-event benevolent telos and no reprobation. Part I draws only what it states: the archetype's God as the one whose working admits no remainder outside his counsel (axiom II.1, carried here as support, argued in Part II).

One further datum from the same verse belongs to the archetype's portrait of mutuality, at its lexical tag. Eklerothemen (1:11) is best read "we were made God's own portion": believers are claimed as God's heritage, the majority rendering "we have obtained an inheritance" remaining defensible but not lexically stronger (study 06, finding 6; BDAG reached through the NET note, mediation labeled in the study). [Lexical verdict, MEDIUM-HIGH.] Part I receives it as congruence, not pillar: the communion is, in the sentence's own diction, mutual in this precise sense, that God takes the heirs as his portion even as they receive him as theirs. The finding expressly "does not load on the corroborant," and it loads on nothing here either.

### 7.2 Hebrews 13:20: the covenant act toward the Mediator

Study 10's verse shows the archetype in execution at its most concentrated point: God's dealings with the Son himself. The benediction's grammar "makes God the sole actor and Jesus the raised object" (study 10, finding 1) [Explicit text, HIGH]; the raising was effected "in virtue of the covenant blood" (finding 4, en haimati attaching to anagagon) [Necessary inference, MEDIUM-HIGH]; the covenant is the new covenant of the epistle's own argument (finding 5) [Necessary inference, HIGH]; and so "the resurrection is depicted as a covenant act: God's performance, toward the Mediator himself, of what the covenant blood secured," the raising being "the covenant's first executed consequence, rendered to the shepherd before it flows to the sheep" (finding 7, Owen's exposition entering as labeled mediation). [Necessary inference, MEDIUM-HIGH.]

Part I's use of the verse is disciplined by the study's own limits (finding 9), printed here because they mark the Level boundary of section 1: the verse names no covenanting parties in eternity; its aionios is lexically endless, not beginningless; its diction is covenantal, not decretal. Study 10 therefore contributes a pattern, not a premise for Level 1: within the executed covenant, God's action toward the Son is faithful, effectual, and first, which is the shape communion's restoration takes when the archetype acts in history. The eternal counsel behind the pattern is Part II's argument (axioms II.6, II.7, where this verse stands as a chain member, not a source); Part I claims only that the pattern is congruent with the archetype: the God who raises the shepherd in virtue of the covenant blood acts as the communion God toward his own Son, and the sheep receive communion by receiving its first consequence.

### 7.3 Revelation 21-22: the archetype displayed

If Ephesians 1 shows communion's location and Hebrews 13 its execution, Revelation 21-22 shows its finished form, and study 11's findings supply the display on which thesis T-VIII.4 and axiom VIII.5 rest. The inhabitants of the consummated city are God's douloi who render him latreia, see his face, and reign forever and ever (22:3-5): "Rest in the consummation is not inactivity, and rule is not autonomy: both verbs describe a communion in which being God's own and exercising dominion coincide permanently" (study 11, finding 6). [Explicit text for the simultaneity of service and reign, HIGH; Theological synthesis for the communion framing, MEDIUM-HIGH.] The state is fenced by absolute negations (finding 1: no death, mourning, crying, or pain; no cursed thing; no night; nothing common entering the city) [Explicit text, HIGH], populated by gift and cleansing rather than attainment (finding 7) [Explicit text, HIGH], and its curse-less condition is grounded in presence: pan katathema is absent, "and the immediately following clause locates the throne of God and of the Lamb in the city: the text's own logic ties the curse's absence to the presence, not to the mere passage of time or the exhaustion of evil" (finding 8). [Necessary inference, MEDIUM-HIGH.]

For the archetype, the display matters in one specific way: the consummation is not a different good from the beginning's pattern but the same communion with its enemies excluded and its duration made maximal. The end is God's dwelling with his people (21:3; "Revelation 21:1-4 describes consummated dwelling with God," Triune Loving Communion systematic theology, v0.1, sec. 6), the archetype's own content at created scale and without remainder; and the end exceeds Eden on Eden's own terms, described "with exactly the vocabulary Genesis withheld" (study 11, finding 5). [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH.] Part VIII argues the consummation; Part I registers that the archetype is visible at both ends of the canon: the life God is eternally (sec. 2), and the life he brings his people into at the end, with creation and redemption as its imaging and restoring between.

### 7.4 Genesis 50:20: the archetype's intention in a single event

Study 03 contributes the archetype's moral signature at the level of one historical complex. One verb (chashav) governs both clauses of Genesis 50:20, and its constructions differ: "The brothers devise evil (accusative) against Joseph; God intends the deed (accusative suffix) for good (lamed of destination)"; so "asymmetrical intention is the verse's own grammar, not a theological gloss laid over it" (study 03, findings 1-3). [Explicit text for the constructions, HIGH; Necessary inference for the asymmetry, HIGH.] The good intended is concrete: the lema'an clause specifies "the keeping alive of a numerous people" (finding 4). [Explicit text, HIGH.]

Part I draws one narrow conclusion, at the study's own scope: the verse is confined to the Joseph complex, any universal premise being assigned to Ephesians 1:11 and Romans 8:28-30 (finding 6). The claim is only this: when the archetype God's intending is exhibited in a single event, he intends the preservation of a people, a communion-good, and never intends the evil as evil. The asymmetry is the archetype's fingerprint in providence: the life that is communion in itself intends communion-goods in its government, and evil enters its intentions only as the deed's dark side, borne and redirected, never devised. [Theological synthesis from findings 1-4, MEDIUM: the grammar is HIGH, the archetype framing is this Part's construction.] Part V builds the theodicy on the asymmetry (axiom V.1); Part I cites the verse so the archetype is not an eternal abstraction with no exhibited relation to the evil it will be asked to govern.

## 8. The dyad discipline: communion's fullness argued without a mythology of number

The internal corpus contains a heuristic this Part must handle with exact care, because mishandled it contradicts the Part's own thesis. The v0.1 systematic theology states it compactly: "One is regressive. Two is obsessive. Three is expressive. This heuristic distinguishes reflexive self-reference, reciprocal dyadic relation, and genuinely triadic shared communion. 'Obsessive' applies to the closed-loop model rather than all two-person relationships. A triadic relation requires three distinct participants. That analytic fact does not independently prove that perfect divine love logically requires exactly three persons. God's self-revelation establishes the theological identity; the relational model evaluates explanatory coherence" (Triune Loving Communion systematic theology, v0.1, sec. 2). The argument map carries the corresponding propositions at their own tags: P11, "Triadic relation is categorically different from dyadic reciprocity," a formal relational observation, HIGH in the structural sense; P12, "Three persons are parsimoniously sufficient for complete divine communion," a philosophical thesis, UNCERTAIN as independent necessity (argument map, as inventoried in the research note on the closed dyad, sec. 2.2). Scrutiny item 2 of the corpus-alignment record required the discrimination this section performs, and the research note on the closed dyad (cited below as the dyad note) has tested the distinction and issued its verdict. This section applies that verdict.

### 8.1 The formulation the treatise uses

The dyad note's approved formulation (sec. 6.1), adopted here for Part I, reads:

> "The treatise uses 'closed dyad' only as a defined term: a two-term relation stipulated to terminate in its own members, neither member ordered beyond the relation. So defined, closure is a property of orientation, not of number. A dyad may be open: marriage is God's own remedy for aloneness, a covenant ordered to him and fruitful beyond itself, and the covenant with Israel is two-party in form and universal in intent. A company of any size may be closed: Babel is a multitude incurved on its own name. God is not confessed as triune because three is a better number than two; he is confessed as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit because he has revealed himself so. The revealed communion is the archetype because of what it is, perfect mutual and shared love, not because of its cardinality. The scale one reflexive, two reciprocal, three shared may illustrate structural differences among relation-forms (a triad can share a beloved, witness, and mediate as a dyad cannot); it demonstrates nothing about God."

Every sentence of that formulation is doing discipline work, and the rest of this section shows the grounds. The core of it is a definition and a disjunction: "closed" names self-termination, a property of a relation's orientation; and orientation varies independently of cardinality, so no moral conclusion may be drawn from a count.

### 8.2 The grounds: orientation, not number

The dyad note's scriptural survey runs one way. "No biblical text predicates the moral quality of a relation on the number of its terms" (dyad note, sec. 4.1). The dyad is God's own remedy for aloneness (Genesis 2:18), made "one flesh" at 2:24; Ecclesiastes 4:9 commends the pair, its threefold cord (4:12) being a proverb of strength, not a moral ontology of number; marriage is a covenant dyad with God as witness (Malachi 2:14), ordered beyond itself to fruitfulness (Genesis 1:28); and the supreme covenant administration is two-party in form, God and Israel, and universal in intent (Genesis 12:3). [Finding 1 of the dyad note: Necessary inference from the texts surveyed, HIGH.]

The counterexamples close the disjunction from both sides (dyad note, sec. 4.2). Open dyads: marriage; the God-Israel covenant; David and Jonathan, a two-person covenant friendship that opens outward at cost to Jonathan's own succession; and the two criminals of Luke 23:39-43, in identical dyadic situations, one closing in mockery and one opening in plea, so that "the variable is orientation to Christ, not the number of terms." Closed multitudes: Babel (Genesis 11:4), a whole society, maximal cardinality, closed in on its own name. And the sharpest counterexample is internal to the corpus: the pactum salutis is classically framed between Father and Son, two persons named as parties (the pactum note's Witsius definition), "and no one in the tradition calls the eternal counsel obsessive"; if two-ness as such carried a taint of closure, the treatise's Part II materials would be incoherent at their center. [Finding 2 of the dyad note: closure and openness vary independently of the number of a relation's terms, Necessary inference, HIGH.]

What survives the counterexamples is the structural residue, worth keeping at its own weight. A triadic relation has structural possibilities a dyadic relation lacks: two terms can share a third as a common object of love; a third can mediate, witness, and multiply the lines of relation. "This is a real categorical difference of form" (dyad note, sec. 4.3), the legitimate content of P11. But structural richness is not moral superiority, and the note renames the model's predicate accordingly: obsession is a psychological disorder of a person's attention, which a structural model cannot have; the closed-loop model is accurately named **self-terminating**, a relation whose love is stipulated to end in its own members. [Finding 3 of the dyad note: the distinction is sound exactly and only as a stipulative distinction between relation-forms, with closure defined as self-termination; as a description of two-person relationships, or a moral ranking of cardinalities, it is false. Analytic, HIGH.]

### 8.3 Richard of St Victor, at secondary mediation

The nearest classical relative of the corpus's scale is Richard of St Victor's argument in De Trinitate III, and the dyad note treats it at **secondary mediation throughout** (through Leithart's essay quoting De Trinitate III; the trinities.org paraphrase of chs. 14-19; and Ngien's journal article on the condilectus; De Trinitate was not read directly for the note, and it is not read directly here). Part I presents Richard only in that flagged form, and only for the shape of his argument, which the three secondary sources state consistently.

As reported: charity cannot be solitary; mutual love between two (dilectio) is true love; consummate charity requires condilectio, shared love, "when a third person is loved by two persons harmoniously and in community," the co-beloved being the condilectus. Three observations follow (dyad note, sec. 4.4). First, Richard does not pathologize the pair: two-person mutual love is genuine love, lacking not health but a further perfection, the sharing of delight in a co-beloved; the corpus's word "obsessive" has no counterpart in Richard. Second, his operative variable is sharing, not counting: the perfection is love that wills its joy to be shared, and a third person is the first occasion of that sharing, which supports the orientation finding (sec. 8.2) rather than any ranking of numbers. Third, Richard argues inside faith, from "God is love" (1 John 4:8) as a revealed datum; he does not offer the scale as an independent proof that God must be three. The corpus's own posture (P12 at UNCERTAIN; the model evaluates coherence, revelation establishes identity) "is exactly Richard's posture, and Part I should keep it."

A caution from the other classical direction is recorded in the same note and is adopted here with its flag: Augustine's Trinitarian triads in De Trinitate are intra-personal (memory, understanding, will within one mind), not three participants, so the tradition's own use of "three" is analogical and varies its referent (general knowledge; Augustine's De Trinitate not consulted in text for the note, and standing at NOT YET CONSULTED in this Part's register). Cardinality language about God is therefore doubly analogical, and the corpus's simplicity guard applies: intra-Trinitarian "relation" is not a creaturely relation with more terms (dyad note, sec. 4.4; Triune Loving Communion systematic theology, v0.1, sec. 9).

### 8.4 The three senses of "closed," and the rule for Part I

The dyad note closes by separating the three senses the corpus's most loaded adjective now carries (sec. 5): the **closed self** (moral: a person's fallen orientation, the subject of axiom IV.1); the **closed dyad or loop** (structural: the stipulated relation-form of this section); and **epistemically closed** (the Covenant and Communion volume's warrant sense, "unwarranted to assert"). The senses differ in subject and scope: the closed self can inhabit a relation of any size, and the heuristic's own scale assigns self-reflexive closure to the "one" position, not the "two"; the incurved self is the monadic case. Part I therefore observes the note's rule, which is also this Part's rule for every use of the word: **"closed" predicates orientation wherever it is used; when it is used of a relation, the relation's self-termination must be shown from the case, never inferred from its size.**

The consequence for the archetype claim is direct, and it strengthens rather than weakens T-I.1. The archetype is not "three-person communion" as a numerical exhibit; it is the revealed communion, perfect mutual and shared love, whose triune form is confessed because God has revealed himself as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The heuristic (one reflexive, two reciprocal, three shared) may be quoted in this Part only as a heuristic, labeled as such, with "obsessive" replaced by the accurate predicate (self-terminating), and only under the formulation of section 8.1. Whether the heuristic is retained in the finished treatise at all is a drafting decision reserved to the treatise's owner (dyad note, sec. 7, item 2); this draft retains it in corrected form because the archetype's apologetic presentation (Part IX) uses the scale's illustration of structural difference, and the correction costs the argument nothing it was entitled to.

For the record, the dyad note's must-not-claim list (sec. 6.2) binds this Part in full: no claim that two-person relationships are obsessive, deficient, or fallen as such; none that perfect love logically requires exactly three persons; no interchange of "closed dyad" with "closed self," and no work for the dyad term in the definition of sin; no suggestion that the pactum's Father-Son framing is or suffers from a closed dyad; no ontology of number from Matthew 18:20 or Ecclesiastes 4:12; and no ranking of companies by size for openness, love, or nearness to the archetype.

## 9. Counter-positions in strongest form

The strongest-form rule (Prolegomena, sec. 4.5) binds this Part on three fronts: the unitarian alternative to the archetype, the purely voluntarist alternative to its Euthyphro resolution, and the social-trinitarianism cautions against its own preferred way of speaking. Each is stated as its ablest defender would state it, and each answer is kept at the level of the objection (axiom X.1).

### 9.1 The unitarian archetype

Stated strongly: a solitary God is not obviously a deficient archetype. A unitarian God can be confessed as loving: he loves himself with perfect rectitude, and he loves creatures with a love that, because it begins with creation, is wholly gracious, owed to nothing and elicited by nothing. On this account the treatise's archetype is an unnecessary multiplication: the moral pattern could be a single perfect person's ordered self-love and benevolence, and the Trinity would be a doctrine about God's inner life with no special archetypal yield.

The answer grants the position's coherence at the level of bare possibility and presses its archetypal consequences, which is where the objection lives. If the eternal archetype of love is a solitary person's self-love, then self-love is not merely permitted by the pattern; it is the pattern's whole eternal content. Creaturely self-love ordered under God would image the archetype directly, and love of another would be a temporal addition to the good rather than its eternal form. The treatise's root distinction (communion against closure; axiom IV.1) would lose its archetypal ground: closure could no longer be defined as the eternal pattern's opposite, since a pattern of perfect self-regard is, in form, what closure imitates at creaturely scale. Conversely, if the unitarian archetype locates eternal love's object in creatures, love becomes contingent on creation, and section 2's difficulty returns in its sharpest form: God would need the world to be what the treatise confesses him eternally to be. The triune archetype avoids both: love's eternal object is internal to God's life, so love of another is the good's eternal form, and creation remains free overflow rather than metaphysical need. [Theological synthesis, argued from axioms I.2 and IV.1 and section 2's texts; the treatise's own argument, at the archetype's MEDIUM.]

The honesty clause is as important as the argument. The treatise nowhere claims that perfect love logically requires exactly three persons, or that reason unaided can demonstrate the Trinity from the concept of love: the corpus holds the numerical thesis at UNCERTAIN (P12), withdraws the descriptive dyad claim (sec. 8), and registers Richard's stronger construction at secondary mediation only (sec. 8.3). The treatise's order is the corpus's own: revelation establishes that God is triune; the archetype claim then observes what that revealed life, and no rival, supplies as the moral pattern. A unitarian interlocutor is answered at the level of the datum (sec. 2) and of archetypal consequence (this section), not by a numerical proof the treatise does not possess.

### 9.2 Pure voluntarism

Stated strongly: the voluntarist hears the resolution of section 6 as a evasion. Either God's will is bound by his character, in which case the character is a standard his will consults and the first horn returns under the name "nature"; or his will is free in the only sense worth defending, in which case it could have willed otherwise about anything, cruelty included, and the character clause is decoration. Voluntarism's defender adds that creaturely talk of God's "character" as a constraint imports creaturely psychology into God and compromises the freedom the doctrine of God exists to protect.

The answer accepts the dilemma's form and denies that its first alternative is the first horn. A standard is external, separable, and in principle surveyable apart from the one it binds; God's character is none of those. God's will does not consult his nature as a king consults a law code; his willing is his nature in act, and his inability to will against it is his inability to deny himself (2 Timothy 2:13, as carried in the corpus's Axiom 1), which the tradition counts as perfection, not limitation: God cannot lie, not because a rule prevents him, but because he is truth. The corpus's axiom states the ground (God "is the ground of coherence itself") and writes the exclusion of voluntarism into the axiom itself (Covenant and Communion, Axiom 1 and its exclusions); Calvin's formulation draws the same line from the will side (sec. 6). What voluntarism protects, God's freedom from external constraint, the resolution protects in full; what it surrenders, a distinction between God's willing and God's character, is one the texts do not draw: "God is love" (1 John 4:8) predicates what God is, and his willing is nowhere set against it. [Theological synthesis; corpus Axiom 1, axiom IX.2, and section 2's datum, at their tags.]

### 9.3 The social-trinitarianism cautions

The third counter-position is aimed at the treatise's own side, and it is the one this Part must answer most carefully, because it grants the Trinity and disputes the archetype's idiom. Stated strongly: talk of the Trinity as "communion" risks projecting creaturely sociality onto God. Human communities are aggregates of separate centers of consciousness who negotiate and deliberate; if the persons' eternal communion is modeled on that, then (a) the one will of axiom I.3 dissolves into concord among three wills, tritheism with good manners; (b) divine simplicity is compromised, since a society has parts; and (c) the archetype becomes anthropomorphic in the strict sense, reading the church's social ideals upward into God and back down with borrowed authority, a circle that generates ethics from projection rather than revelation.

The cautions are received as disciplines, and each is answered by a guard already in the treatise's set. (a) The communion confessed here is one undivided life and will, personally distinct (sec. 4): the persons do not deliberate toward agreement, and the audit's level discipline forbids picturing the eternal life as negotiation (audit item (xiv); axiom I.3). (b) The corpus's own objection register carries the simplicity guard in its shortest form: "Classical divine simplicity constrains relational analogies" (Triune Loving Communion systematic theology, v0.1, sec. 9), and the dyad note applies it to this Part's exact vocabulary: intra-Trinitarian relation is not a creaturely relation with more terms (sec. 8.3 above). (c) The direction of derivation is fixed by the corpus's method axiom: God's nature is known from Scripture, not constructed philosophically and read back (sec. 5, fourth clarification). The archetype's predicates are therefore analogical throughout: "communion" said of God and "communion" said of the church are neither univocal (the projection the caution fears) nor equivocal (which would void the archetype), but analogical, the created term a true and lesser likeness of the uncreated. [The analogical-predication framing is the treatise's standard guard, stated here as synthesis at the archetype's calibration.] Where the caution presses further, into the detailed doctrine of simplicity and processions, it meets this Part's registered frontier: Aquinas on God and the Trinity and Augustine's De Trinitate stand at NOT YET CONSULTED (register, Part I, entries 5 and 7), and the fuller engagement belongs to the revision of this Part after those texts are opened, and to Part X's register of objections in the meantime. The caution is recorded as standing, not as answered beyond the guards named.
