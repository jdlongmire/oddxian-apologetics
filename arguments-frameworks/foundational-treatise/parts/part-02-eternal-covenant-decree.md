## 1. The question and the theses

Part I has placed the treatise's archetype before the reader: God's own eternal life is triune loving communion, and that life is the pattern from which the treatise understands the good, sin, and the consummated state. Part II asks the next question in systematic order. How is God's eternal purpose toward creation and redemption to be stated? More particularly, how can the one God, whose will is not divided against itself, purpose all things in one counsel, choose a people in Christ before the foundation of the world, appoint a Mediator, and bring that purpose to execution without turning eternity into a negotiation, election into a response to foreseen human worth, or providence into a claim that every event is itself good?

The answer is developed under four connected theses.

**T-II.1.** The decree is God's one eternal counsel, and the pactum salutis (covenant of redemption) is its covenantal form toward the Mediator: formally distinct from the decree, not a second decree, and an appropriation of the one divine will, never a negotiation between wills. [Theological synthesis; decree unity HIGH within the treatise's confessional frame; pactum MEDIUM for scriptural explicitness and HIGH for attestation in the Reformed tradition from 1638 onward; axioms II.1, II.6, I.3.]

**T-II.2.** Election is unconditional and pretemporal, corporate in form and Christ-located, personal in its objects. [Explicit text, HIGH, for the divine grounds and pretemporal dating in Ephesians 1; Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH, for the individual entailment; axiom II.5.]

**T-II.3.** Romans 8:28's governing premise is secure against its textual variant, because Ephesians 1:11 supplies the agency that Romans 8:28's syntax and textual history leave distributed among construals. The premise does not depend on printing ho theos in Romans 8:28. [Necessary inference, HIGH; axioms II.1 and II.3.]

**T-II.4.** The resurrection of the Son is the covenant's first executed consequence, rendered to the Mediator on the ground of the covenant blood. [Necessary inference, MEDIUM-HIGH, for Hebrews 13:20's attachment and covenant-act reading; the verse's place in the pactum chain is Theological synthesis, MEDIUM; axiom II.7.]

These theses require three distinctions at the outset.

First, the eternal triune life, the eternal decree, and the pactum are not interchangeable names for one undifferentiated idea. The triune life is what God is eternally: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit in communion. It is not a transaction and has no parties, conditions, or promises. The decree is God's one eternal determination concerning what he will do and permit in the created order, stated in this Part chiefly as that determination comes into view in redemption. The pactum is the covenantal form in which a major stream of Reformed theology states the decree toward the Mediator. A sentence that moves directly from triune communion to a covenantal transaction, without passing through the decree and the scriptural collation, commits the level error identified in the Phase 2 consistency audit, item (vi). This Part therefore keeps the three levels distinct in every section. [Methodological rule; audit item (vi), disposition CONSISTENT WITH STATED DISCIPLINE.]

Second, the goodness of the decree is not the goodness of every decreed or permitted event considered in itself. Part II calls the decree good in four exact senses: its source is the God whose character Part I identifies as triune loving communion; its redemptive end is adoption, inheritance, conformity to the Son, resurrection, and glorification; its form is one coherent and undivided counsel; and its issue for the called is the Romans 8 good, worked through all things toward that Christological and eschatological end. None of this converts each event into a good event, supplies a visible reason for each evil, or settles the mode of God's working through secondary causes. Those questions belong principally to Part V. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH, inheriting the tags of axioms I.2, II.1, II.3, and II.4.]

Third, Deuteronomy 29:29 marks the Part's epistemic boundary. In context it assigns hidden covenant treachery and its judgment to the LORD and the revealed law to Israel for obedience; its extension to God's unrevealed counsel begins within the chapter but remains an extension. The rule that the invisibility of a morally sufficient reason does not prove that no such reason exists is a valid consequence of the verse's structure, not a statement of the verse, and the verse supplies no positive evidence about any particular case. [PRO.1, Explicit text, HIGH; PRO.2, Theological synthesis, MEDIUM; study 04, findings 1-7; audit item (v).]

### 1.1 Terminology for the eternal covenant

This Part uses "pactum salutis (covenant of redemption)" at first use, then "pactum" thereafter. ## 2. One good counsel: the decree in Ephesians 1 and Romans 8

The decree's textual base is not a single slogan detached from its sentences. Ephesians 1:3-14 and Romans 8:28-30 divide the labor. Ephesians supplies the divine grounds, the pretemporal dating, the domain of God's working, and election's corporate Christ-located form. Romans supplies RP's beneficiaries, the definition of its good, and the sequence from foreknowledge to glorification. Neither supplies everything: Ephesians 1:11 does not specify mode, state a per-event benevolent telos, or state reprobation; Romans 8:28 does not assert that each particular evil was instrumentally necessary or itself good. The audit calls the relation CONSISTENT: each study polices the other's use (item (iv)).

### 2.1 The grounds are on God's side

Ephesians 1 is a single sustained blessing of God for blessings given in Christ. Its grammar repeatedly places God as subject and locates the grounds of his acts in himself. He chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world (exelexato hemas en auto pro kataboles kosmou, 1:4). He predestined us for adoption as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the good pleasure of his will (proorisas hemas eis huiothesian dia Iesou Christou eis auton, kata ten eudokian tou thelematos autou, 1:5). He made known the mystery of his will according to his good pleasure, which he purposed in Christ (kata ten eudokian autou, hen proetheto en auto, 1:9). In Christ we were allotted, having been predestined according to the purpose of the one who works all things according to the counsel of his will (prooristhentes kata prothesin tou ta panta energountos kata ten boulen tou thelematos autou, 1:11).

The accumulation of determination language is emphatic, not taxonomic. Prothesis is the purpose set forth; in 1:9 the cognate verb proetheto presents that purpose as set forth in Christ, not as an abstract plan floating behind him. Bouleh is counsel: deliberated and settled resolve. Thelema is will or determination. Eudokia is good pleasure. Study 06 judges that the sentence gives one determination described under several aspects, not three decrees or a sequence of divine deliberations. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM, for the one-determination judgment; the lexical aspects are Explicit text in study 06, sec. 3.6.] Part II receives that judgment as a controlling rule. The decree is one because God is one and because the sentence's own rhetoric accumulates names for a single settled purpose. Multiplicity of aspect must not be converted into multiplicity of decrees.

That unity is also why the decree cannot be treated as bare will detached from God's character. The nouns are not synonyms for arbitrary force. Counsel is the will's deliberated form; purpose is the will set forth in Christ; good pleasure is the will's delight in the act. Part I's axiom I.3 adds the dogmatic guard: God's will is one and undivided, and the distinction between secret counsel and revealed precept is a distinction of manner and of human apprehension, not two contrary wills in God. [Theological synthesis, HIGH within the treatise's confessional frame.] The good decree is therefore neither a standard above God to which his will conforms nor an uncharactered fiat beneath explanation. It is the settled counsel of the triune God whose life is loving communion. The specifically Euthyphro-shaped development of that claim belongs to Parts I and IX; Part II presupposes it because the eternal covenant cannot be understood if the decree behind it is made into a transaction between strangers or an act of morally blank power.

### 2.2 The domain of the working

The decisive clause for domain is Ephesians 1:11: God is tou ta panta energountos, "the one who works all things." Study 06 finds the point grammatically unambiguous. Energeo is used transitively here, on Thayer's classification; the articular participle has God as its subject by the structure of the genitive chain; ta panta is the definite totality. The nearest antecedent in 1:10 glosses the same ta panta by a merism: "things in heaven and things on earth" are to be summed up in Christ (anakephalaiosasthai ta panta en to Christo). No narrowing signal follows in 1:11. The scope is therefore all things without exception within the created totality the sentence itself defines. [Necessary inference from discourse structure, HIGH; study 06, findings 3-4; axiom II.1.]

The exact force of "without exception" must be preserved. It concerns domain: no remainder lies outside God's counsel. It does not concern manner: the verse does not say how the working relates to secondary causes, creaturely intention, or evil as evil. It does not concern outcome at the level of each event: there is no eis agathon in 1:11, and no promise that every event is beneficial to every person. It does not concern reprobation: the non-elect are absent from the sentence, neither affirmed nor denied as an object of a decree in this clause. [Explicit text as a set of verified absences, HIGH; study 06, finding 5; axiom II.2.] The sentence's universal is a description of God, not a phenomenological claim about how events appear to sufferers.

### 2.3 The governing premise in Romans 8:28

Romans 8:28 states the treatise's governing premise, RP: for those who love God, those called according to his purpose, all things are worked together toward the good. The good is not defined by present relief or by the apparent moral quality of the events. It is defined forward and Christologically in 8:29-30: conformity to the image of the Son and glorification. The beneficiaries are doubly described as those who love God and as those called according to purpose; the second description interprets the first by grounding the people's identity in God's prothesis. [Explicit text, HIGH; study 05, findings 1-3; axiom II.3.]

The textual question at 8:28 is real. The shorter reading lacks the explicit subject ho theos; the longer reading makes God the grammatical subject. Study 05 judges the shorter reading most probably original, while stressing that the longer reading is an ancient clarifying addition rather than a late invention. [Textual verdict, MEDIUM-HIGH.] The doctrinal result does not rise or fall with that judgment. On the longer reading, divine agency is explicit in the verse. On the shorter reading with an embedded divine subject, it remains embedded in the grammar. On the shorter reading with panta as subject, Romans 8:28 asserts the convergence of all things toward good for the called without naming the agent in that clause; the agency is then carried by the immediate context, where God searches hearts in 8:27 and is the subject of every verb in 8:29-30, by the phrase kata prothesin, and by corroborating texts, above all Ephesians 1:11. Beneficiaries, comprehensiveness, and telos are identical on every attested reading; only the grammatical location of agency varies. [Necessary inference, HIGH; study 05, finding 5.]

Romans 8:29-30 then displays the decree as a sequence of divine acts: those whom God foreknew he predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son; those predestined he called; those called he justified; those justified he glorified. The verbs are aorist active indicatives with God as subject, and their objects are persons, not bare facts about persons. Glorification is stated in the same aorist series although, in the chapter's own horizon, it remains future and hoped for. The sequence has no human co-agent as a link. [Theological synthesis, HIGH, for the direction of the argument; study 05, finding 7; axiom II.8.] Part II calls this the decree chain, not because Romans uses that label, but because the passage itself binds the acts into one directed sequence whose security rests on the actor rather than on the beneficiary's performance.

Two limits govern every later use of RP. First, Romans 8:28 does not teach event-by-event indispensability. It does not say that a particular evil had to occur as an instrument, or that the event is good because it is included in panta. [Explicit text for the negative point, HIGH; study 05, finding 6; axiom II.4.] Second, the stronger universal claim that no suffering is gratuitous is not established by this verse alone. In the treatise's architecture that claim is a synthesis resting on wider theodicy premises and remains at MEDIUM where Part V states it (axiom V.4). Part II's narrower premise is secure precisely because it does not pretend to carry Part V's whole conclusion.

## 3. The boundary: secret and revealed things

Deuteronomy 29:29 is not a decree text in the same direct sense as Ephesians 1 or Romans 8. It supplies this Part's epistemic frame: "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever, that we may do all the words of this law." Its first doctrinal contribution is a boundary of belonging. A domain is reserved to the LORD; a domain is entrusted to his people forever; the entrusted domain is given for obedience. [Explicit text, HIGH; study 04, finding 1; PRO.1.]

### 3.1 The verse's own referents

Neither hannistarot ("the hidden things") nor hanniglot ("the revealed things") carries an object in the verse. Every specification of content must therefore be argued from context or canon, not assumed from the participles. [Explicit text, grammatical, HIGH; study 04, finding 2.] In the immediate context, the hidden side includes concealed covenant treachery and its judgment: the self-blessing man of 29:19 whose apostasy is hidden in his heart, whose detection and judgment belong to God. The revealed side is the law, named in the purpose clause as "all the words of this law." [Necessary inference from context, HIGH that these referents are in view and MEDIUM that they exhaust the verse's sense; study 04, finding 3.]

The chapter also begins the extension to God's future dealings. In 29:22-28 a later generation and foreigners ask why the land lies devastated; the answer identifies covenant cause without disclosing when, whether, or in what particulars such judgments will fall. The reserved domain therefore cannot be restricted to hidden human sins. Its extension to unrevealed counsel and future is legitimate, but it remains an extension beyond the primary judicial referent and must be cited as one. [Theological synthesis from context, MEDIUM-HIGH; study 04, finding 4; audit item (v).]

This ordering matters for the treatise's use of Calvin and Rashi. Study 04 accepts the contextual-judicial reading as primary in context and accepts Calvin's counsel reading as the verse's theological trajectory; it declines Calvin's dismissal of the judicial reading. The two readings are ordered, not flattened into rivals: judicial first in context, counsel by the verse's own abstraction and the chapter's future horizon. [Study 04, sec. 5.1-5.2.] Part II follows that order. It does not invoke Deuteronomy 29:29 as though Moses had written a chapter on predestination; it receives the boundary the verse establishes and the extension the chapter begins.

### 3.2 The epistemic rule as consequence

From the category of a real hidden domain, a limited epistemic consequence follows. If some things bearing on God's governance are legitimately withheld, then "no morally sufficient reason for this evil is visible to us" does not entail "there is no such reason." Study 04 calls this rule a valid consequence of the verse's structure, not a statement of the verse. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM; study 04, finding 6; PRO.2.] The same silence limits the rule's positive use. Deuteronomy 29:29 does not prove that a reason exists for a particular evil, identify that reason, or authorize a speaker to silence lament by pointing to the secret things. The lament guardrail is carried by the Psalms and Job, not by this verse. [Study 04, finding 7.]

The Westminster Confession's use is consistent with that discipline: WCF 3.8 cites the verse with Romans 9:20 and 11:33 for handling predestination with special prudence and care. Applied here, the boundary yields three rules: revealed grounds may be asserted; unrevealed mechanics may not be invented and called exegesis; and particular evils may not be explained by appeal to the category of the hidden. The verse may not be cited as addressing any particular evil.

## 4. Election in Christ

Election is the decree's redemptive concentration in Ephesians 1. The sentence does not begin with isolated individuals deciding their relation to God, nor with an abstract class awaiting members. It blesses the God and Father of Jesus Christ, who chose and predestined persons in Christ, in corporate plurals, before the foundation of the world. Part II's statement must preserve all four features together: divine grounds, pretemporal dating, corporate form, and personal objects.

### 4.1 Chosen and predestined to blessings

The choosing verb exelexato is aorist middle: God chose out for himself. Study 06, following Thayer's account of eklegomai, stresses both the personal object and the sphere: he chose us (hemas), and he chose us in him (en auto), so that the ground of the choice lies in Christ. The infinitive that follows states the end: that we should be holy and unblemished before him (einai hemas hagious kai amomous katenopion autou). Holiness is the choosing's purpose, not a prerequisite God observes before choosing. [Explicit text, HIGH; study 06, sec. 3.1 and finding 1.]

Proorizo, to mark out in advance or predetermine, occurs at 1:5 and 1:11 and in Romans 8:29-30. In Ephesians its objects are persons and its goals are blessings: adoption as sons in 1:5, and the allotted inheritance complex in 1:11. The sentence nowhere predestines anyone to unbelief or ruin, and it does not raise that subject. Part II records the silence as a limit on citation, not as a datum for either affirmation or denial. [Study 06, sec. 3.2 and finding 5(c).]

The dating is equally explicit. The choosing is pro kataboles kosmou, before the foundation of the world. Paul's pro- compounds accumulate around the sentence: proorisas (1:5), proetheto (1:9), prooristhentes (1:11), and the horizon of the fullness of times in 1:10. The determination precedes the history it governs. [Explicit text, HIGH; study 06, finding 2.] This dating is one reason the corporate reading's strong negative claim fails in section 6.6 below: an eternal act with persons as objects cannot have as its object a merely indeterminate group whose membership remains wholly future and unspecified in the purpose of God.

### 4.2 The inheritance clause and its limit

At 1:11 the passive eklerothemen has generated a real lexical debate. The majority rendering in English versions is "we have obtained an inheritance." Study 06 judges "we were made a heritage," or "we were appointed as God's own portion," lexically stronger: BDAG, as quoted in the NET note, classifies kleroo at this place as "we were appointed [as a portion] by lot," distinguishing kleroo from the more frequent kleronomeo, "obtain a portion, inherit." Robertson and the English Revised Version support the same direction, and the NET renders "we too have been claimed as God's own possession," with Deuteronomy 32:8-9 in view. The majority rendering remains intelligible from the sentence's wider inheritance vocabulary in 1:14 and 1:18, and Hoehner's discussion at Ephesians, pp. 226-27, is the standard modern treatment of the alternative. [Lexical verdict, MEDIUM-HIGH; study 06, sec. 3.8 and finding 6; register entry 40 for BDAG as CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION through the NET notes; entry 32 for Hoehner as CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION through the NET note.]

Part II adopts the lexically stronger reading with the study's limit attached: the prothesis and energountos clauses are invariant across the kleroo debate, so no scope finding in section 2.2 depends on it, and neither side of the lexical debate may recruit this Part's decree claims as if it did.

### 4.3 Corporate form, personal object

The berakah speaks in plurals and locates every blessing in Christ. Election is therefore corporate in form and Christ-located in every clause. Study 06 accepts that register without qualification: an account that begins from isolated individuals and adds Christ afterward has reversed Paul's order. At the same time, the verbs take persons as objects before the foundation of the world. The sentence's shape is corporate in form, personal in object, Christological in location. [Theological synthesis, MEDIUM-HIGH; study 06, finding 7; axiom II.5.]

The distinction between form and object prevents two reductions. Against bare individualism, the plurals, the en Christo saturation, and the we/you movement forbid imagining election as a list of autonomous selves unrelated to the body and its head. Against corporate-only reduction, the middle voice, the personal accusative hemas, and the pretemporal aorists forbid dissolving the objects into an unpopulated abstraction. The formula is individual by necessary entailment, not individualistic in presentation: determinacy of persons is claimed because grammar and dating jointly require objects; no roster and no mechanism of incorporation is claimed.

Romans 8:29 supplies an important control. Proginosko there has persons as its object: "those whom he foreknew" are those predestined to conformity. Study 05 notes the same use at Romans 11:2, where God has not rejected "his people, whom he foreknew." The treatise therefore reads foreknowledge in the decree chain as fore-appointment of persons, not bare prescience of their choices. [Explicit text, HIGH, for morphology and personal objects; study 05, sec. 3.4.] That reading is not imported into Ephesians 1, where proginosko is absent. Each passage is allowed its own vocabulary; their convergence is canonical synthesis, labeled as such.

### 4.4 Election and the decree chain

Election in Ephesians and the chain in Romans are not two competing maps. Ephesians states the eternal ground and goal: chosen in Christ, predestined to adoption and inheritance, according to pleasure, purpose, and counsel. Romans states the sequence by which the predestined end is secured: foreknowledge, predestination to conformity, calling, justification, glorification. In neither passage is faith named as the ground of the eternal acts. In Ephesians, believing appears in the order of experience at 1:13, when the hearers believe and are sealed with the promised Holy Spirit. In Romans, faith belongs to the justified life the chain produces and sustains, but it is not a link supplied by the beneficiary to complete an otherwise open sequence.

This concerns the named grounds of the eternal acts, not the reality of faith in the order of experience, which study 06 expressly preserves: believing at Ephesians 1:13 is real, sequential, and necessary, and holiness is election's stated end (1:4). Unconditionality here means that no human quality, act, or foreseen response is named as the reason God chose and predestined.

## 5. The eternal covenant: the pactum as the decree's covenantal form

The pactum is the Part's most tradition-dependent doctrine, and therefore the place where calibration matters most. The research note's verdict is exact: the eternal pact between Father and Son grounding the covenant of grace is genuinely attested in the Reformed tradition from 1638 onward, in full doctrinal form by the 1640s-1670s [HIGH]; and it both rests on scriptural appointment, giving, sending, obedience, and reward data and packages those data in a covenantal form no single text states in full [HIGH as a description of the construction]. Axiom II.6 therefore tags it Theological synthesis, MEDIUM for scriptural explicitness and HIGH for traditional attestation.

### 5.1 A guarded statement of the pactum

Part II states the doctrine as follows.

> The eternal counsel of redemption is fittingly stated in covenantal form as the pactum salutis (covenant of redemption): the Father appointing the Son to be Mediator, stipulating his obedience unto death, and promising him reward and headship; the Son undertaking suretyship for the elect; and the Holy Spirit named explicitly, not as a fourth negotiator and not as an afterthought, but as the Spirit in whose communion the counsel stands and by whom the Son's offering and the covenant's application are effected in the economy. The pactum is a theological construction by collation from the appointment, giving, sending, obedience, and reward texts. It is formally distinct from the decree and not a second decree. It is one divine will appropriated distinctly to the persons, never two wills bargaining. [Axiom II.6; Theological synthesis, MEDIUM for scriptural explicitness; HIGH for attestation.]

**The Father appointing the Son.** Appointment language preserves personal order without inventing inequality of essence. The Son is sent, given a people, given work, and promised a reward in the economy of redemption. Those relations are real because Scripture's giving and sending language is real. They do not make the Son a lesser god; Cocceius's formulation expressly identifies the unity of the divine essence at the point where the mediatorial office is appropriated to the Son (pactum note, sec. 1.2).

**Stipulating obedience unto death.** "Stipulation" names the ordered relation in the covenantal form between the Mediator's undertaking and the promised consequence. It does not name uncertainty in God, a possibility that the decree might fail, or a commercial contingency external to the divine purpose. The obedience is genuinely the Son's obedience as Mediator, and in its temporal execution it is the obedience of the incarnate Son treated in Part VII. It is not the human will of Christ appearing as a second divine party in eternity; audit item (xiv) bars that confusion. The eternal appropriation and the incarnate execution belong to related but distinct loci.

**The Son undertaking suretyship.** Hebrews calls Christ the engyos, surety, of a better covenant (Hebrews 7:22). The federal tradition gathers that suretyship into the eternal statement: the Son presents himself to do the Father's will, assumes responsibility for the elect as their head and sponsor, and restipulates the promised kingdom and glory. Witsius's definition, read directly in the English reproduction for Book II, chapter 2, sections 1-2, is the classic formulation: the compact is "the will of the Father, giving the Son to be the head and Redeemer of the elect; and the will of the Son, presenting himself, as a sponsor or surety for them." [Register entry 13, CONSULTED DIRECTLY for II.ii.1-2 in excerpt.]

**The Holy Spirit named explicitly.** Classical formulations are predominantly Father-Son in form because the appointment, sending, and obedience texts foreground those personal appropriations. Letham's omission charge is, at this point, the critique's fairest observation. Part II therefore names the Spirit in its own statement. The naming does not assign the Spirit a separate bargain or make him a fourth party. It confesses that the counsel is the counsel of the triune God and that the covenant's execution and application are not Father-Son transactions conducted outside the Spirit's personal agency. Grudem's 1994 restatement is registered because it makes the Spirit's applicatory role explicit; this Part follows that restating instinct while recording the mediation: Grudem's text was not opened and reaches the register through Clark's discussion. [Register entry 27, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.]

**One will appropriated distinctly.** This is the doctrine's central Trinitarian guard. Owen's formula, as quoted by Swain, says that the will of God with regard to the Father's peculiar actings is the will of the Father, and with regard to the Son's peculiar actings is the will of the Son, "not by a distinction of sundry wills, but by the distinct application of the same will unto its distinct acts in the persons of the Father and the Son." [Register entry 15, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION; entry 26, CONSULTED DIRECTLY in article excerpt.] The pactum therefore does not posit a conference at which an initially undecided deity reaches agreement. It states, in covenantal form, the one eternal will according to the distinct personal appropriations under which redemption is executed.

### 5.2 The scriptural collation

The pactum has no single proof text, and Part II does not manufacture one. Its evidence is a collation whose members contribute distinguishable data. The tradition's lists differ by author, a fact Richard A. Muller stresses: Dickson, Bulkeley, and Cloppenburg used largely different texts, indicating independent formulations from a shared exegetical background rather than one inherited proof mechanically repeated. [Register entry 18, CONSULTED DIRECTLY in substantial portions.] The collation may be grouped under five heads.

**Appointment and giving.** Psalm 2:7-8 joins the Father's decree ("You are my Son") to the grant of the nations as inheritance on the Son's asking; Gillespie's further claim that hoq ("decree") itself carries covenantal senses is his lexical argument, not settled philology, and is not used. [Pactum note, sec. 2, MEDIUM-HIGH.] Isaiah's servant texts add appointment and mission (42:1; 49:4-6; 50:5-9), and Ephesians 1:4-5, 2 Timothy 1:9, Titus 1:2, and 1 Peter 1:20 supply the decree-side frame: election in Christ and the Son foreknown before the foundation of the world. [HIGH for the grouped data.]

**The Son's consent and obedience.** Psalm 40:7-9, as applied in Hebrews 10:5-10, gives the Son's delight to do God's will. John 6:38-40 gives the mission form: the Son came down not to do his own will but the sender's, specified as a definite saving purpose, to lose nothing of what the Father gave him and to raise it at the last day. [Study 08, findings 1-4; Explicit text, HIGH.] John 17:4-6 gives the completed account: the work finished, the given ones kept. The texts establish consent, mission, and obedience as data; their gathering into eternal covenantal form remains synthesis.

**Stipulated reward.** Isaiah 53:10-12 joins the servant's offering to seed, prolonged days, the prospering of the LORD's pleasure in his hand, and a portion with the strong. Psalm 2 joins asking to inheritance. John 17 joins completed work to glory and to the given people. Hebrews 12's "for the joy set before him" is not in the Phase 1 pactum collation and is therefore not added here as though the note had verified it. The discipline is deliberate: Part II builds from the collation its sources actually examined.

**Suretyship.** Hebrews 7:22 calls Jesus the surety (engyos) of a better covenant, and 7:28 joins that status to the oath and the Son consecrated forever. Witsius's initial proof trio was Luke 22:29, Hebrews 7:22 and following, and Galatians 3:17, the last contributing the priority of the confirmed covenant concerning Christ to the later law. [Pactum note, sec. 2, HIGH for Hebrews; MEDIUM-HIGH for Galatians.] Suretyship is where the tradition's claim cannot avoid legal vocabulary: the Mediator stands in the place of responsibility for those given to him.

**Appointment in covenantal idiom.** Luke 22:29 has carried load-bearing weight because Jesus says the Father "appoints" (diatithemai) a kingdom to him as he appoints one to the disciples. Beza's 1567 Latin rendering gave the verb covenantal force against the Vulgate's dispono, and the register flags that the covenantal reading partly depends on that rendering. [Entry 8, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.] The verse is a genuine member of the collation, not a self-sufficient demonstration of the whole doctrine.

### 5.3 Hebrews 13:20: chain member, not source

Hebrews 13:20 calls God "the God of peace who brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus, the great shepherd of the sheep, by the blood of the eternal covenant" (en haimati diathekes aioniou). Study 10 gives the verse a disciplined and limited role.

First, the covenant whose blood was shed is the new covenant. Diatheke occurs within Hebrews' sustained first/new contrast (chapters 8-10 and 12); the blood is the blood that ratified the new covenant (9:15-20), sanctified the people (13:12), and speaks (12:24). No third covenant is introduced at 13:20. [Necessary inference from the epistle's usage, HIGH; study 10, finding 5.]

Second, aionios at this occurrence is classified by the consulted lexica under "without end," in company with eternal redemption (9:12) and eternal inheritance (9:15). Beginninglessness is a sense the word bears elsewhere, but it is not the sense assigned at this slot. [Lexical verdict, HIGH for the classification; study 10, finding 6.] Part II therefore calls the covenant eternal at Hebrews 13:20 in the sense of endless and irrevocable, not beginningless on the strength of this adjective.

Third, en haimati attaches to the raising (anagagon): the Father brought the shepherd up from the dead in virtue of the covenant blood. [Necessary inference, MEDIUM-HIGH; study 10, finding 4.] The resurrection is thus depicted as a covenant act: God's performance toward the Mediator himself of what the covenant blood secured, rendered to the shepherd before it flows to the sheep. [Necessary inference, MEDIUM-HIGH; study 10, finding 7.] Owen's exposition, consulted through a modern editor's excerpt, answers the question in those terms: by the blood atonement was made and the promises of the new covenant confirmed, so that the raising of Christ in the purpose of God necessarily ensued once satisfaction had been made. [Register entry 15, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.]

The verse consequently contributes two things in one clause: the covenant whose blood was shed is named eternal within the lexical limit stated, and the resurrection is enacted in that blood. Its refusals are equally explicit. It names no covenanting parties in eternity, states no pretemporal transaction, and distinguishes no covenant of redemption from the covenant of grace. It was absent from every classical proof list examined in the pactum note (Witsius's trio; the Cocceius and Witsius Psalm and Isaiah lists; Dickson's proofs; Berkhof's basis list) [HIGH as a statement about the lists examined]. This Part does not present it as a classical proof text, does not call its covenant beginningless on this adjective, and does not read decree vocabulary back into it. Study 10 also bars a verbal shortcut: Hebrews 13:21's "working" is poieo, not energeo, which does not occur in Hebrews; any parallel with Ephesians 1:11 is conceptual synthesis, not a lexical link. [Explicit text, HIGH; study 10, finding 2.]

### 5.4 Attestation: antecedents, formulation, and confessional boundary

The doctrine's history protects it from two equal and opposite errors: that it is a medieval or Reformation invention without antecedents, and that it was taught in full form from the beginning.

**Antecedents.** Muller traces covenantal packaging before the federal theologians: Luther's 1519 Lectures on Galatians (Christ as immortal God making a pactum, as about-to-be-mortal a testamentum; entry 6, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION); Oecolampadius on Isaiah 54:10 and 55:3 (1525; entry 7, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION); Beza's 1567 rendering of Luke 22:29 (entry 8). Olevianus, De substantia (1585), 1.2, presents an early form: the Son appointed Mediator becomes guarantor for those the Father gave him. He does not use pactum salutis or foedus redemptionis or the two-pact structure, and Heppe and Schrenk's "fully present" claim is an overstatement on Bierma's reading. [Entry 9, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION; pactum note, sec. 1.5, MEDIUM.] Olevianus is cited as antecedent, never as origin. Witsius's own list of prior formulators (II.ii.16: Arminius, Ames, Gomarus, Cloppenburg, Voetius, Essenius, Owen) confirms multiple independent formulation. [Entry 16, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.]

**Full formulation.** Dickson's 1638 General Assembly speech is the first attested use of "covenant of redemption" (Records of the Kirk, vol. I, pp. 158-159, via Muller and the RTS history). Therapeutica Sacra I.iv (Latin 1656; English 1664, self-translated) is the first full treatment as its own locus: a pactum between God the Father and God the Son, designated Mediator, "before the world was, in the council of the Trinity," concerning the elect. [Entry 10, CONSULTED DIRECTLY for ch. 4 in excerpt; the speech CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.] The Sum of Saving Knowledge (1648) gives the compact form: God gave a certain number of lost mankind to the Son before the world began, on condition of his humiliation, obedience, and cursed death, for ransom, righteousness, and eternal life. [Entry 11, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.]

Cocceius, Summa doctrinae (1648), orders the system so that the covenant of grace rests on the testament, and the testament on an eternal pactum between God and the Mediator (iv.87; v.88-99, fuller at v.88-176; Summa theologiae XV, caps. 33-34; locus numbers via Muller, the Latin not read page by page). [Entry 12, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.] He is a formulator and systematizer. He did not invent the doctrine in 1648, and this Part does not claim he coined the label.

Witsius, De oeconomia foederum (1677; 2nd ed. 1685), Book II, ch. 2, secs. 1-16 and ch. 3, gives the classic formulation and the ordering rule: the covenant with the elect presupposes and is founded on the pact with the Mediator. [Entry 13, CONSULTED DIRECTLY for II.ii.1-2 and the ch. 3 outline; II.ii.16 CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.] His historical restraint at II.ii.16, that the pactum was not found among the Reformers and their immediate successors, is quoted rather than smoothed away: a doctrine whose classic expositor declines to claim Reformation-era attestation may not be given it by a later treatise.

Turretin, Institutes XII, Q.2, secs. 12-14, teaches a twofold pact within the covenant of grace: "The prior pact is that which intervenes between the Father and the Son, for executing the work of redemption. The posterior is that which God contracts with the elect in Christ." The wording reaches this Part through Hodge's Latin quotation and McMahon's analysis, not from the English edition opened directly. [Entry 14, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.] He is cited for substance and structure, not for a separate covenant-of-redemption name he does not use.

**Confessional boundary.** Westminster does not name a covenant of redemption: WCF 7.3 speaks of the covenant of grace, WCF 8.1 of Christ appointed Mediator in God's eternal purpose, and WLC Q.31, the closest text, says the covenant of grace was made with Christ as the second Adam, and in him with all the elect as his seed, which states a covenant with Christ, not a formulated intra-Trinitarian pact. [Entries 1-2, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.] Savoy (1658) ch. 8, par. 1 and the Second London Baptist Confession (1689) ch. 8, par. 1 amend Westminster by adding "according to a covenant made between them both": the strongest confessional attestation, and post-Westminster. [Entries 3-4, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.] The Helvetic Consensus Formula (1675) is registered but unverified and is not cited. [Entry 5, NOT YET CONSULTED.] Owen's system and his Hebrews exposition, used at sections 5.1 and 5.3, are likewise registered CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION (entry 15) and are not upgraded here.

### 5.5 Nonclaims printed with the doctrine

So that the pactum cannot be quoted detached from its calibrations, the refusals are printed with the doctrine.

This Part does **not** claim:

1. that Westminster names or confesses a covenant of redemption;
2. that Zechariah 6:13 proves the pactum, rather than supplying the tradition's later label "counsel of peace";
3. that Hebrews 13:20 was a classical proof text for the pactum, or the source from which the tradition derived it;
4. that Cocceius invented the doctrine in 1648, or coined the label pactum salutis;
5. that the Reformers or their immediate successors taught the pactum in its later form;
6. that Turretin used "covenant of redemption" as the name of a separate covenant;
7. that Olevianus originated the doctrine or stated its two-pact structure;
8. that any single text states the pactum in full, or that the pactum is explicit text rather than Theological synthesis;
9. that the Father and Son bargain as two wills, two independent legal centers, or two gods;
10. that the Holy Spirit is absent from the eternal counsel, merely because classical formulations foreground the Father's appointment and the Son's undertaking.

## 6. Counter-positions in strongest form

A doctrine carried at MEDIUM for scriptural explicitness must meet its critics at full strength. Each objection below identifies a real pressure point; the answers therefore combine rebuttal, concession, and self-discipline.

### 6.1 The one-will objection: a covenant needs two legal subjects

**Strongest form.** If God is one, with one mind and one will, a covenant between Father and Son appears to require what classical theism denies: "two distinct subjects and therefore as two legal subjects who can have dealings and enter into obligations with one another" (Barth, as quoted by Swain; register entry 21, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION). The plain form of the objection, as the RTS summary states it, is that agreement would already exist by virtue of the one shared mind and will. If the persons must negotiate, unity is threatened; if they cannot negotiate, "covenant" is an empty metaphor. Pressed further, the language tends toward tritheism on one side or modal theater on the other: either separate centers of will transact, or one undifferentiated subject pretends to transact with himself.

**Answer.** The objection succeeds against a pactum stated as bargaining between two wills. That is why this Part refuses that statement in its thesis, axiom, guarded formulation, and nonclaims. The tradition's own defense is not that God has more than one divine will. It is that the one will is appropriated distinctly to the persons according to their distinct acts. Owen's formula, quoted in section 5.1, is internal to the doctrine's classical form, not a modern repair imposed after Barth. A Brakel asks the same question and answers in the same terms (entry 26, CONSULTED DIRECTLY in excerpt). Cocceius likewise guards the unity of the divine essence at the point of appropriation.

Covenant language is not thereby illegitimate, provided the analogy is controlled. "Covenant" names ordered personal relations, appointment, undertaking, promise, and fidelity; it does not import every feature of contracts between independent parties. The Father's giving and the Son's undertaking are not agreement discovered after disagreement but the eternal counsel stated in the personal order of its execution. Where a formulation loses the one-will guard, Barth's charge lands; where the guard is constitutive, the charge marks the doctrine's boundary rather than its refutation. [Assessment.]

### 6.2 The thin-warrant objection: no text states an intra-Trinitarian covenant

**Strongest form.** Loonstra and Beckwith argue, as Muller reports and disputes them, that no text states an intra-Trinitarian covenant. The traditional lists are collations of texts about appointment, sending, obedience, and reward, none of which says "covenant" in the required sense. Zechariah 6:13, the one text whose traditional label sounds covenantal, concerns the union of kingly and priestly offices in the Branch and does not bear the weight. On this objection, calling the construction scriptural confuses a dogmatic arrangement of texts with an exegetical finding. [Entry 22, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION through Muller, who disputes them; their own texts were not opened.]

**Answer.** The factual premise is largely conceded. No single text states the pactum in full; Part II tags it Theological synthesis, not Explicit text. The disputed inference is whether collation is thereby illegitimate. Muller answers that the doctrine arose from a concerted examination of a series of texts by the era's methods, together with doctrinal questions the decree alone did not answer, including how a new federal head can be given to humanity without removing the legal foundations of the world order. The differing lists used by Dickson, Bulkeley, and Cloppenburg support independent convergence on a construction rather than copying from one proof text.

Part II accepts Muller's defense at the level it can sustain. Collation can warrant a synthesis when its members are accurately graded and the synthetic step is named; it cannot convert the synthesis into a quotation from Scripture. Hence the dual tag is the answer's core: HIGH for attestation and for the appointment, giving, sending, obedience, surety, and reward data; MEDIUM for the covenantal form's explicitness. A critic who requires explicit statement for every doctrine will still decline the pactum; the disagreement is then honestly located at the rule of good and necessary consequence, not hidden by inflated tags. [Entry 18.]

### 6.3 The invention charge: Cocceius created the doctrine in 1648

**Strongest form.** Robert Letham's 1993 charge, anticipated by Wilhelm Gass in the nineteenth century, is that Cocceius invented the pactum salutis in 1648. The charge is strengthened by three observations: the technical vocabulary becomes prominent with Cocceius's generation; Westminster, completed in the same decade, does not name the doctrine; and Witsius admits that the Reformers did not teach it. Letham adds that the doctrine omits the Holy Spirit, introduces subordinationism of the Son, and tends toward tritheism. [Entries 23-24, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.]

**Answer.** The historical form of the charge fails. Dickson used "covenant of redemption" in 1638 and gave the doctrine a full locus in Therapeutica Sacra. The Sum of Saving Knowledge also dates to 1648 and cannot be reduced to Cocceius's invention. Witsius lists multiple prior formulators, and Muller traces antecedents through Olevianus, Beza, Oecolampadius, Perkins, Polanus, and Luther. Cocceius systematized and transmitted the doctrine; he did not create it from nothing in a single year. Nor does this Part claim that he coined the label pactum salutis.

The charge leaves two permanent corrections. The doctrine is early modern in formulation, not a patristic or Reformation formulary, and its history is told that way here. And Letham's Spirit observation is fair against formulations that leave the Spirit unnamed; Part II answers by naming the Spirit explicitly in its own statement, following the direction of Grudem's restatement as registered through Clark. [Entry 27, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.] The subordination and tritheism charges are answered at section 6.1.

### 6.4 The commercial-language charge: bargain, price, condition, reward

**Strongest form.** The pactum's own vocabulary commercializes the relations of the Trinity. Dickson himself calls the arrangement a "bargain." Witsius speaks of sponsor and surety; the Sum speaks of condition, ransom, and purchase; the wider formulary speaks of price and reward. The charge therefore does not depend on a hostile caricature. It attaches to the tradition's words. If those words are pressed univocally, the Father becomes a creditor, the Son an independent contractor, salvation a purchase negotiated between separate interests, and grace the settlement of an account.

**Answer.** The lexical facts are conceded and the univocal pressing is declined. Legal and commercial terms in theology are analogical: they identify real relations of obligation, satisfaction, suretyship, and promised consequence without transferring the whole social world of a market into God. Scripture itself supplies much of the vocabulary field: surety (engyos), ransom, redemption, reward, inheritance, and the Son's asking and receiving. The federal theologians did not invent a commercial register from nothing; they gathered biblical legal and economic metaphors into a covenantal form.

But analogy is not immunity. Part II disciplines its own vocabulary: appointment, giving, undertaking, suretyship, promise, reward, and headship are its ordinary terms; "bargain," "price," and "condition" are reported from sources and glossed, "condition" naming the ordered relation between obedience and promised consequence within the one decree, not uncertainty or external contingency. The Father does not profit from the Son, and the Son does not purchase favor he lacked. The charge is strongest against careless restatement; the doctrine survives it only with its analogical controls printed, as here.

### 6.5 Conditional election: foreseen faith as the ground

**Strongest form.** The conditional-foreknowledge reading protects human responsibility and the passage's silence about reprobation. Before the foundation of the world, God foreknew who would freely believe; he chose those believers in Christ and predestined them, not to faith, but to the blessings faith receives: holiness, adoption, and inheritance. Prothesis is the plan of salvation and the manner of its administration, and "works all things" describes sovereign administration of the plan rather than determination of who believes. On this reading, Ephesians 1 grounds salvation in God's purpose without making faith itself an unconditional gift in the eternal ground.

**Adjudication.** Study 06 declines the reading as an account of this sentence's grounds, at HIGH confidence. No condition is expressed across a passage dense with grounding phrases: kathos, kata ten eudokian twice, kata prothesin, and kata ten boulen tou thelematos. Every ground assigned is on God's side. The infinitive of 1:4 reverses the proposed order: God chose us to be holy (einai hagious); holiness is the choosing's purpose, not its observed prerequisite. The middle voice of exelexato and the dating pro kataboles kosmou locate the determination in God's self-directed purpose, and foreknowledge terminology is absent from the passage. Romans 8:29 cannot be imported to supply it without respecting that passage's own personal-object grammar.

### 6.6 Corporate-only election: the group without chosen individuals

**Strongest form.** Abasciano defines corporate election as "the choice of a group, which entails the choice of its individual members by virtue of their membership in the group. Thus, individuals are not elected as individuals directly, but secondarily as members of the elect group." Klein's thesis is sharper: "we can indeed speak of God's choice of the church in Christ without also implying that God specifically chose the individuals who will comprise that church"; "Ephesians 1:4 affirms precisely the church's election in Christ without any hint of God's choice of individuals to populate that body." Snodgrass's commentary form says that "individuals are not elected and then put in Christ. They are in Christ and therefore elect." The reading's exegetical pillars are the en Christo saturation of the sentence, the uniformly plural pronouns, the Old Testament pattern of a people chosen in its head, and the incorporation of the "you" at hearing and believing in 1:13. [Entries 28-30, all CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION in the forms the register specifies.]

**Adjudication.** Study 06 accepts the register and declines the strong negative claim. The corporate form and Christ-location are the text's own. But exelexato is middle voice with a personal object; the predestining aorists are dated before the foundation of the world with persons as objects and blessings as goals; and the we/you movement of 1:12-13 describes the blessing's arrival in experience, not the constitution of the elect set. An eternal act cannot have a wholly indeterminate group as its object; if determinacy of membership is supplied from future faith, the reading has relocated itself into the conditional account and inherits section 6.5's difficulties. The verdict is corporate in form, individual by necessary entailment. [Assessment in study 06, sec. 5.1: HIGH on the middle-voice and we/you points; MEDIUM-HIGH on the eternal-object inference.]

### 6.7 Two textual counterpressures on the decree premise

The **scope-restricted reading of Romans 8** holds that panta concerns the sufferings in view for the groaning community, not a universal providence thesis. Study 05 partially accepts its pastoral restriction: RP is addressed to the called and defines its good by 8:29-30. It declines the further inference that the verse contributes nothing wider once Ephesians 1:11 is allowed its own domain claim; the reach comes from the two texts' division of labor, not from inflating Romans 8 alone.

The **association reading of Romans 8:28**, represented in the register by N. T. Wright's rendering, takes synergeo with full cooperative force and the dative as association: God works together with those who love him. Study 05 partially accepts the texture (God's working is not aloof from his people's agency) and declines the synergy thesis, because the second dative redescribes the same people as called according to purpose and the chain of 8:29-30 contains no human co-agent. The specific Wright work was not identified in the study. [Entry 37, CONSULTED VIA MEDIATION.]

## 7. Contribution statement

Part II's contribution is checked against all 40 entries in Appendix C, Part II, including the seven entries whose status is NOT YET CONSULTED: the Helvetic Consensus Formula (entry 5), Edwards on the decree (17), Barth's Church Dogmatics II/2 (31), Lincoln (33), O'Brien (34), Murray (38), and Vos (39). No claim below is stated as field-wide priority over sources the register has not opened.

**What Part II adds beyond its sources.** (1) It gives the decree a register-checked division of labor between its two Pauline anchor texts: Ephesians 1:11 supplies agency, grounds, dating, and domain; Romans 8:28-30 supplies beneficiaries, telos, and chain; each text is used to secure the other at the exact point of its own weakness, and each is barred from the other's overclaim. The variant-independence of RP is therefore not an apologetic aside but part of the Part's architecture. (2) It states the pactum inside a three-level separation (triune life, decree, covenantal form) and a dual calibration (HIGH attestation, MEDIUM explicitness), with the one-will and Spirit-naming guards built into the formulation rather than appended as replies. (3) It incorporates Hebrews 13:20 at its disciplined value, as the resurrection member of a mediating chain, while expressly refusing the historically false claim that it was a classical proof text and the lexically false claim that its aionios here means beginningless. (4) It preserves election's corporate form and personal entailment together, refusing both the bare-individual presentation the sentence does not use and the corporate-only negative claim its grammar does not sustain.

**What Part II does not claim as novel.** The pactum itself is the tradition's doctrine, not this treatise's invention. Unconditional election is the Reformed reading the register documents, not a new discovery. The collation defense is Muller's; the classic pactum formulation is Witsius's; the formal distinction is Dickson's; the one-will guard is Owen's and the tradition's. The contribution is synthesis, calibration, and integration under one set of stated disciplines, together with the refusal of several inherited overclaims that the register shows the sources themselves do not support.

**Register-imposed limits.** Part II may not present its kleroo verdict as apparatus-complete until BDAG is consulted directly and the deferred Ephesians commentaries are opened (entries 32-34, 40). It may not quote Turretin XII.ii from the English edition, Cocceius Summa v.88-99 from the Latin or Van Asselt, or Olevianus De substantia 1.2 from the Latin until those checks are done (entries 9, 12, 14). It may not use Edwards, Murray, or Vos to confirm the formulary until they are consulted (entries 17, 38, 39). It may not settle Barth's full doctrine of election until Church Dogmatics II/2 is opened (entry 31). Those limits do not leave the Part's theses unsupported; they mark the points at which later verification could refine wording, add interlocutors, or require a contribution statement to be narrowed.

Part II therefore contributes a disciplined architecture rather than a new dogma: one good counsel, stated from its texts at their exact strengths, covenantally formed toward the Mediator at an honestly labeled level of synthesis, and bounded by the distinction between the things revealed and the things that belong to the LORD.
