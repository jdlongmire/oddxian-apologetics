# Research Note: Historical Attestation of the Pactum Salutis

Purpose: WP research gate 3 (historical source verification for treatise Part II) and corpus-alignment scrutiny item 5 (distinguish eternal triune life from the constructed doctrine of a covenant of redemption). Status: research note, not a treatise draft. No commit or push was made for this note.

## 0. Summary verdict

The eternal pact between the Father and the Son, grounding the covenant of grace, is genuinely attested in the Reformed tradition from the 1630s onward, in full doctrinal form by the 1640s-1670s [HIGH]. The doctrine is best described as BOTH of the things the scrutiny item asks to distinguish: it rests on scriptural data about the Father's appointment of the Son and the Son's consent (appointment, giving, sending, obedience unto death, promised reward), and it packages that data in a covenantal form (parties, conditions, promises) that no single text states in full. The packaging is a theological construction by collation, in the tradition's own terms a formal distinction from the decree, not a second decree and not a textually explicit covenant [HIGH]. Any treatise claim stronger than that (for example, that the pactum is a confessed doctrine of the Westminster Standards, or that Zechariah 6:13 proves it) would overstate the evidence.

## 1. Attestation by author

### 1.1 David Dickson: first use of the term, first full treatment

Term: "covenant of redemption" (English); in the Latin works, foedus redemptionis and pactum. [HIGH]

- First attested use of the term by name: Dickson's speech to the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland, 1638, against Arminian teaching. He presented it as a standard point of theology, not as an innovation, and gave no proof texts in the speech itself. Records of the Kirk of Scotland, vol. I, pp. 158-159, as cited by Muller and by the RTS history. [HIGH, via Muller and RTS, secondary citation of the Records]
- Full treatment as its own locus: Therapeutica Sacra, Book I, chapter 4, "Of divine Covenants about the eternal salvation of men; and in special, of the Covenant of redemption, showing that there is such a Covenant, and what are the articles thereof." Correction to the common shorthand: the Latin original is London, 1656; the English edition (Edinburgh, 1664) was translated by Dickson himself. So 1656 is the Latin publication date, not the first English use; the term itself predates both (1638 speech), and the 1664 English text is the first English book-length treatment. [HIGH]
- Definition (Therapeutica Sacra I.iv.1, 1664 ed. p. 23, as quoted by Muller): a pactum or foedus "between the God the Father and God the Son, the designated Mediator," adding in the English editions "before the world was, in the council of the Trinity," made "concerning the elect." Dickson calls it a "bargain" in the same locus and adds that the covenant of redemption is "in effect one with the decree of redemption," yet distinct in form: the decree fixes salvation and its means; the covenant states that willing in covenantal form. [HIGH, via Muller quotation and the Monergism text of chapter 4]
- Structure (I.iv.1-53): three divine covenants about eternal salvation: redemption (God and Christ the appointed Mediator, before the world, in the council of the Trinity); works (God and men in Adam); grace and reconciliation (God and believers with their children, in Christ). Articles of the covenant of redemption include the price to be paid by Christ in the fullness of time and the Father's promises to the Son. [MEDIUM-HIGH: chapter 4 text read in excerpt, not in full]
- The Sum of Saving Knowledge (1648, probably Dickson with James Durham) gives the classic compact definition: God, having chosen a certain number of lost mankind, gave them before the world began to God the Son, appointed Redeemer, on condition that he humble himself, assume human nature, submit to the law as surety, and satisfy justice by obedience unto the cursed death of the cross, that he should ransom and redeem them and purchase righteousness and eternal life with all saving graces. [HIGH, via RTS quotation]

### 1.2 Johannes Cocceius: the eternal pactum in the Summa (1648)

Work: Summa doctrinae de foedere et testamento Dei (1648), reprinted in Opera omnia, vol. VII, pp. 39-130. [HIGH]

- Locus: the pactum is treated at Summa de foedere iv.87 and v.88-99, with the fuller treatment at v.88-176; Cocceius returns to it in his Summa theologiae, XV, caps. 33-34, under the consilium gratiae et irae and the eternal testamentum Dei. In both places the doctrine precedes and grounds the treatment of the covenant of grace. [MEDIUM-HIGH: locus numbers from Muller's footnotes, not from a page-by-page reading of the Latin]
- Content: the covenant of grace rests on a testament or inheritance, and the solidity of the testament rests on an eternal pactum, not between God and fallen humanity but between God and the mediator. The pactum consists in the will of the Father to give the Son as the redemption of humanity and the will of the Son to procure this salvation. Cocceius is careful to identify the unity of the divine essence at this point: the Son's mediatorial office is appropriated to the Son without dividing the one God. [HIGH, via Muller's exposition]
- Terminology note: Cocceius formulates the eternal pactum; the technical label pactum salutis became standard with his generation and after. The treatise should not claim Cocceius coined the label. [MEDIUM: Muller's article is titled around the association, and Gass's claim that Cocceius invented the concept is explicitly called an overstatement by Muller]
- Polemical context: Cocceius argues the point against Remonstrant denials of the necessity of Christ's obedience as sponsor and against Bellarmine on Christ as mediator according to both natures; the mediator-according-to-both-natures thesis is what generates the pactum in his argument, since the Son as God assumes the form of a servant. [MEDIUM, via Muller]

### 1.3 Herman Witsius: the classic formulation (1677)

Work: De oeconomia foederum Dei cum hominibus, libri quatuor (Leeuwarden, 1677; 2nd ed. 1685); English, The Economy of the Covenants between God and Man (London, 1763; revised 1775; facsimile reprint of the 1822 London edition by Reformation Heritage Books, 2010). [HIGH]

- Locus: Book II, chapter 2, sections 1-16, and Book III (chapter 3 in the English chaptering of the excerpt sites) on the nature of the covenant more fully explained. The opening distinction is at II.ii.1; the definition of the compact at II.ii.2; the proof texts at II.ii.6-9; Witsius's own historical survey of prior formulators at II.ii.16. Chapter numbering verified as far as accessible sources allow: the 1712 edition citation in Hodge's footnote reads "lib. II. ii. 1, p. 130," and the Beeke introduction to the reprint cites "Economy of the Covenants, 2.2.1" for the same passage, so Book II, chapter 2, section 1 is secure; the English chapter title for chapter 3 ("The Nature of the Covenant Between the Father and the Son More Fully Explained") is taken from the A Puritan's Mind reproduction. [HIGH for II.ii.1-2; MEDIUM for the English chapter-3 title]
- Content (II.ii.1-2, in the Monergism translation, read directly): "two things are above all to be distinctly considered. First, the covenant which intervenes between God the Father and Christ the Mediator. Secondly, that testamentary disposition by which God bestows, by an immutable covenant, eternal salvation upon the elect. The former agreement is between God and the Mediator: the latter, between God and the Elect. This last pre-supposes the first, and is founded upon it." The compact itself is "the will of the Father, giving the Son to be the head and Redeemer of the elect; and the will of the Son, presenting himself, as a sponsor or surety for them." Scripture represents the Father as demanding the Son's obedience even unto death and, on condition of that obedience, promising him the name above every name and headship of the elect in glory; the Son presents himself to do the Father's will and, by virtue of the compact, requires the kingdom and glory promised. [HIGH: text read in the Monergism reproduction]
- Chapter 3 expansion: three periods of the covenant (eternal counsel of the Trinity, with the approbation of the Holy Spirit; Christ's intercession immediately upon the fall, offering himself to the offended God; completion in time); the law of the covenant binding the Son; whether the Son could have withdrawn (denied: the decree, promises, and oath would otherwise be void); the reward promised to the Son. [MEDIUM-HIGH: chapter 3 read in excerpt]
- Witsius's own historical honesty (II.ii.16, via Muller): Witsius notes that the pactum salutis was not found among the Reformers and their immediate successors, and lists Arminius (1603 oration), Ames, Gomarus, Cloppenburg, Voetius, Essenius, and Owen as prior formulators, omitting Cocceius (Muller suggests reserved polemic). The treatise should quote this: the tradition's own classic expositor did not claim Reformation-era attestation. [HIGH, via Muller]

### 1.4 Francis Turretin: the pact within one covenant of grace

Work: Institutes of Elenctic Theology, Twelfth Topic (covenant of grace). [HIGH]

- Locus and correction: the relevant discussion is Topic XII, Question 2, sections 12-14. Hodge's footnote cites "XII. ii. 12; edit. Edinburgh, 1847, vol. ii. pp. 157, 158"; Muller notes Turretin, Inst. XII.ii.14, also cites Luke 22:29. [MEDIUM-HIGH: numbering from two independent secondary citations, not from the English edition itself]
- Substance, in Turretin's own words as quoted by Hodge: it is superfluous to dispute whether the covenant was made with Christ as a contracting party and in him with all his seed (as the first covenant was made with Adam and in him with his posterity), or with Christ as mediator standing between dissenting parties; "it is certain that a twofold pact must necessarily be attended to here, or two parts and degrees of one and the same pact. The prior pact is that which intervenes between the Father and the Son, for executing the work of redemption. The posterior is that which God contracts with the elect in Christ, for their salvation through and on account of Christ, under the condition of faith and repentance. The prior is made with the Sponsor and head for the salvation of the members; the posterior with the members in the head and sponsor." [HIGH for the substance, via Hodge's Latin quotation]
- Terminology correction: Turretin does NOT use "covenant of redemption" as the name of a separate covenant. Like the Westminster Confession, he works with a single covenant of grace and compartmentalizes it into the twofold pact above. The treatise must therefore cite Turretin for the pact's substance and structure, not for the separate name, and must not list him alongside Witsius as a "covenant of redemption" theologian without that qualification. [MEDIUM-HIGH: McMahon's analysis, secondary]
- Definition (as quoted by Scott Swain and reproduced in First Things): the pact between the Father and the Son contains the will of the Father giving the Son as Redeemer and head of his mystical body and the will of the Son offering himself as sponsor for his members to work out redemption; the Father stipulates obedience even unto death and promises a name above every name; the Son promises faithful performance and restipulates the kingdom and glory promised. This matches Witsius II.ii.2 almost verbatim, which is itself evidence of a shared formulary. [MEDIUM: secondary quotation of Swain quoting Turretin]

### 1.5 Caspar Olevianus: early form, not the full doctrine

Work: De substantia foederis gratuiti inter Deum et electos (Geneva, 1585), two books. [HIGH for the work's existence and date]

- What is attested: Olevianus, De substantia 1.2, treats the pre-temporal covenant as a ground of Christian confidence, with the eternal generation of the Son as an element of the substance of the covenant of grace; 1.2.1 (as cited by Clark): the Son, appointed by God as Mediator of the covenant, becomes guarantor on two counts, to satisfy for the sins of all whom the Father has given him, and to bring it about that, planted in him, they enjoy freedom of conscience and daily renewal in the image of God. Muller cites De substantia pp. 23, 63, 106 for the same material, following Bierma's analysis (German Calvinism in the Confessional Age, pp. 107-112). [MEDIUM: via Muller and Clark, not from the Latin text itself]
- What is NOT attested: Olevianus does not use the technical terms pactum salutis or foedus redemptionis, and does not give the doctrine its later two-pact structure. Heppe and Schrenk's claim that the doctrine is "fully present" in Olevianus is an overstatement on Bierma's more precise reading; Muller presents Olevianus as antecedent and groundwork, not as a formulator. The treatise may cite Olevianus as an early form and antecedent, at MEDIUM confidence, and must not cite him as the doctrine's origin. [MEDIUM-HIGH, via Muller and Bierma as reported]
- Note on availability: at the time of Clark's writing, De substantia had not yet appeared in English in the Classic Reformed Theology series; this note likewise did not consult an English translation, so all Olevianus claims here are secondhand. [HIGH, as a statement about this note's own sources]

### 1.6 Confessional status (needed for Part II's claims)

- Westminster Confession of Faith: does NOT name a covenant of redemption. WCF 7.3 and 8.1 speak of the covenant of grace and of Christ's appointment and ordination as Mediator "in his eternal purpose," which the tradition read as implicitly presupposing the pact. [HIGH]
- Westminster Larger Catechism Q. 31: the covenant of grace was made with Christ as the second Adam, and in him with all the elect as his seed. This is the closest Westminster text to the pact, and it states the covenant with Christ, not a separate intra-Trinitarian pact. [HIGH, standard text]
- Savoy Declaration (1658) and Second London Baptist Confession (1689), chapter 8, paragraph 1: both amend the Westminster wording by adding "according to a covenant made between them both," making explicit what Westminster left implicit. This is the strongest confessional attestation available, and it is post-Westminster. [MEDIUM-HIGH: via RTS quotation of the amendment]
- Helvetic Consensus Formula (1675) is listed by the RTS history among confessional documents showing the doctrine's spread in the later seventeenth century; this note did not verify its wording and the treatise should not cite it without a direct check. [LOW]

## 2. Proof texts the tradition itself used

The tradition's lists are collations, not a single proof text, and the lists differ by author. Muller stresses that Dickson, Bulkeley, and Cloppenburg used largely different texts, which indicates independent formulations from a shared exegetical background rather than a single inherited proof. [HIGH]

Core texts, with their traditional function:

- Luke 22:29: the Father "appoints" (Greek diatithemai, rendered by Beza in 1567 as "made a covenant with") a kingdom to the Son, as the Son appoints one to his disciples. The covenantal reading depends on Beza's translation against the Vulgate's dispono. Witsius's initial proof trio; Turretin XII.ii.14. [HIGH]
- Psalm 2:7-8: the Father's decree ("I will tell of the decree") and the gift of the nations as inheritance. Gillespie argued the Hebrew hoq (decree) carries covenantal senses and that ancient interpreters rendered it as covenant. Dickson's starting Old Testament text. [MEDIUM-HIGH: traditional function secure; the hoq-equals-covenant lexical claim is Gillespie's, not settled philology]
- Isaiah 53:10-12: "he shall see his seed," the pleasure of the LORD prospering in his hand, the servant's offering and reward. Cited by Cocceius, Witsius, Dickson, and Ames (via Witsius II.ii.16, on the ratification of the covenant entered by Christ). [HIGH]
- Isaiah 49:4-6; 42:1; 50:5-9: the servant's appointment, trust in the Father, and submission. Bulkeley's and Witsius's lists. [MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Psalms of the messianic ruler: 2:8; 16:2; 22:3; 40:7-9 (Hebrews 10:5-10 applies it as the Son's consent to do God's will before incarnation); 45:8; 80:17; 86:2; 119:122. Shared by Cocceius and Witsius. [MEDIUM-HIGH, via Muller]
- John 6:38-40; 17:4-6, 24: sending, giving, work finished, the elect given to the Son, none lost, raised at the last day. The Johannine core of the tradition's case; the Sum of Saving Knowledge cites John 6:37 on its title page. [HIGH]
- Galatians 3:15-17: the covenant confirmed beforehand with reference to Christ, prior to the law; Cloppenburg's and Witsius's argument that the Abrahamic covenant rests on a prior agreement in God concerning Christ. [MEDIUM-HIGH]
- Hebrews 7:22, 28: Christ as surety (engyos) of a better covenant, consecrated forever by the word of the oath. Witsius's initial proof trio; Cocceius's argument that Hebrews 7 requires Christ as mediator according to both natures. [HIGH]
- 1 Peter 1:20; Ephesians 1:4-5; 2 Timothy 1:9; Titus 1:2: foreordination before the foundation of the world, election in Christ, grace given in Christ before time began. The decree-side texts that the pactum formulations presuppose. [HIGH]
- Zechariah 6:13, "counsel of peace" (Vulgate consilium pacis): a LATER and SECONDARY locus. Muller shows it played no role in the initial formulations of Dickson, Bulkeley, or Cloppenburg and was drawn in as a significant locus only by Cocceius and Witsius and their successors. Berkhof judges the proof-text use a mistake: the verse concerns the union of kingly and priestly offices in the Branch, not an intra-Trinitarian agreement. The treatise may use "counsel of peace" as the tradition's own label for the pactum, but must not present Zechariah 6:13 as a proof text. [HIGH for the historical point; MEDIUM-HIGH for Berkhof's exegetical judgment, which is a judgment, not a datum]
- Hebrews 13:20: PLACE IN THE CLASSICAL LISTS: ABSENT from the primary lists examined for this note. Neither Witsius's initial trio (Luke 22:29; Hebrews 7:22ff.; Galatians 3:17), nor Cocceius's and Witsius's Psalm and Isaiah lists, nor Dickson's Therapeutica Sacra proofs, nor Berkhof's scriptural basis (Ephesians 1:4ff.; 3:11; 2 Thessalonians 2:13; 2 Timothy 1:9; 1 Peter 1:2) features Hebrews 13:20 as a founding proof. Its phrase "the blood of the eternal covenant" supplies traditional language for the covenant's eternity, and it is legitimate corroborating language for Part II and unit 10, but the treatise must not present it as the classical proof text, and must not imply the tradition derived the pactum from it. [HIGH as a statement about the lists examined; MEDIUM for the general claim, since no exhaustive search of all seventeenth-century loci was possible]

## 3. The modern critical question, strongest form on both sides

### 3.1 The critique

1. One will needs no agreement. If God is one, with one mind and will, a covenant between the persons seems to require two wills and two legal subjects. Karl Barth's charge, as quoted by Swain: the pactum requires thinking of Father and Son "as two distinct subjects and therefore as two legal subjects who can have dealings and enter into obligations with one another," tending toward tritheism. The RTS summary states the same objection in its plainest form: agreement would already exist by virtue of the shared mind and will. [MEDIUM-HIGH: Barth via Swain, secondary quotation]
2. Exegetical warrant is thin. Loonstra and Beckwith argued the doctrine lacks genuine exegetical support: no text states an intra-Trinitarian covenant, and the lists are collations of texts about appointment, sending, and reward, none of which says "covenant" in that sense. Zechariah 6:13, the one text with covenant-like language in the traditional label, does not bear the weight (Berkhof's judgment above). [MEDIUM-HIGH: Loonstra and Beckwith via Muller, who disputes them]
3. Invention, not recovery. Robert Letham (1993) claimed Cocceius invented the pactum salutis in 1648 and criticized it for omitting the Holy Spirit, introducing subordinationism of the Son, and tending toward tritheism. Gass (1854-1867) made the same invention claim earlier. [MEDIUM: Letham via Clark's summary]
4. Commercial language. The pactum's own vocabulary (bargain, price, condition, reward, sponsor, surety) invites the charge that it commercializes intra-Trinitarian relations, turning the Father-Son relation into a transaction. Dickson himself used "bargain," so the charge attaches to the tradition's own words, not to a caricature. [HIGH that the language is the tradition's own; the evaluative charge itself is a judgment]

### 3.2 The defense

1. The tradition's own self-understanding was not tritheist. Witsius, Cocceius, Owen, and a Brakel all insist on one divine will, appropriated distinctly to the persons: Owen's formula, via Swain, is that the will of God with regard to the Father's peculiar actings is the will of the Father, and with regard to the Son's peculiar actings is the will of the Son, "not by a distinction of sundry wills, but by the distinct application of the same will unto its distinct acts in the persons of the Father and the Son." A Brakel asks the same question the critics ask (how can one will transact with itself?) and answers it the same way: the one will viewed from a twofold perspective. The defense is therefore internal to the doctrine, not imposed on it. [MEDIUM-HIGH: Owen and a Brakel via Swain, secondary quotation]
2. The exegetical trajectory is real, even if no single text states the doctrine. Muller (2007) argues against Loonstra and Beckwith that the doctrine arose from a concerted examination of a series of texts, collated by the era's methods, together with doctrinal questions the decree alone did not answer: how a new federal head can be given to humanity that violated the covenant of works without removing the legal foundations of the world order. The antecedents run from Luther's 1519 Galatians lectures (Christ as immortal God "made a pactum," as about-to-be-mortal "made a testamentum"), Oecolampadius on Isaiah 54:10 and 55:3 (1525), Beza's 1567 translation of Luke 22:29, and the predestinarian designation language of Perkins and Polanus, into the 1638-1648 formulations. [MEDIUM-HIGH: Muller's argument, read in the article]
3. The pactum is formally distinct from the decree, not a rival to it. Given divine simplicity, decree and pactum are essentially identical in willing but formally distinct: the decree wills all things; the pactum states the intratrinitarian willing concerning the work of salvation in covenantal form, and presupposes election rather than replacing it. Dickson: "in effect one with the decree of redemption," distinct in form. This answers the "speculative and unnecessary" charge on the tradition's own terms: the pactum does work the decree does not, namely grounding the covenant of grace in a personal transaction rather than a bare decree. [MEDIUM-HIGH, via Muller]
4. The tradition itself was not uncritical. Witsius admits the doctrine is not found among the Reformers; Berkhof rejects the Zechariah proof text while retaining the doctrine; the Westminster Standards do not name it. A defense that pretends otherwise is weaker than the doctrine's own record. [HIGH]

### 3.3 Assessment for the treatise

The strongest critique (one will, no explicit text) and the strongest defense (one will appropriated to persons; collation as the era's legitimate method; formal distinction from the decree) both survive contact with the sources. The treatise should present the pactum as a theological construction of HIGH traditional attestation and MEDIUM direct scriptural explicitness, and should carry the two guards the tradition itself supplies: (a) one will, appropriated distinctly, never two wills bargaining; (b) the Spirit named explicitly in the pactum's statement, since the classical formulations are Father-Son in form and the omission is Letham's fairest point (Grudem's 1994 restatement made the Spirit's applicatory role explicit, and Part II should do the same).

## 4. Verdict: what Part II may claim

May claim, at HIGH confidence, citing the loci above:
- That the Reformed tradition from 1638 (Dickson) through 1648 (Cocceius, Sum of Saving Knowledge) to 1677 (Witsius) taught an eternal pact between the Father and the Son as the foundation of the covenant of grace, with the Father stipulating the Son's obedience unto death and promising reward and headship, and the Son undertaking suretyship and restipulating the promised kingdom and glory.
- That Turretin (XII.ii.12-14) teaches the same twofold pact within a single covenant of grace, with the qualification that he does not name a separate covenant of redemption.
- That the doctrine's proof structure is a collation (Luke 22:29; Psalm 2; Isaiah 53; John 6 and 17; Galatians 3:17; Hebrews 7; 1 Peter 1:20), not a single text, and that its earliest formulators presented it as the covenantal form of the eternal decree, formally distinct from the decree itself.

May claim, at MEDIUM confidence:
- That Olevianus (1585) is an early form and antecedent, in the suretyship and appointment material of De substantia 1.2, not the doctrine's origin.
- That the Savoy and 1689 confessions give the doctrine its strongest confessional attestation ("according to a covenant made between them both"), while Westminster itself leaves it implicit.

Must NOT claim:
- That Westminster names or confesses a covenant of redemption. It does not.
- That Zechariah 6:13 proves the pactum, or that Hebrews 13:20 was a classical proof text. Neither is true of the tradition's own lists.
- That Cocceius invented the doctrine in 1648, or that it was taught by the Reformers. Both extremes fail against Witsius's own historical survey.
- That the pactum is an explicit text (tag: Theological synthesis, MEDIUM) rather than a construction from texts (tag: Necessary inference at most, and only in the tradition's own good-and-necessary-consequence sense; the treatise should label it Theological synthesis to stay honest with corpus-alignment scrutiny item 5).

Open items for Part II drafting:
- Direct check of Turretin XII.ii in the English edition (this note used Hodge's Latin quotation and McMahon's analysis).
- Direct check of Cocceius Summa v.88-99 in Latin or in Van Asselt's translation and summary.
- Direct check of De substantia 1.2 in Latin (no English translation was consulted for this note).
- A decision, for JD, on whether Part II states the pactum as "covenant of redemption" (Dickson/Witsius usage) or as "pactum salutis" (the later standard label), with the other term glossed at first use. Recommendation: use "pactum salutis (covenant of redemption)" at first use, then "pactum" thereafter, since that matches both the tradition and the treatise's own vocabulary.
