# Research Note: Stress-Test of the Universal Sin-Root Claim

**Gate:** Corpus-alignment scrutiny item 1 (filed 2026-10-31), gating Part IV.
**Claim under test (universal form):** Every sin derives from closed self-interest, the self turned in on itself (incurvatus in se) against triune loving communion. Source of the claim: the internal corpus (triune-loving-communion systematic theology; Covenant and Communion systematic theology), as summarized for this test.
**Question:** Does the universal form survive the four hardest counterexample classes (ignorance, omission, fear, misguided altruism), and in what formulation may Part IV state it?
**Method:** Strongest biblical cases per class, adjudicated [Absorbed / Absorbed with qualification / Not absorbed] with confidence. Scripture is quoted or closely paraphrased from the ESV as consulted (biblia.com and esv.org displays), unless another version is named. Secondary sources are labeled by mediation in section 6 and the source list.
**Status:** Research note. It decides nothing by itself; it recommends a formulation for JD's approval before Part IV is drafted.

## 1. Ignorance

**The cases.**
- Leviticus 4-5 institutes atonement for sins committed unintentionally: the anointed priest (4:3), the whole congregation (4:13, "the thing is hidden from the eyes of the assembly"), a leader (4:22), a common person (4:27). In each case guilt is real before it is known: the formula is "when the sin which they have committed becomes known" (4:14, 23, 28), and then atonement is made and "it shall be forgiven." Leviticus 5:17 is the cleanest statement: "If anyone sins... though he did not know it, then he shall be guilty and shall bear his iniquity." Guilt here attaches to the act and the state of the person, with no self-interested willing anywhere in view.
- Numbers 15:22-29 provides atonement and forgiveness for the unintentional sin of congregation and individual alike, "because it was a mistake" (15:25). Verses 30-31 set the contrast class: the sin "with a high hand" is not atoned by this provision; that person "reviles the LORD... he has despised the word of the LORD." The text itself grades the two by orientation (error versus contempt), not by outcome.
- Luke 23:34: "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do." Ignorance is pleaded as ground for intercession, not as proof of innocence.
- Acts 3:17: "I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers." Peter still calls the same audience to repent (3:19). Ignorance and culpability coexist in one breath.
- 1 Timothy 1:13, 15: Paul "received mercy because I had acted ignorantly in unbelief," and in the same passage calls himself "the foremost of sinners." Ignorance mitigates (it is the stated reason mercy was extended) without removing the sinner status.

**Adjudication.** No proximate self-interested motive can be found in the Leviticus cases, and inventing one would contradict the texts, whose whole point is that the guilt does not wait for a motive. What the incurvatus thesis can truthfully claim here is one level down: these sins inhere in a condition and an order prior to willing. That is not an evasion; it is the treatise's own doctrine of inherited condition doing its proper work. A humanity curved inward is characteristically inattentive to the law it has not studied and the neighbor it has not noticed (Leviticus 5:1, the witness who does not speak, sits exactly on this seam between ignorance and omission), and the fallen condition explains why error about God's commands is the default rather than the exception. But the universal claim, if read as a claim about the motive of each sin, fails on this class. If read as a claim about sin's root condition and orientation, it stands, and Numbers 15 itself supplies the supporting distinction: Scripture locates the aggravated root in despising the word, that is, in orientation toward God, not in the surface psychology of the act.

**Verdict: [Absorbed with qualification] [HIGH]**
Qualification Q1 (condition, not motive): the claim must be stated at the level of root condition and orientation. Guilt is not motive-indexed; ignorant sins are rooted in the closed condition (and its inherited disorder) even where no self-interested willing is present or discoverable.

## 2. Omission

**The cases.**
- James 4:17: "So whoever knows the right thing to do and fails to do it, for him it is sin." Note that James builds knowledge into the definition; this case is a knowing refusal or neglect, which is the easier end of the class.
- Matthew 25:41-46: the goats are condemned entirely for things not done (no feeding, no welcome, no clothing, no visit). Their defense is a profession of ignorance: "Lord, when did we see you hungry or thirsty..." (25:44). The King's reply (25:45) treats the failure to serve "the least of these" as failure toward himself. The text gives no evidence of advantage-seeking; it gives evidence of a life in which the suffering neighbor simply never registered.
- Luke 10:30-32: the priest and the Levite each "saw him and passed by on the other side." Passing by is itself an act of avoidance, done with the victim in view. Possible motives (fear of robbers on a dangerous road, ritual purity concerns for a possibly dead body, cost and delay) are not stated in the text and must not be asserted as fact; what is stated is deliberate non-approach.

**Adjudication.** This is the class where the word "closed" earns its place and the word "interest" is at risk of stretching. If "self-interest" means active pursuit of one's own advantage, most omissions are not explained: the goats gained nothing; the sin is that love never went out. If, however, the thesis's root term is the *closed* self, the self that does not open toward God and neighbor, omission is not a counterexample but the purest specimen: closure with no grasping at all, the self remaining undisturbed inside its own round. There is also a defensible negative sense of interest: the omission preserves the agent's existing state (comfort, schedule, safety, purity) against the interruption the neighbor represents, and the priest and Levite's visible avoidance fits that reading. But the term must be defined to carry this, or the claim proves itself by equivocation. Barth's hamartiology is directly useful here (section 6): his second form of sin is sloth, the inert refusal of one's God-given freedom, and Jenson's reading holds pride and sloth together as distinct expressions of the one incurvature. The tradition itself distinguishes the inert mode rather than pretending every sin grasps.

**Verdict: [Absorbed with qualification] [MEDIUM]**
Qualification Q2 (closure is the load-bearing term; modes must be named): Part IV must define "closed" as primary (the self not opened by love toward God and neighbor) and name at least the inert mode (sloth, inattention, non-approach) alongside the grasping mode, with "interest" expressly covering the negative preference for remaining undisturbed. Absent those definitions, the absorption of this class is verbal, not real; hence MEDIUM, not HIGH.

## 3. Fear

**The cases.**
- Matthew 26:69-75: Peter, who had protested undying loyalty (26:33-35), denies Jesus three times before servants and bystanders. The motive on the surface of the text is self-preservation under perceived threat; there is no advantage sought beyond safety.
- Proverbs 29:25: "The fear of man lays a snare, but whoever trusts in the LORD is safe." Scripture itself names fear of man as a trap mechanism, contrasted with trust, which locates fear-sin in the trust/love order rather than in appetite.
- John 9:22: the parents of the man born blind deflect the interrogation ("He is of age; ask him") "for fear of the Jews," because expulsion from the synagogue had been agreed for confessors. Social self-preservation produces evasive speech that falls short of bearing witness.
- Matthew 25:24-25: the one-talent servant states his motive himself: "I was afraid, and I went and hid your talent in the ground." The master's verdict joins fear to sloth and wickedness: "You wicked and slothful servant" (25:26). Note that the fear rests on a false picture of the master ("a hard man"), which ties this case to the falsehood mode as well.

**Adjudication.** Self-preservation is not identical to closed self-interest, and the treatise must not say it is. Preservation of one's own life is a created good: flight from danger is commended or at least permitted in Scripture (Matthew 10:23, "flee to the next"; Paul's escape from Damascus, Acts 9:25). Fear becomes sin at the point where the good of safety is absolutized, ranked above faithfulness and truth, so that the self's survival and standing govern conduct in place of God. That is precisely a disorder of loves, and it is incurvature in a defensive posture: the self contracted around its own preservation, closed against the claim of God and the witness owed to the neighbor. The qualification the thesis needs is therefore structural, not cosmetic: self-interest has a defensive mode, distinguishable from its acquisitive mode, and fear is that mode's characteristic passion. Proverbs 29:25 supports the framing (snare versus trust), and Revelation 12:11 supplies the positive counter-image ("they loved not their lives even unto death"), showing that what fear-sins surrender is exactly the ordered subordination of one's life to God.

**Verdict: [Absorbed with qualification] [HIGH]**
Qualification Q3 (defensive mode; preservation itself is good): Part IV must state that creaturely self-preservation is good and becomes sin only when absolutized, and must carry fear as the named defensive mode of the closed self rather than assuming fear reduces to advantage-seeking.

## 4. Misguided altruism

**The cases.**
- Uzzah (2 Samuel 6:6-7, ESV): when the oxen stumbled at the threshing floor of Nacon, "Uzzah put out his hand to the ark of God and took hold of it," and God struck him down "because of his error." The text does not state Uzzah's motive; care for the ark is a reasonable inference from the act, not a datum. What is textual is the surrounding order: the ark was being carried on a new cart, Philistine fashion (compare 1 Samuel 6:7-8), against the prescribed carrying by poles on Levites' shoulders (Exodus 25:13-15; Numbers 4:15, "they must not touch the holy things, lest they die"; 7:9). Modern commentary consulted (Crossway, "Tough Passages" on 2 Samuel 6) agrees the action was "not motivated by irreverence" yet "still sacrilegious, violating the holiness of God," and observes that Uzzah would not have been in that position had the transport been ordered rightly in the first place.
- Saul (1 Samuel 15): the sparing of Agag and "the best of the sheep and of the oxen... all that was good" (15:9) is defended by Saul as altruism toward God's honor: "the people spared the best of the sheep and of the oxen, to sacrifice to the LORD your God" (15:15, 21). The narrative does not let the rationale stand at face value. Samuel names the act "pounce on the spoil" (15:19) and pronounces the rule "to obey is better than sacrifice" (15:22); Saul blames the people (15:15, 21, 24) while claiming "I have obeyed" (15:20); and 15:12 records, almost in passing, that Saul "set up a monument for himself" at Carmel. Self-interest is not merely inferable here; the text arranges the evidence of it around the pious explanation.
- Peter (Matthew 16:22-23): "Far be it from you, Lord! This shall never happen to you" is spoken out of evident love for Jesus, and Jesus' diagnosis is precise: "you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man." The sin is located not in appetite but in arbitration: Peter's own conception of the good, sincerely benevolent, set against the declared will of God.
- Martha (Luke 10:40-42), borderline as assigned: "distracted with much serving," she asks Jesus to conscript Mary into Martha's own pattern of service. Jesus' correction is gentle and diagnostic ("anxious and troubled about many things... one thing is necessary"). If this is sin and not merely a corrected imbalance, its root is service curved back into self-reference: recognition, fairness to oneself, one's own standard of devotion imposed on another.

**Adjudication.** This class is the strongest of the four, and it splits. Saul and Peter are absorbed, and the mechanism can be named from the texts: the self as arbiter of the good. In both cases the agent substitutes his own judgment of what God should want for God's stated word, and in Saul's case the text independently exposes self-interested motives beneath the altruistic account (spoil, blame-shifting, the monument). This mechanism is not a stretch of the thesis; it is Luther's central case, the religious person who "uses God himself" for his own ends and does not know he does it (section 6). Martha is absorbed on the same terms, weakly, being borderline as sin at all. Uzzah is the residue. At the level of the ordered act, the thesis explains the judgment: a procession designed by human judgment in place of the revealed order is presumption at the corporate level, David's before it is Uzzah's. At the level of Uzzah's individual act, the text discloses no motive, the act may have been near-reflexive, and a thesis that requires a self-interested motive in Uzzah himself asserts what Scripture does not. The honest treatment is to concede that limit and absorb the case at the level of act and order (unauthorized handling, judged as error) rather than at the level of proven motive.

**Verdict: [Absorbed with qualification] [MEDIUM]**
Qualification Q4 (arbitral mode, and a motive-opacity limit): Part IV must name the arbitral mode (the self as arbiter of the good: presumption, religious self-direction) as a distinct mode of the closed self, and must adopt the rule that where Scripture judges an act without disclosing a motive, the treatise claims the root at the level of the disordered act and its ordering, and asserts no motive.

## 5. Cross-class synthesis

The four classes converge on one diagnosis. Every difficulty arose from the same ambiguity in the claim as stated: "root" could mean (i) the proximate motive of each sinful act, or (ii) the fallen orientation and condition from which sins of every mode proceed. Read as (i), the claim fails on ignorance (no willing at all), is strained on omission (nothing grasped), and overreaches on Uzzah (no motive disclosed). Read as (ii), all four classes are not merely absorbed but positively mapped: the closed self grasps (pride, spoil), contracts (fear), rests inert (omission, sloth), and directs itself religiously (presumption), while its inherited disorder generates guilt-bearing error even where the will is not engaged in the particular act. The tradition's own differentiation of sin's forms under one incurvature (Augustine's two loves; Luther on the religious man; Barth's pride, sloth, falsehood) supports (ii) and supplies the modal vocabulary, so the repair is traditional, not improvised.

## 6. What the tradition contributes (from sources consulted)

- **Augustine, City of God XIV.28** (text consulted in the Dods translation, thenagain.info and writewellgroup.com displays): "two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self." Contribution: Augustine states the root as a *love*, an orientation of the whole person and society, not as a per-act motive inventory. That framing is exactly what qualification Q1 requires, and it protects the thesis from the ignorance class: amor sui is a settled direction of the heart that precedes and produces particular acts, including acts the agent does not recognize as sins. The same book (XIV.13, as quoted in a consulted secondary display) gives the root's first form: "by craving to be more, man becomes less; and by aspiring to be self-sufficing, he fell away from Him who truly suffices him," which grounds the arbitral mode of section 4. A consulted Cambridge excerpt (Ogle, Politics and the Earthly City in Augustine's City of God) notes, as mediation, that amor sui grammatically collapses subject and object into the self, whereas amor Dei is relational in its very grammar; that observation fits the treatise's communion frame and may be cited only as that secondary reading, not as Augustine's text.
- **Luther, Lectures on Romans** (scholion on Romans 5 consulted in the uecf.net full-text display; also the Mockingbird glossary entry citing Luther's Works vol. 25, p. 345, mediation labeled): "Our nature, by the corruption of the first sin, [is] so deeply curved in on itself that it not only bends the best gifts of God towards itself and enjoys them... or rather even uses God himself in order to attain these gifts, but it also fails to realize that it so wickedly, curvedly, and viciously seeks all things, even God, for its own sake," with Jeremiah 17:9 and Psalm 19:12 ("hidden faults") as his proof texts. Contribution: threefold. (1) Incurvature is predicated of *nature by the corruption of the first sin*, that is, of the inherited condition, which is the level Q1 assigns to the ignorance cases. (2) Its paradigm theater is religion itself, which absorbs the altruism class's mechanism (the self directing its own service of God). (3) It is characteristically unconscious ("fails to realize"), which is Luther's own bridge to sins their agent does not know he commits.
- **Karl Barth, Church Dogmatics IV/1-3** (consulted through Matt Jenson, The Gravity of Sin, as reported in the Gospel Coalition's Themelios review and the Princeton Center for Barth Studies summary; secondary mediation throughout, no direct CD text consulted for this note): Barth treats sin in three forms, pride (IV/1), sloth (IV/2), and falsehood (IV/3), and Jenson's summary (p. 131, as quoted in the review) is that each is developed "first and foremost by broken relationships in which people live for themselves rather than for God and others." Contribution: Barth demonstrates that the incurvatus tradition itself refuses to flatten sin into pride or grasping alone. Sloth gives dogmatic standing to the inert mode required by the omission class; falsehood names the self-deceiving rationalization on display in Saul; pride covers the grasping and arbitral modes. The modal repair proposed in section 7 is therefore not an ad hoc rescue of the treatise's claim but the tradition's own internal differentiation, gathered under the treatise's single root image.
- **Bonhoeffer:** not consulted from primary text for this note and not relied on. If Part IV wants a Bonhoeffer voice (for example on the self's self-justification), it must be added from the text in a later pass, not from reputation.

## 7. Conclusion and recommended formulation

**Recommendation: (b). The claim stands as a claim about sin's root orientation, with named qualifications.** Option (a) fails on Leviticus 4-5 and Numbers 15, which establish guilt without willing, and on the undisclosed motive of Uzzah. Option (c), narrowing the claim to some sins only, is unnecessary: once "root" is fixed at the level of condition and orientation, and the modes are named, every case in all four classes is located without residue except the single stated limit on motive attribution (Q4), which is a rule of method, not an exception class.

**Qualifications to be carried into Part IV verbatim in substance:**
- Q1 (condition, not motive): the root is the fallen condition and orientation of the self, inherited before it is chosen; guilt is not motive-indexed (Leviticus 5:17; Numbers 15).
- Q2 (closure primary, modes named): "closed" is the load-bearing term; the modes are at least grasping, defensive, inert, and arbitral; "interest" expressly includes the negative preference for remaining undisturbed.
- Q3 (defensive mode): self-preservation is a created good and is sin only when absolutized over faithfulness; fear is the defensive mode of the closed self (Proverbs 29:25).
- Q4 (arbitral mode; motive-opacity limit): presumption, the self as arbiter of the good, is a distinct mode (1 Samuel 15; Matthew 16:23); where Scripture does not disclose a motive, none may be asserted (2 Samuel 6).

**Draft formulation proposed for Part IV:**
"Sin's root is the self closed in on itself. Made for triune loving communion, the fallen self takes itself, rather than God and neighbor, as its governing center; this closure is a condition inherited before it is chosen, and it is the orientation from which particular sins proceed. It proceeds in distinguishable modes: grasping, as the self asserts and acquires for itself; defensive, as self-preservation is absolutized and fear governs; inert, as the self rests undisturbed and love does not go out to the neighbor; and arbitral, as the self sets its own judgment of the good in place of God's word, most subtly in religion itself. Particular sins need not each carry a conscious self-interested motive: Scripture holds men guilty of sins they did not know they were committing, because guilt rests on the disordered condition and the act against God's order, not on the agent's awareness of his own curvature, which he characteristically does not perceive. The claim is therefore universal at the level of root and orientation, and deliberately non-committal at the level of individual motive, where Scripture itself is often silent."

**Gate disposition:** with formulation (b) adopted, scrutiny item 1 is satisfied and Part IV may be drafted as established only in the qualified form. Drafting in the unqualified universal-motive form remains barred by the evidence of Leviticus 4-5 and Numbers 15.
