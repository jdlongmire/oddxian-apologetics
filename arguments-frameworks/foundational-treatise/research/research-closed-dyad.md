## 1. The scrutiny item, verbatim

Item 2 of the "Outstanding scrutiny" list in corpus-alignment.md reads, in full:

> "2. Distinguish a closed dyad by definition from the assertion that any two-person relationship is obsessive."

Context in the same document. The list it belongs to is introduced as tests the triune-loving-communion framework still owes ("Outstanding scrutiny"), and the document's integration decisions already warn against overclaiming from the framework: "Do not claim that the earlier slavery manuscript already proves Trinitarian ontology. The imago Dei connection to self-love is a theological inference, not an explicit biblical definition." Item 2 therefore asks for a discrimination between two claims that the corpus's dyad language might be heard as making:

- (a) A definitional claim: a "closed dyad" is closed by stipulation; the term names a two-term relation defined as terminating in its own members.
- (b) An empirical-universal claim: two-person relationships as such are obsessive (closed, self-absorbed) by virtue of being two-person.

The item is satisfied if (a) and (b) are cleanly separated, (a) is kept only in its stipulative form, and (b) is either proved or withdrawn. This note argues (b) is false and must be withdrawn; (a) survives, with its vocabulary tightened.

## 3. What distinction is being drawn, and what work it does

Reconstructing from sec. 2.1-2.2, the corpus operates a scale of relational forms:

- **One term:** reflexive self-reference. The single self as its own object ("regressive").
- **Two terms:** reciprocal relation. Each term is the other's object; the relation can be modeled as a closed loop in which love circulates between the two and terminates in the pair ("obsessive," in the heuristic's word).
- **Three terms:** shared communion. Two terms jointly love a third; love is not only exchanged but shared, witnessed, and delighted in together ("expressive").

The work the scale does in the corpus's argument:

1. **Apologetic framing for Part I.** It presents the triune archetype as explanatorily richer than its rivals: a solitary divine archetype could model only self-love or love that needs creation; a merely dyadic archetype, on the closed-loop model, pictures love as mutual absorption. Triadic communion pictures love that is mutual and shared at once. Note the corpus's own ceiling on this work: the model "evaluates explanatory coherence"; revelation, not the model, establishes that God is triune (systematic-theology sec. 2; argument-map dependency note).
2. **A foil for the definition of sin.** The closed loop is the relational portrait of the closed self: a circuit that terminates in its members rather than opening to God and neighbor. This is where the dyad language touches the sin-root claim, and where conflation threatens (sec. 5).
3. **A parsimony claim (P12), held at UNCERTAIN by the corpus itself.** "Three is sufficient" is asserted as philosophical parsimony, expressly not as demonstrated necessity. P11's "categorically different" is a structural observation: a triadic relation has features no dyadic relation has (joint attention to a shared object, mediation between two by a third, the possibility of shared delight in another's love). That is true as far as it goes; the question is what moral or theological weight it can bear.

## 4. Testing the distinction

### 4.1 Scriptural footing: the texts load orientation, not number

No biblical text predicates the moral quality of a relation on the number of its terms. The survey runs the other way:

- **The dyad is God's own remedy for aloneness.** Genesis 2:18: "It is not good that the man should be alone"; the remedy instituted is a two-person relation, and Genesis 2:24 makes the pair "one flesh." Whatever the closed-loop model describes, it cannot describe the pair as such without contradicting the text that institutes pairing as good.
- **The dyad is commended.** Ecclesiastes 4:9: "Two are better than one." The same passage ends with the threefold cord (4:12), a proverb of strength under strain, not a moral ontology of number; it commends two first.
- **Marriage is a covenant dyad, ordered outward.** Malachi 2:14 calls the wife "your companion and your wife by covenant." The marriage dyad is constituted by covenant with God as witness and is ordered beyond itself: to fruitfulness (Genesis 1:28) and to the neighbor. A dyad can be structurally open.
- **The supreme covenant administration is two-party.** God's covenant with Israel is formally dyadic (God and Israel), and it is given to be opened outward: "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3). Dyadic form, open orientation.
- **The corpus's own number-texts do not bear numerical weight.** Matthew 18:20 ("where two or three are gathered") is, by the corpus's own reading (systematic-theology sec. 6), about Christ-centered gathering in discipline, "not a proof of Trinitarian ontology." John 17:24 and 1 John 4:8, the corpus's load-bearing texts, establish that God's love is eternal and essential; neither argues from three-ness.

**Finding 1.** Scripture attaches moral weight to a relation's orientation (toward or away from God and neighbor) and never to its cardinality. [Necessary inference from the texts surveyed, HIGH.]

### 4.2 Counterexamples

**Open dyads (against claim (b)):**
1. Marriage (Genesis 2:18-24; Malachi 2:14): divinely instituted, covenantal, ordered to God and to fruitfulness. Not obsessive, not closed by its two-ness.
2. The God-Israel covenant (Genesis 12:3): two-party in form, universal in intent.
3. The two criminals (Luke 23:39-43): identical dyadic situation, crucified beside Christ; one closes in mockery, one opens in plea and is received. The variable is orientation to Christ, not the number of terms.
4. David and Jonathan (1 Samuel 18-20): a two-person covenant friendship that opens outward, costing Jonathan his succession and protecting David for the sake of God's anointed; Scripture's portrait is commendation, not pathology.

**Closed multitudes and closed triads (against the converse slide, that three or more escapes closure):**
5. Babel (Genesis 11:4): a whole society, maximal cardinality, closed in on itself ("let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be dispersed over the face of the whole earth"). Closure at scale is still closure; indeed the sin-root note's grasping mode is here collectivized.
6. The triangulated family or clique of three: three persons whose shared bond is constituted by excluding a fourth (the scapegoat, the outsider) exhibit every feature of the "closed loop" with three terms. Family-systems literature names three-person closure "triangulation" as a pathology of three, not a cure for two (general knowledge; no text consulted for this note, flagged as illustration, not evidence).
7. The corpus's own tradition supplies the sharpest internal counterexample: the pactum salutis is classically framed between Father and Son (see the pactum research note: Witsius's definition is "the will of the Father, giving the Son... and the will of the Son, presenting himself"), two persons named as parties, and no one in the tradition calls the eternal counsel obsessive. The pactum note's own guards (one will appropriated to the persons; the Spirit to be named explicitly) handle that locus without any help from the dyad term. If two-ness as such carried a taint of closure, the corpus's Part II materials would be incoherent at their center. They are not; therefore two-ness carries no such taint.

**Finding 2.** Closure and openness vary independently of the number of a relation's terms. Any finite company, from one member to a nation, can terminate in itself; a company of two can be ordered beyond itself. [Necessary inference, HIGH.]

### 4.3 The analytic residue: what survives

What is true in P11 survives the counterexamples, because it is structural, not moral. A triadic relation has structural possibilities a dyadic relation lacks: two terms can share a third as a common object of love; a third can mediate, witness, and multiply the lines of relation (three dyadic lines plus the triadic whole, against the dyad's one line). This is a real categorical difference of form, and it is the legitimate content of "genuinely triadic shared communion" in the corpus's heuristic. But structural richness is not moral superiority, and the heuristic's predicates must be re-sorted:

- "Regressive" and "expressive" describe orientations (turning back on oneself; going out beyond oneself), which a relation of any size can take.
- "Obsessive" is the wrong word even for the model. Obsession is a psychological predicate, a disorder of a person's attention and desire; a structural model cannot be obsessive. The closed-loop model is accurately named **self-terminating**: a relation whose love is stipulated to end in its own members. Renaming costs the heuristic nothing and removes the standing invitation to hear claim (b).

**Finding 3.** The distinction is sound exactly and only as a stipulative distinction between relation-forms, with closure defined as self-termination. As a description of two-person relationships, or as a moral ranking of cardinalities, it is false. [Analytic, HIGH.]

### 4.4 Tradition check

No consulted source draws the corpus's exact distinction (no source in the corpus or the Phase 1 research notes uses "closed dyad" or moralizes two-ness). The nearest classical relative is Richard of St Victor, De Trinitate III, whose argument the corpus's scale resembles and probably descends from, at whatever remove. Mediation: **SECONDARY throughout this subsection** (Peter Leithart, "Love for a Third," Theopolis Institute essay, quoting De Trinitate III; the trinities.org paraphrase of De Trinitate III chs. 14-19; Dennis Ngien, "Richard of St. Victor's Condilectus," European Journal of Theology 12.2 (2003), summary). De Trinitate was not read directly for this note.

Richard's argument, as reported: charity cannot be solitary ("no one is properly said to have charity on the basis of his own private love of himself"); mutual love between two (dilectio) is true love, but consummate charity requires condilectio, shared love, "when a third person is loved by two persons harmoniously and in community." Three observations for the treatise's use:

1. **Richard does not pathologize the pair.** For Richard, two-person mutual love is dilectio, genuine love; what it lacks is not health but a further perfection, the sharing of delight in a co-beloved (condilectus). The corpus's word "obsessive" has no counterpart in Richard.
2. **Richard's operative variable is sharing, not counting.** The perfection he argues for is love that wills its joy to be shared; a third person is the first occasion of that sharing. This supports Finding 2: the moral weight sits on other-directedness, and "three" names the smallest company in which shared (not merely exchanged) love is possible.
3. **Richard argues inside faith, from the revealed datum.** His "necessary reasons" proceed from "God is love" (1 John 4:8) as given; he does not offer the scale as an independent proof that God must be three, and the standard difficulty (why the regress stops at three) is answered structurally, not numerically: with three, every pair has the third as condilectus, so shared love is complete at every line. The corpus's own discipline (P12 at UNCERTAIN; the model evaluates coherence, revelation establishes identity) is exactly Richard's posture, and Part I should keep it.

A caution from the other classical direction: Augustine's Trinitarian triads in De Trinitate are intra-personal (memory, understanding, will within one mind), not three participants; the tradition's own use of "three" is analogical and varies its referent. [General knowledge; De Trinitate (Augustine) not consulted in text for this note; flagged so Part I does not cite it from this note.] Cardinality language about God is therefore doubly analogical, and the corpus's remaining guard, divine simplicity (systematic-theology sec. 9), applies: intra-Trinitarian "relation" is not a creaturely relation with more terms.

## 5. The conflation risk: "closed" has three senses in the corpus

The scrutiny item matters beyond Part I because "closed" is the corpus's most loaded adjective and it now carries three distinct senses:

1. **The closed self** (moral): the fallen orientation of a person, incurvatus in se, adopted for Part IV in the qualified form (Q1-Q4) by JD on 2026-11-01.
2. **The closed dyad / closed loop** (structural): a stipulated relation-form, the subject of this note.
3. **Epistemically closed** (warrant): the WNA sense in the Covenant and Communion volume, "unwarranted to assert."

Senses 1 and 2 must not be fused. They differ in subject (a person's orientation vs a relation's form), in scope (the closed self can inhabit a relation of any size: a solitary, a marriage, a church, the Babel multitude), and in direction (the heuristic's scale actually assigns self-reflexive closure to the "one" position, not the "two": the incurved self is the monadic case). If Part IV prose slides from "the self is closed" to "the dyad is closed" to "two-person relations image the Fall," it will have committed claim (b) by equivocation, contradicted Genesis 2, and misplaced the sin-root, which the adopted formulation fixes at the level of the self's orientation precisely so that it is not indexed to circumstances such as relational company. The safe rule: **"closed" predicates orientation wherever it is used; when it is used of a relation, the relation's self-termination must be shown from the case, never inferred from its size.**

## 6. Verdict

**QUALIFY. Keep the distinction in stipulative form; retire the descriptive claim and the word "obsessive."** Scrutiny item 2 is satisfied by the following dispositions:

- Claim (a), the stipulative distinction, stands. [Analytic, HIGH.]
- Claim (b), that two-person relationships as such are obsessive or closed, is false on scriptural and structural grounds and is withdrawn. [HIGH.] The corpus's own gloss in systematic-theology sec. 2 ("applies to the closed-loop model rather than all two-person relationships") already performs this withdrawal; this note makes it binding on treatise drafting and extends it: the withdrawal covers any ranking of cardinalities, not only the word "obsessive."
- P11 stands as a formal observation about relation-forms (structural possibilities), at HIGH in the structural sense only, exactly as the argument map classified it.
- P12 remains a philosophical thesis at UNCERTAIN, usable as illustration of explanatory coherence, never as demonstration. The parsimony heuristic (one reflexive, two reciprocal, three shared) may be quoted in Part I as a heuristic, labeled as such, with "obsessive" replaced.

### 6.1 Formulation the treatise may use

For Part I (theology proper), and adapted for Part IX (ethics and apologetics):

> "The treatise uses "closed dyad" only as a defined term: a two-term relation stipulated to terminate in its own members, neither member ordered beyond the relation. So defined, closure is a property of orientation, not of number. A dyad may be open: marriage is God's own remedy for aloneness, a covenant ordered to him and fruitful beyond itself, and the covenant with Israel is two-party in form and universal in intent. A company of any size may be closed: Babel is a multitude incurved on its own name. God is not confessed as triune because three is a better number than two; he is confessed as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit because he has revealed himself so. The revealed communion is the archetype because of what it is, perfect mutual and shared love, not because of its cardinality. The scale one reflexive, two reciprocal, three shared may illustrate structural differences among relation-forms (a triad can share a beloved, witness, and mediate as a dyad cannot); it demonstrates nothing about God."

For Part IX specifically, one sentence is mandatory wherever the dyad term is used in ethical or apologetic application: the moral predicate is self-termination, wherever it is found, in an individual, a couple, a family, a church, or a nation; the term may never be used to disparage two-person relationships (marriage, friendship, the parent-child pair, the two-person household) as such.

### 6.2 What the treatise must not claim

1. That Scripture teaches, implies, or illustrates that two-person relationships are obsessive, deficient, or fallen as such. (Genesis 2:18-24; Ecclesiastes 4:9; Malachi 2:14 stand against it.)
2. That perfect love logically requires exactly three persons. (P12 is UNCERTAIN; the corpus itself concedes the point twice, systematic-theology secs. 2 and 9.)
3. That "closed dyad" and "closed self" are interchangeable, or that the dyad term may do any work in the definition of sin. Sin's root, in the adopted formulation, is the self's closure, which operates in companies of every size.
4. That the Father-Son framing of the pactum salutis is a "closed dyad" or suffers from one. Intra-Trinitarian acts are not creaturely relations (simplicity guard), and the pactum's own guards (one will appropriated; the Spirit named) govern that locus.
5. That Matthew 18:20 or Ecclesiastes 4:12 teaches an ontology of number. (The corpus already declines the first; the second is a proverb of strength.)
6. That a larger company is, by its size, more open, more loving, or nearer the archetype than a smaller one. (Babel stands against it.)
