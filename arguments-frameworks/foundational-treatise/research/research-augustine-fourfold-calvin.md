# Research Note: Augustine's Fourfold State of Man and Calvin on the Decree and the Fall

Project: foundational treatise (repo jdlongmire/oddxian-apologetics), Parts IV and V and the Appendix C broader-corpus source register.
Purpose: edition-specific sourcing for (1) Augustine's fourfold state of man, (2) Augustine, City of God XIV.13 and XIV.28, and (3) Calvin, Institutes 1.18, 3.23, 1.15 (with 2.1-2 flagged). Every source is labeled by mediation:

- DIRECT: the text was opened and read in this research pass (web reproduction of a named translation or parallel Latin text).
- SECONDARY: the wording or datum comes from a named secondary source quoting or describing the primary text; the primary text itself was not opened.
- NOT CONSULTED / UNVERIFIED: register data recorded for completeness; nothing in the treatise may rest on it until it is opened and checked.

A standing caution for Appendix C: the neat four-tag scheme (posse peccare et posse non peccare / non posse non peccare / posse non peccare / non posse peccare) is the later dogmatic tradition's standardized labeling of Augustine's distinctions. Augustine's own verified verbatim collocations are posse non peccare, non posse peccare, posse non mori, non posse mori (De correptione et gratia 12.33), and his prose descriptions in Enchiridion 104-107. The tag non posse non peccare for the fallen state was NOT found verbatim in any Augustinian text opened in this pass; it is the tradition's label (used, e.g., in the editor's note in Bavinck, Reformed Ethics, vol. 1) for the condition Augustine describes in prose. The treatise should present it that way and not put the tag in quotation marks as Augustine's ipsissima verba. See section 1.5.

## 1. Augustine: the fourfold state

### 1.1 State (a): Adam before the fall - posse peccare et posse non peccare

Primary locus: De correptione et gratia (On Rebuke and Grace), ch. 12, sec. 33 (caput XII). Latin, DIRECT (parallel Latin text, catholiclibrary.org reproduction of the Migne text):

"Quapropter, bina ista quid inter se differant, diligenter et vigilanter intuendum est; posse non peccare, et non posse peccare, posse non mori, et non posse mori, bonum posse non deserere, et bonum non posse deserere. Potuit enim non peccare primus homo, potuit non mori, potuit bonum non deserere. ... Prima ergo libertas voluntatis erat, posse non peccare; novissima erit multo major, non posse peccare: prima immortalitas erat, posse non mori; novissima erit multo major, non posse mori: prima erat perseverantiae potestas, bonum posse non deserere; novissima erit felicitas perseverantiae, bonum non posse deserere."

Wallis translation (NPNF First Series, vol. 5), DIRECT (logoslibrary.org): "Therefore the first liberty of the will was to be able not to sin, the last will be much greater, not to be able to sin; the first immortality was to be able not to die, the last will be much greater, not to be able to die; the first was the power of perseverance, to be able not to forsake good - the last will be the felicity of perseverance, not to be able to forsake good."

The mechanism is given in ch. 11-12 (secs. 29-34). Adam received an aid (adjutorium) of the "without which" kind only. Wallis, ch. 12, DIRECT: "The aid without which a thing does not come to pass is one thing, and the aid by which a thing comes to pass is another. ... Therefore to the first man, who, in that good in which he had been made upright, had received the ability not to sin, the ability not to die, the ability not to forsake that good itself, was given the aid of perseverance, not that by which it should be brought about that he should persevere, but that without which he could not of free will persevere." And ch. 11, DIRECT: "This first is the grace which was given to the first Adam; but more powerful than this is that in the second Adam. For the first is that whereby it is affected that a man may have righteousness if he will; the second, therefore, can do more than this, since by it is even effected that he will." This contrast (a leavable aid for Adam, an efficacious grace in Christ) is the "greater liberty" (maior libertas) argument the assignment points to: ch. 11 ends (sec. 32) with the angels, who stood by the same free will and received as reward "such a fulness of blessing that by it they might have the fullest certainty of always abiding in it," and with the maxim, "For what shall be more free than free will, when it shall not be able to serve sin?"

Corroborating locus: Enchiridion 28.104-107 (Outler translation, Library of Christian Classics, at CCEL), DIRECT. Sec. 105: "Thus it was fitting that man should be created, in the first place, so that he could will both good and evil - not without reward, if he willed the good; not without punishment, if he willed the evil. But in the future life he will not have the power to will evil; and yet this will not thereby restrict his free will. Indeed, his will will be much freer, because he will then have no power whatever to serve sin." Sec. 107: "Man was, therefore, made upright, and in such a fashion that he could either continue in that uprightness - though not without divine aid - or become perverted by his own choice." Outler's editorial footnotes 229-230 to this passage (SECONDARY, editor's apparatus) themselves supply the Latin tags: "Man's original capacities included both the power not to sin and the power to sin (posse non peccare et posse peccare). In Adam's original sin, man lost the posse non peccare ... In the fulfillment of grace, man will have the posse peccare taken away and receive the highest of all, the power not to be able to sin, non posse peccare. Cf. On Correction and Grace XXXIII."

### 1.2 State (b): fallen man - non posse non peccare (tradition's tag; Augustine's prose)

Augustine's own descriptions of the fallen condition, DIRECT:

- Enchiridion ch. 30 in the NPNF (Shaw) chaptering, as reproduced at Wikisource (the modern CCL section number for this passage was not verified in this pass; see section 5): "For, as a man who kills himself must, of course, be alive when he kills himself, but after he has killed himself ceases to live, and cannot restore himself to life; so, when man by his own free-will sinned, then sin being victorious over him, the freedom of his will was lost. 'For of whom a man is overcome, of the same is he brought in bondage' [2 Pet 2:19]. ... For he is freely in bondage who does with pleasure the will of his master. Accordingly, he who is the servant of sin is free to sin. And hence he will not be free to do right, until, being freed from sin, he shall begin to be the servant of righteousness."
- De correptione et gratia ch. 12 (Wallis), DIRECT: "But now, since that great freedom has been lost by the desert of sin, our weakness has remained to be aided by still greater gifts."
- De natura et gratia (On Nature and Grace, AD 415), argument as summarized in Schaff's editorial introduction (SECONDARY, CCEL/NPNF 1-05 front matter): Pelagius "alleged the possibility of not sinning to be inherent in man; and this he attributed to God's grace, on the ground that God is the author of that nature in which is inseparably inherent this possibility of avoiding sin." Augustine's reply defends grace "not indeed as in opposition to nature, but as that which liberates and controls nature" (Retractationes II.42, quoted in the same front matter, SECONDARY). That is: the posse of state (a) is not an inseparable property of nature as now propagated; fallen nature has lost it.

### 1.3 State (c): man under grace - posse non peccare (recovered, but in via never sinless in fact)

- De correptione et gratia ch. 12 (Wallis), DIRECT, on the saints predestined to the kingdom: to them "the aid of perseverance that is given is not such as the former, but such that to them perseverance itself is bestowed; not only so that without that gift they cannot persevere, but, moreover, so that by means of this gift they cannot help persevering." Yet in this life they still fight sin: "although as long as they live here they are fighting against sinful lusts, and some sins creep upon them unawares, on account of which they daily say, 'Forgive us our debts,' yet they do not any more obey the sin which is unto death."
- De natura et gratia 36.42 (the Mary passage), SECONDARY (quoted in Juniper Carol's study of the Immaculate Conception, citing Migne PL 44:267): "Now with the exception of the holy Virgin Mary in regard to whom, out of respect for the Lord, I do not propose to have a single question raised on the subject of sin - after all, how do we know what greater degree of grace for a complete victory over sin was conferred on her who merited to conceive and bring forth Him who all admit was without sin - to repeat then: with the exception of this Virgin, if we could bring together into one place all those holy men and women, while they lived here, and ask them whether they were without sin, what are we to suppose that they would have replied?" Register significance: for Augustine, the grace-state's posse non peccare is real but is never, Mary excepted by a singular grace, a sinlessness in fact during this life. Part IV should not state state (c) as attained sinlessness.
- Precision on the tag: the assignment's short form for state (c) is posse non peccare. Bavinck's editor (Reformed Ethics, vol. 1, ed. Bolt, Baker 2019; editor's note, SECONDARY, publisher's sample PDF) lists state (3) grace more fully as "able to sin or not to sin (posse peccare aut non peccare)," reserving unqualified posse non peccare language for the contrast with glory. Appendix C should note both forms.

### 1.4 State (d): glory - non posse peccare

- De correptione et gratia 12.33, Latin and Wallis as in 1.1 above: the novissima libertas "will be much greater, not to be able to sin" (non posse peccare), with the parallel pair posse non mori / non posse mori. DIRECT.
- Enchiridion 28.104-105 (Outler), DIRECT: in the better state man "not only would have been unable to sin, but would not have had even the will to sin" (sec. 104, of the state Adam would have reached had he stood); sec. 105, "his will will be much freer, because he will then have no power whatever to serve sin."
- City of God XXII.30 is the standard additional locus for the confirmed will in the final state. It was NOT opened in this pass: UNVERIFIED, and one secondary excerpt of Bavinck in circulation cites the posse distinction to "City of God XVI.27," which does not match the standard locus and should not be repeated. Do not cite CG XXII.30 (or XVI.27) for a claim until the text is opened.

### 1.5 Do not conflate: the other Augustinian fourfold

Enchiridion 31.118 sets out a different fourfold, a salvation-historical sequence: ante legem, sub lege, sub gratia, in pace ("Harum quattuor differentiarum prima est ante legem, secunda sub lege, tertia sub gratia, quarta in pace plena atque perfecta"). SECONDARY (Janssen, "Augustine's Enchiridion: An Anti-Pelagian Interpretation of the Creed," Religions 14 (2023), citing CCL page 112, lines 42-44). This is not the posse scheme of De correptione et gratia 12.33. Part IV uses the posse scheme; if the Enchiridion stages are mentioned, keep them distinct.

## 2. City of God XIV.13 and XIV.28 (the sin-root loci for Part IV)

### 2.1 XIV.28: the two cities founded by two loves

Dods translation (NPNF First Series, vol. 2), DIRECT (CCEL): "Accordingly, two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self. The former, in a word, glories in itself, the latter in the Lord."

Latin, SECONDARY (two independent academic citations of the critical text agree verbatim): "Fecerunt itaque civitates duas amores duo, terrenam scilicet amor sui usque ad contemptum Dei, caelestem vero amor Dei usque ad contemptum sui." Cited to CCL 48 (Dombart and Kalb, Corpus Christianorum Series Latina) in both; the page is reported inconsistently in the secondary literature (451 in one article, 415 in another, with 15.1 at 453 in the same article that gives 451, which fits the 451 reading). Register the edition as CCL 48, page UNVERIFIED. Dyson's Cambridge translation (1998) renders the sentence at p. 632 (SECONDARY, via a citing footnote).

### 2.2 XIV.13: pride as the beginning of sin; the evil will precedes the evil act

Dods translation, DIRECT (CCEL and logoslibrary.org, same text): "Our first parents fell into open disobedience because already they were secretly corrupted; for the evil act had never been done had not an evil will preceded it. And what is the origin of our evil will but pride? For 'pride is the beginning of sin.' And what is pride but the craving for undue exaltation? And this is undue exaltation, when the soul abandons Him to whom it ought to cleave as its end, and becomes a kind of end to itself. This happens when it becomes its own satisfaction." Later in the chapter, DIRECT: "The devil, then, would not have ensnared man in the open and manifest sin of doing what God had forbidden, had man not already begun to live for himself. It was this that made him listen with pleasure to the words, 'Ye shall be as gods'... By craving to be more, man becomes less; and by aspiring to be self-sufficing, he fell away from Him who truly suffices him."

Scripture reference: the NPNF footnote at "pride is the beginning of sin" reads "Ecclus. x. 13" (Sirach/Ecclesiasticus 10:13). The underlying Latin tag is initium omnis peccati superbia; the Clementine Vulgate places that wording at Ecclesiasticus 10:15 ("Initium omnis peccati superbia: qui tenuerit illam adimplebitur maledictis..."). Mediation for the verse-number split: SECONDARY (the NPNF note for 10:13; a scholarly article citing Ecclus 10:14-15 alongside the tag for 10:15). Hence the dual citation Sirach 10:13/15 is correct and should be retained with this explanation. The corresponding Latin of Augustine's own sentence in XIV.13 (initium enim omnis peccati superbia est, or similar) was NOT verified from a Latin text of book XIV opened in this pass: UNVERIFIED as to Augustine's exact wording; the English above is verified.

Corroboration for the angelic fall, DIRECT (parallel Latin, catholiclibrary.org): City of God XI.15, on "the devil sinneth from the beginning" (1 John 3:8): the devil is not to be thought to have sinned "ab initio ex quo creatus est" (from the beginning of his created existence) "sed ab initio peccati, quod ab ipsius superbia coeperit esse peccatum" (but from the beginning of his sin, which began to be sin from his pride). Pride is thus Augustine's named root for the angelic fall as well as the human fall; useful for Part IV's root-of-sin argument.


## 3. Calvin, Institutes (1559 Latin final edition; cited by book.chapter.section)

Translation discipline for this note: where the wording is Beveridge (1845, Calvin Translation Society) it is labeled Beveridge; the web text at calvinstitutes.org, sermonindex.net, biblestudytools.com, and worthychristianbooks.com reproduces Beveridge. Where the wording is Battles (Ford Lewis Battles, ed. John T. McNeill, Library of Christian Classics vols. 20-21, Westminster Press, 1960), it reached this note through verbatim quotation on secondary pages; two independent such quotations of 3.23.7 agree word for word, and the famous sentence is independently confirmed with its Latin. Treat Battles wording below as SECONDARY (verbatim as quoted, cross-checked) and Beveridge wording as DIRECT.

### 3.1 Institutes 1.18.1: the rejection of bare "permission"

Chapter title (Beveridge): "The instrumentality of the wicked employed by God, while He continues free from every taint."

Beveridge, DIRECT: "Hence a distinction has been invented between doing and permitting because to many it seemed altogether inexplicable how Satan and all the wicked are so under the hand and authority of God, that he directs their malice to whatever end he pleases, and employs their iniquities to execute his Judgments. ... It seems absurd that man should be blinded by the will and command of God, and yet be forthwith punished for his blindness. Hence, recourse is had to the evasion that this is done only by the permission, and not also by the will of God. He himself, however, openly declaring that he does this, repudiates the evasion. That men do nothing save at the secret instigation of God, and do not discuss and deliberate on any thing but what he has previously decreed with himself and brings to pass by his secret direction, is proved by numberless clear passages of Scripture."

On Job 1, DIRECT: "From the first chapter of Job we learn that Satan appears in the presence of God to receive his orders, just as do the angels who obey spontaneously. The manner and the end are different, but still the fact is, that he cannot attempt anything without the will of God. But though afterwards his power to afflict the saint seems to be only a bare permission, yet as the sentiment is true, 'The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away; as it pleased the Lord, so it has been done,' we infer that God was the author of that trial of which Satan and wicked robbers were merely the instruments."

On Ahab (1 Kings 22), DIRECT: "If the blinding and infatuation of Ahab is a Judgment from God, the fiction of bare permission is at an end; for it would be ridiculous for a judge only to permit, and not also to decree, what he wishes to be done at the very time that he commits the execution of it to his ministers."

Close of sec. 1, DIRECT: "it is perfectly clear that it is the merest trifling to substitute a bare permission for the providence of God, as if he sat in a watch-tower waiting for fortuitous events, his Judgments meanwhile depending on the will of man."

Register note: Calvin does not merely qualify "permission"; he calls the doing/permitting distinction, as used to insulate God, an invented evasion and a fiction. Part V may cite 1.18.1 for the rejection of bare (nud-) permission. The Latin wording of the sentence (commonly printed with nuda permissio) was NOT verified from a Latin text opened in this pass: UNVERIFIED as to Latin wording.

### 3.2 Institutes 1.18.2: hardening; the sum of the chapter's first movement

Beveridge, DIRECT: the blinding and hardening texts (Exod 4:21; Josh 11:20; Ps 105:25; Isa 10:6) "many would confine to permissions as if, by deserting the reprobate, he allowed them to be blinded by Satan. But since the Holy Spirit distinctly says, that the blindness and infatuation are inflicted by the just Judgment of God, the solution is altogether inadmissible." One nuance Part V should keep: Calvin can still use "permitted" of Satan's own success, subordinately: "Satan himself performs his part, just as he is impelled, and succeeds only in so far as he is permitted." Sum, DIRECT: "since the will of God is said to be the cause of all things, all the counsels and actions of men must be held to be governed by his providence; so that he not only exerts his power in the elect, who are guided by the Holy Spirit, but also forces the reprobate to do him service."

### 3.3 Institutes 1.18.3: one undivided will, not two contrary wills

The objection: "if nothing happens without the will of God, he must have two contrary wills, decreeing by a secret counsel what he has openly forbidden in his law." Calvin's own framing is important for Part V: he does NOT rest his answer on two wills in God. Beveridge, DIRECT: "Still, however, the will of God is not at variance with itself. It undergoes no change. He makes no pretence of not willing what he wills, but while in himself the will is one and undivided, to us it appears manifold, because, from the feebleness of our intellect, we cannot comprehend how, though after a different manner, he wills and wills not the very same thing." He then quotes Augustine, Enchiridion chs. 100-101 (the good son and the bad son both wishing concerning their father's death; "the things which God rightly wills, he accomplishes by the evil wills of bad men"), and Augustine on Psalm 111:2: "that is not done without his will which is done contrary to it, because it could not be done if he did not permit; nor does he permit it unwillingly, but willingly." So Calvin's operative distinctions in 1.18 are: God's one will, hidden (secret counsel/decree) and revealed (law/precept), with the difference located in manner and in our apprehension, not in a divided God. The sharp will-versus-precept distinction is drawn in sec. 4.

### 3.4 Institutes 1.18.4: will is not precept; no excuse for the wicked

Beveridge, DIRECT: "Here will is improperly confounded with precept, though it is obvious, from innumerable examples, that there is the greatest difference between them. ... Thus we must hold, that while by means of the wicked God performs what he had secretly decreed, they are not excusable as if they were obeying his precept, which of set purpose they violate according to their lust." And the Augustinian resolution Calvin adopts: "there is no concurrence between God and man, when by His righteous impulse man does what he ought not to do," the same act carrying different reasons in God and in man ("Since the Father delivered up the Son, Christ his own body, and Judas his Master, how in such a case is God just, and man guilty, but just because in the one act which they did, the reasons for which they did it are different?" - Augustine, Ep. 194 in some numberings; Calvin's own citation reads "Ep. 48, ad Vincentium" in the Beveridge text; the letter number varies by edition and is flagged here, not resolved).

### 3.5 Institutes 3.23.7: decretum quidem horribile, fateor

Context (Beveridge, DIRECT, biblestudytools.com): sec. 7 opens by answering the objection, "They deny that it is ever said in distinct terms, God decreed that Adam should perish by his revolt. As if the same God, who is declared in Scripture to do whatsoever he pleases, could have made the noblest of his creatures without any special purpose. They say that, in accordance with free-will, he was to be the architect of his own fortune, that God had decreed nothing but to treat him according to his desert. ... But whether they will allow it or not, predestination is manifest in Adam's posterity."

The sentence itself, Battles translation of 3.23.7 (Institutes, vol. 2, p. 955), SECONDARY (verbatim Battles text as quoted on two independent pages, wording identical): "Again I ask: whence does it happen that Adam's fall irremediably involved so many peoples, together with their infant offspring, in eternal death unless because it so pleased God? Here their tongues, otherwise so loquacious, must become mute. The decree is dreadful indeed, I confess. Yet no one can deny that God foreknew what end man was to have before he created him, and consequently foreknowledge because he so ordained by his decree. If anyone inveighs against God's foreknowledge at this point, he stumbles rashly and heedlessly. What reason is there to accuse the Heavenly Judge because he was not ignorant of what was to happen? If there is any just or manifest complaint, it applies to predestination. And it ought not to seem absurd for me to say that God not only foresaw the fall of the first man, and in him the ruin of his descendants, but also meted it out in accordance with his own decision." (One clause above, "and consequently foreknowledge because," is garbled in one of the two web reproductions; the sense, confirmed by the other reproduction, is "and consequently foreknew because he so ordained by his decree." Flagged deliberately: quote from a print Battles before the treatise goes to press.)

Latin, SECONDARY (quoted identically in two sources, one a scholarly monograph foreword): "Decretum quidem horribile, fateor." French 1561 version: "Je confesse que ce decret nous doit epouvanter" (accent on the first e of epouvanter normalized for ASCII). Semantics: horribile here is "dreadful" in the sense of awe-full, not "vile" or "repulsive"; McNeill's note in the Battles edition (Institutes, vol. 1 series numbering aside, at 3.23.7, fn 17), SECONDARY (quoted in Robert L. Reymond's response in Perspectives on Election: Five Views, 2006, p. 330 n. 22): "Calvin is awestruck but unrelenting in his declaration that God is the author of reprobation." Reymond adds the cross-reference to horribilis Dei maiestas ("God's dread majesty") at Institutes 3.20.17. Register caution: in its paragraph, the "decree" Calvin calls dreadful is the decree as it encompasses Adam's fall and the ruin of his posterity, within a chapter (3.23) whose subject is the refutation of objections to election and reprobation; it is not a freestanding label for reprobation alone, and Part V should quote it with its paragraph.

### 3.6 Institutes 3.23.8: falls by providence, falls by his own fault

Battles, SECONDARY (verbatim as quoted): "For the first man fell because the Lord had judged it to be expedient; why he so judged is hidden from us. ... Besides, their perdition depends upon the predestination of God in such a way that the cause and occasion of it are found in themselves. ... Man falls according as God's providence ordains, but he falls by his own fault."

Beveridge, DIRECT (biblestudytools.com): "Man therefore falls, divine providence so ordaining, but he falls by his own fault. The Lord had a little before declared that all the things which he had made were very good (Gen. 1:31). Whence then the depravity of man, which made him revolt from God? Lest it should be supposed that it was from his creation, God had expressly approved what proceeded from himself. Therefore man's own wickedness corrupted the pure nature which he had received from God, and his ruin brought with it the destruction of all his posterity." Latin tag for the key clause, SECONDARY (Shedd, Dogmatic Theology, quoting Calvin): "suo vitio cadit" (he falls by his own fault). Register significance: Calvin runs the Gen 1:31 "very good" approval as an argument that depravity is not from creation; directly usable in Part IV alongside Augustine XIV.13 ("vice is contrary to nature," Dods).

Related 3.23 sentences in Battles, SECONDARY (verbatim as quoted): sec. 2, God's will "is not only free of all fault but is the highest rule of perfection, and even the law of all laws"; sec. 4, "I admit that in this miserable condition wherein men are now bound, all of Adam's children have fallen by God's will."

### 3.7 Institutes 1.15.8: Adam's original state - upright, mutable, not yet confirmed

Beveridge, DIRECT (sermonindex.net and worthychristianbooks.com, same text): "It were here unseasonable to introduce the question concerning the secret predestination of God, because we are not considering what might or might not happen, but what the nature of man truly was. Adam, therefore, might have stood if he chose, since it was only by his own will that he fell; but it was because his will was pliable in either direction and he had not received constancy to persevere, that he so easily fell. Still he had a free choice of good and evil; and not only so, but in the mind and will there was the highest rectitude..." (the sentence continues, in Strong's quotation of the same section, SECONDARY: "...and all his organic parts were rightly disposed to obedience, till, destroying himself, he corrupted all his excellencies"). This is Calvin's exact counterpart to Augustine's state (a): integrity without confirmation; the will upright but pliable (flexibilis), lacking the constancy (constantia) that belongs to state (d). Cite as 1.15.8.

Institutes 2.1-2 (original sin; the remnants of free will) were NOT opened in this pass: UNVERIFIED as loci. The treatise's claims about Adam's original state do not need them; they are carried by 1.15.8, 3.23.8, and Augustine. If Part IV wants Calvin on the transmission of corruption, open 2.1.4-5 and 2.2 in a later pass before citing.
