# Research Note: The Two Wills of Christ and His Impeccability

**Purpose.** Exposition note for treatise Part VII, and the verification base for
section 6.24 of the working paper "From Very Good to Perfected Loving Communion."
It covers (1) the Sixth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople III, 680-681),
(2) Maximus the Confessor on natural and gnomic will, (3) the Reformed
exposition (Turretin, the Westminster standards, the peccability debate), and
(4) a synthesis assigning each claim its dogmatic tier.

**Mediation key.** Every source is labeled. [Direct] means the text itself was
read in this session (in English translation where the original is Greek or
Latin; the translation is named). [Secondary] means the content is reported
through a named scholarly source that was read. [Reported] means the material
is quoted or cited by another author and was not independently opened.
[Not consulted] is stated plainly where it applies.

## 1. The Sixth Ecumenical Council: Constantinople III (680-681)

**Setting.** [Direct: the acts in the Schaff translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene
Fathers, second series, vol. 14, as reproduced at patristics.co; introductory
material in the same translation at clerus.org.] The council was convened by
the emperor Constantine IV, sat in eighteen sessions from 7 November 680 to
16 September 681, and received as consonant with Chalcedon the dogmatic letter
of Pope Agatho and the synodal letter of the Roman synod of 125 bishops held
under him. Its Definition of Faith stands in the acts of Session XVIII.

**Finding 1.1: The Definition confesses two natural wills and two natural
operations.** [Direct.] "Defining all this we likewise declare that in him are
two natural wills and two natural operations indivisibly, inconvertibly,
inseparably, inconfusedly, according to the teaching of the holy Fathers." The
four Chalcedonian adverbs are carried over from natures to wills and
operations, so that the completeness of each nature is protected at the level
of volition and activity, not merely at the level of essence.

**Finding 1.2: The two wills are expressly declared not contrary; the human
will follows and is subject to the divine will.** [Direct.] "And these two
natural wills are not contrary the one to the other (God forbid!) as the
impious heretics assert, but his human will follows and that not as resisting
and reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and omnipotent will." The
Definition adds, after Athanasius, that "it was right that the flesh should be
moved but subject to the divine will," and that the human will, "although
deified, was not suppressed, but was rather preserved," quoting Gregory the
Theologian: "His will is not contrary to God but altogether deified."

**Finding 1.3: The Definition's own scriptural anchor for the wills is John
6:38.** [Direct.] The council reads Christ's word as the will of his flesh
speaking: "as he himself says: I came down from heaven, not that I might do my
own will but the will of the Father which sent me! where he calls his own will
the will of his flesh, inasmuch as his flesh was also his own." The Gethsemane
word (Matt 26:39; Luke 22:42) is expounded at length in the materials the
council received and approved, namely Agatho's dogmatic letter and its
patristic florilegium, where Athanasius (against Apollinaris) is quoted on
"Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless not my
will but yours be done": "he shows that there are two wills, the one human
which is the will of the flesh, but the other divine." [Direct: Agatho's
letter as printed in the acts.] Precision point: in the Schaff translation the
Definition proper quotes John 6:38; Luke 22:42 enters through Agatho's letter
and the florilegium, which the council adopted. Treatise citations should keep
that distribution exact.

**Finding 1.4: Two operations are proved from Leo's Tome.** [Direct.] "For
each form does in communion with the other what pertains properly to it, the
Word, namely, doing that which pertains to the Word, and the flesh that which
pertains to the flesh." The Definition refuses "one natural operation in God
and in the creature," on the ground that this would either exalt the created
into the divine essence or degrade the divine nature to the place of the
creature.

**Finding 1.5: The council anathematizes the monothelite teachers by name.**
[Direct.] The Definition names as the instruments of the error Theodore of
Pharan; Sergius, Pyrrhus, Paul, and Peter of Constantinople; Honorius of Rome;
Cyrus of Alexandria; Macarius of Antioch; and Stephen, Macarius's disciple.
Its closing canon deposes bishops or clerics who teach a different faith and
anathematizes monks or laymen who do so. The council's address to the emperor
(the Prosphoneticus) states the sentence in terms: "we cast out of the Church
and rightly subject to anathema" the named teachers, "and all who have taught,
or do teach, or shall presume to teach one will and one operation in the
incarnate Christ." A formal sentence against the Monothelites also stands in
the acts at Session XIII. [Direct.]

**Finding 1.6: What the council does not define.** [Direct, by inspection of
the Definition.] The council confesses Christ "in all things like us, sin only
excepted" (the Chalcedonian predicate, restated), but it nowhere uses a modal
formula of inability to sin, and it nowhere defines the term impeccability.
Its decree settles the number, naturality, and harmony of the wills. The modal
question (could Christ have sinned?) is left to theological inference; see
section 4.

## 2. Maximus the Confessor: Natural Will and Gnomic Will

**Finding 2.1: The natural will (thelesis physike) is a faculty of the nature,
not of the person.** [Direct: Opusculum 3, in an English translation
reproduced at 4marksofthechurch.com; the translator and edition could not be
verified from that page, and the excerpt is used with that caveat.] Maximus:
when the Fathers speak of two wills in Christ "they do not speak of quantity in
relation to gnomic wills, but only in relation to natural wills, rightly
calling the essential and natural laws and principles of what has been united
wills." The natural will is "the power that longs for what is natural" and
"contains all the properties that are essentially attached to the nature";
without it "the human nature cannot be." Will therefore belongs to nature as
such: a nature without a natural will is an incomplete nature, and a Christ
without a human natural will would not be fully man.

**Finding 2.2: The gnomic will (thelema gnomikon, from gnome) is a mode of
willing, not a second faculty.** [Secondary: "Holy Scripture in the
Monothelite Controversy: The Standpoint of Maximus the Confessor," Vox Patrum
37 (2017), a peer-reviewed article consulted in full text at the journal site,
citing Maximus at PG 91.] In Maximus's analysis the gnomic will is the
deliberative inclination of a particular person who must weigh alternatives
under partial ignorance: it "implies hesitation and deliberation since a
person does not know what he/she wants," and in fallen persons it is the seat
of wavering and of sin. Gnomic willing is thus a way (a tropos) in which
fallen hypostases use the natural will, not a constituent (a logos) of human
nature itself. That is why its absence from Christ is not a defect of his
humanity: what is not assumed is not healed applies to nature and its
faculties, not to a fallen mode of exercising them.

**Finding 2.3: Christ has the natural human will and does not have a gnomic
will.** [Secondary: the Vox Patrum article, summarizing the Disputation with
Pyrrhus and the Opuscula.] "Christ did not possess gnomic will. He was never
in a state of ignorance concerning what he wanted. His human will was perfect
and could not oppose the divine will or lead to sin. It was a deified will."
The Disputation with Pyrrhus (the debate of 645 with the deposed monothelite
patriarch Pyrrhus, the locus classicus for the distinction) argues that Christ
is a willing agent in both natures: Maximus, as quoted by the article from the
Disputation (PG 91, 324C), insists that Christ was endowed with will "not only
as God and consubstantial with the Father, but also as man and consubstantial
with us." A scholarly excerpt of Maximus's reasoning puts the point as a
reductio: had Christ possessed a gnomic will, he would have been
"double-minded and double-willed, and fighting against himself, so to speak,
in the discord of his thoughts, and therefore double-personed," since "it was
only this difference of gnomic wills that introduced into our lives sin and
our separation from God." [Secondary: scholarly article on Maximus's
dyothelitism consulted via ResearchGate; the excerpt quotes Maximus without,
in the portion read, pinning the passage to a single opusculum.]

**Finding 2.4: Luke 22:42 in Maximus's argument: the recoil is the natural
will, and it is not opposition.** [Direct: Opusculum 3; Secondary: Jonathan
Bieler, "Maximus the Confessor on Christ's Human Will," Communio 43 (2016),
consulted in full text via academia.edu.] Maximus reads the agony as the human
natural will doing what human nature does in the face of death, and then being
freely conformed to the divine will. Opusculum 3: the Saviour "possesses as a
human being the natural disposition to will, and this is moved and shaped by
his divine will"; he wills "in a fleshly way the shrinking in the face of death
together with the rest of the passions," and quotes the prayer itself:
"Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not mine,
but your will be done," "showing, in the shrinking, the determination of the
human will shaped and brought to be (in harmony with the divine will)." The
conclusion: "The Saviour therefore possesses as a human being a natural will,
which is shaped, but not opposed, by his divine will." Bieler's study
confirms the reading and its polemical target: the deprecation of the chalice
"was a natural act of his truly human will" and "in no way did this natural
act of deprecation contradict the will of the Father, who created nature and
its natural fear of death"; the deprecation itself belongs to the economy of
salvation, against Sergius's suggestion that the prayer shows a human will
contradicting the divine. Following Cyril, Maximus adds the soteriological
point: in the agony Christ assumes our shrinking from death in order to remove
it from our nature.

**Finding 2.5: A modern critical caveat.** [Secondary: a critical article on
Maximus's theory of the two wills in the particular man, consulted via
ResearchGate.] Not all modern readers find the construction costless: the
critic notes that on general anthropological grounds Maximus attributes both a
natural and a gnomic will to ordinary human beings, and argues that he was
driven by the Christological case to deny the gnomic will to Christ and to
treat gnome as bound up with sin. The treatise should therefore present the
natural/gnomic distinction as Maximus's theological construction (received by
the tradition and ratified in effect by the council's outcome), not as though
Constantinople III had defined the gnome apparatus itself. It did not; see
Finding 1.6 and section 4.

**Mediation status for Maximus.** The Disputation with Pyrrhus was NOT read in
full in this session, and Joseph P. Farrell's English translation (St.
Tikhon's Seminary Press, 1990) is [Reported] only, as cited in secondary
literature. The Greek text (PG 91) and the modern critical editions were NOT
consulted. Primary-text access in this session is excerpt-level: Opusculum 3
in an unattributed web translation, and Disputation passages as cited with PG
references in the Vox Patrum article. Greek and Latin critical verification of
Maximus remains OPEN.

## 3. The Reformed Exposition

### 3.1 Turretin, Institutes of Elenctic Theology, Thirteenth Topic

[Direct: Francis Turretin, Institutes of Elenctic Theology, trans. George
Musgrave Giger, ed. James T. Dennison Jr., vol. 2 (P and R, 1994), read in a
full-text PDF reproduction; the Latin Institutio was not consulted.]

**Finding 3.1.1: There is no standalone question on the two wills or on
impeccability in the Thirteenth Topic.** The topic "The Person and State of
Christ" runs to nineteen questions (verified against the volume's table of
contents). The two-wills doctrine and Christ's sinlessness are treated inside
other questions, chiefly Q. V (the nature assumed), Q. VII (the hypostatic
union), Q. XI (the conception), Q. XII (the graces bestowed on the human
nature), and Q. XIII (the knowledge of Christ's soul). Citations of Turretin
"on the two wills" should name these loci rather than imply a dedicated
question.

**Finding 3.1.2: The two wills are affirmed as a proof of the unconfused
union, on the basis of Luke 22:42.** In Q. VII, section XIV (vol. 2, pp.
320-321), arguing against Eutychian confusion of the natures, Turretin gives
as his second reason: "(2) two wills are ascribed to him ('not my will, but
thine, be done,' Lk. 22:42)." He immediately blocks the Nestorian inference:
it does not follow that there are two willing persons, "because the will
belongs to the nature, while willing belongs to the person"; and the number of
wills does not track the number of persons, since "in God there are three
persons, but only one will." This is the standard Reformed reception of
Constantinople III: dyothelitism is presupposed and deployed, with the
nature/person distribution doing the analytical work.

**Finding 3.1.3: Sinlessness is confessed at every relevant locus; the modal
claim rests on the plenitude and permanence of grace and on the union.** Q. V
(vol. 2, pp. 306-310) establishes that the assumed nature is like ours in all
things, "sin excepted." Q. XII (vol. 2, pp. 347-348) teaches that the human
nature received habitual graces in a "fulness of abundance" beyond all angels
and men, the Spirit given "without measure" (John 3:34), the gifts being not
transient acts but "permanent and fixed" possessions. In Q. XIII, section II
(vol. 2, p. 349), on the knowledge of Christ's soul, Turretin remarks of the
ignorance ascribed to Christ that it cannot be the ignorance of a depraved
disposition, "not the least shadow of [sin] do we acknowledge to have been in
him." The same volume's treatment of the glorified state supplies the modal
vocabulary he works with: paradise knew posse non peccare ("to be able not to
sin"); the glorified state, "the state and condition of the elect angels," is
non posse peccare ("not to be able to sin"). [Direct: vol. 2, in the locus on
the state of glory.]

**Finding 3.1.4: Turretin's position and its scholarly reception.** That
Turretin holds impeccability, and argues for the compatibility of necessity
with free and meritorious obedience in Christ, is the received scholarly
reading. [Secondary: "Christological Arguments for Compatibilism in Reformed
Theology," Themelios, consulted at thegospelcoalition.org, summarizing Paul
Helm: Turretin and Edwards were "adherents to Chalcedonian Christology" who
affirm both dyothelitism with "volitional non-contrariety" and "Christ's
impeccability," and who reject the notion that freedom requires indifference
by appealing to both.] The article locates Turretin's free-will discussion of
these matters in vol. 1 (the Tenth Topic) at 1:501-508; that volume was NOT
consulted in this session, and the citation is [Reported] through the article.

### 3.2 The Westminster Standards: the confessional boundary, stated precisely

[Direct: WCF ch. 8 and WLC QQ. 36-40 in their standard texts, consulted at
thewestminsterstandard.org, reformedforum.org, and refcm.org.]

**Finding 3.2.1: What the standards confess.** WCF 8.2: the Son took "man's
nature, with all the essential properties and common infirmities thereof, yet
without sin," so that "two whole, perfect, and distinct natures, the Godhead
and the manhood, were inseparably joined together in one person, without
conversion, composition, or confusion"; the cited proof for "yet without sin"
is Heb 2:14, 16-17 and 4:15. WCF 8.3 adds that he was thereby "holy, harmless,
undefiled, and full of grace and truth." WCF 8.7: "Christ, in the work of
mediation, acteth according to both natures, by each nature doing that which
is proper to itself," with the properties of one nature sometimes attributed
in Scripture to the person denominated by the other. WLC 36: Christ "was and
continues to be God and man, in two entire distinct natures, and one person,
forever." WLC 37: he took "a true body, and a reasonable soul... yet without
sin." WLC 39 gives, among the reasons he had to be man, that he might "have a
fellow-feeling of our infirmities." WLC 40: the Mediator had to be God and man
in one person "that the proper works of each nature might be accepted of God
for us, and relied on by us, as the works of the whole person."

**Finding 3.2.2: What the standards do not say.** Neither WCF 8 nor WLC 36-40
contains the word "will" or "wills" as a predicate of Christ's natures.
There is no two-wills formulary, no statement that the human will follows and
is subject to the divine will, and no statement of impeccability (no non posse
peccare). The standards confess the two complete natures, the one person,
real operations proper to each nature (8.7), and the fact of sinlessness
("yet without sin"). Dyothelitism is entirely consistent with, and arguably
presupposed by, "two whole, perfect, and distinct natures" plus 8.7, but as a
matter of confessional text it is not formulated there. Treatise and working
paper must therefore not cite WCF 8 or WLC 36-40 as though they "confess two
wills": the precision flag raised in the preparation of section 6.24 is
confirmed by direct inspection of the texts. The standards function as the
boundary within which the conciliar doctrine is received, not as a second
formulation of it.

### 3.3 The peccability debate

**Finding 3.3.1: Impeccability stated.** W. G. T. Shedd, Dogmatic Theology,
vol. 2, Christology, ch. 5 (consulted [Direct] in full-text reproduction at
sermonindex.net and purelypresbyterian.com; pagination differs by edition:
pp. 330-349 in the original vol. 2; pp. 659-662 in the modern edition as cited
by Kevin DeYoung's Theological Primer at thegospelcoalition.org [Secondary]).
Shedd: Christ "was characterized not only by the posse non peccare, but by the
non posse peccare. He was not only able to overcome temptation, but he was
unable to be overcome by it."

**Finding 3.3.2: Shedd's proofs.** [Direct.] (a) From Scripture: the
immutability of Christ (Heb 13:8) covers his holiness; a mutable holiness
could not be described in terms proper to unchangeable divine holiness; the
possibility of defeat is inconsistent with his omnipotence, since all
temptation is finite and creaturely in origin; omniscience excludes the
deception on which successful temptation partly depends. (b) From the
constitution of the person: sin is an act of a person, and this person is the
God-man; "the divine determines and controls the human, not the human the
divine," so the person's power of resistance "must be measured not by the
human nature but by the divine." Hence the careful distribution: "Christ
while having a peccable human nature in his constitution, was an impeccable
person." The Logos could consent to suffer in the human nature but not to sin
in it, since the guilt of a sinful act would attach to the whole theanthropic
person. (c) From the two wills expressly: "Again, the impeccability of Christ
is proved by the relation of the two wills in his person to each other. Each
nature, in order to be complete, entire, and wanting nothing, has its own
will; but the finite will never antagonizes the infinite will, but obeys it
invariably and perfectly."

**Finding 3.3.3: The objection in its strongest historical form.** Charles
Hodge denied impeccability (Systematic Theology, vol. 2, p. 457), in words
Shedd himself quotes in a footnote [Reported through Shedd, and independently
through Willmington, "The Impeccability of Jesus Christ," Liberty University
digital commons, consulted]: "The sinlessness of our Lord... does not amount
to absolute impeccability. It was not a non potest peccare. ... Temptation
implies the possibility of sin. If from the constitution of his person it was
impossible for Christ to sin, then his temptation was unreal and without
effect, and he cannot sympathize with his people." Shedd notes that Theodore
of Mopsuestia and Abelard also denied impeccability, and that Augustine and
Anselm affirmed it. The objection has two prongs: (i) a definition (temptation
analytically implies the possibility of succumbing), and (ii) a pastoral
consequence (an impeccable Christ cannot sympathize, against Heb 4:15).

**Finding 3.3.4: The standard reply.** [Direct: Shedd.] (a) The definition is
denied: "It is objected... that it is inconsistent with his temptability. A
person who cannot sin, it is said, cannot be tempted to sin. This is not
correct; any more than it would be correct to say that because an army cannot
be conquered, it cannot be attacked. Temptability depends upon the
constitutional susceptibility, while impeccability depends upon the will."
Christ was open to every temptation that works through natural susceptibility,
"excepting those that spring out of lust or corruption of nature," which he
did not have. (b) The sympathy prong is inverted: because he never yielded,
Christ bore the full and cumulative force of every temptation to its end,
which those who yield early never do; his sympathy is therefore greater, not
less. (c) The analogy of confirmed creatures: "That an impeccable being can be
tempted is proved by the instance of the elect angels. Having 'kept their
first estate,' they are now impeccable, not by their own inherent power, but
by the power of God bestowed upon them. But they might be tempted still,
though we have reason to believe that they are not." And: "redeemed men in
heaven are impeccable through the grace and power of Christ their head. Yet
they are still temptable, though not exposed to temptation." The only
absolutely intemptable being is God (James 1:13). Impeccability in a creature,
or in the God-man according to his humanity, is therefore compatible with
genuine assault, genuine suffering under assault, and genuine sympathy.

## 4. Synthesis for the Treatise

**Tier A: Conciliar dogma (Constantinople III, 680-681).**
- A1. Christ has two natural wills and two natural operations, one divine and
  one human, without division, change, partition, or confusion.
- A2. The two wills are not contrary; the human will follows the divine will,
  not resisting or reluctant, but subject to it; the human will is deified yet
  not suppressed.
- A3. The scriptural anchors as the council itself deploys them: John 6:38 in
  the Definition; the Gethsemane prayer (Matt 26:39; Luke 22:42) through
  Agatho's letter and its Athanasian florilegium; Leo's Tome for the two
  operations.
- A4. Sinlessness is inside the conciliar confession as a fact ("in all things
  like us, sin only excepted," received from Chalcedon), but the council
  defines no modal inability.
What would change this tier: nothing short of showing the received conciliar
text to be corrupt, which is not a live possibility; the open work is textual
(the critical edition of the acts, Acta Conciliorum Oecumenicorum, was not
consulted) and would refine wording, not substance. This tier stands at the
highest confidence.

**Tier B: Confessional (Westminster).**
- B1. Two whole, perfect, distinct and entire natures, inseparably joined in
  one person, forever (WCF 8.2; WLC 36).
- B2. The human nature is complete: true body and reasonable soul, with all
  essential properties and common infirmities, yet without sin (WCF 8.2;
  WLC 37).
- B3. Christ acts according to both natures, each doing what is proper to
  itself; the proper works of each nature are relied on as the works of the
  whole person (WCF 8.7; WLC 40).
- B4. Negative precision: the standards formulate neither two wills nor
  impeccability. Any treatise sentence of the form "the Westminster standards
  confess two wills" is false as a textual claim and must be rewritten as a
  claim of consistency or consequence.
What would change this tier: nothing historical; only confessional revision.
The tier is fixed by the texts, which were inspected directly.

**Tier C: Theological inference (the working paper's MEDIUM tag is
confirmed as the right calibration).**
- C1. Impeccability (non posse peccare). The claim that Christ could not sin
  is a cumulative inference: the acting subject is the divine Son (acts belong
  to the person); God cannot sin or be tempted with evil (James 1:13; Heb
  6:18); the human nature was endowed with the Spirit without measure and with
  habitual grace in its fulness, permanent and fixed (Turretin, XIII.xii); the
  immutability of Christ (Heb 13:8); his own challenge, "Which of you
  convinces me of sin?" (John 8:46); and the decretal necessity that the
  Mediator not fail. It is the majority position of the Reformed orthodox
  (Turretin as received; Shedd's full-dress defense), but it is an inference,
  not a conciliar definition and not a confessional article.
- C2. The Maximian apparatus: that gnome is a fallen mode of using the natural
  will rather than a faculty of nature, and that Christ therefore has no
  gnomic will. As a report of Maximus this is secure at excerpt level; as
  dogma it is derivative, resting on Tier A plus a contested anthropological
  analysis (see Finding 2.5).
- C3. The working paper's use of Luke 22:42, John 6:38, and Heb 4:15 as the
  scriptural controls is verified as the tradition's own anchoring (the
  council, Turretin, and the standards' proof texts respectively), which
  strengthens the paper's procedure without raising C1 above inference: the
  texts state sinlessness, distinction of wills, and subjection of the human
  will; the impossibility is drawn from them together with the person axiom.
What would raise C1: a demonstration, accepted across the Reformed tradition,
that the premises yield impeccability by good and necessary consequence in
the sense of WCF 1.6, together with an answer to Hodge's definition of
temptation that commands the field. What would lower C1: acceptance of
Hodge's premise that temptation analytically requires peccability; or
rejection of the axiom that actions and their moral quality belong to the
person rather than to a nature considered separately; or exegesis showing
that any control text predicates a real possibility of failure of Christ
(none is presently known). The standing dissent of a theologian of Hodge's
weight inside the tradition is itself sufficient reason to keep the tag at
MEDIUM: the inference is strong, multiply grounded, and majority-held, but it
is neither defined nor uncontested.
