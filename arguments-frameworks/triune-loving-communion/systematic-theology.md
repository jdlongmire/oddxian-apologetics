## Abstract
This study proposes that loving communion is the moral ideal grounded in God's eternal triune character. Humanity, created in God's image, is called to love God and neighbor. The proposed universal root of sin is closed self-interest, while individual sins are disordered expressions of that root. Divine law reveals righteousness and governs fallen conduct. Christ accomplishes reconciliation and restores participation in communion, consummated in the new creation. The analysis distinguishes direct biblical claims from systematic inference and philosophical hypotheses.

## Governing thesis
> God's eternal triune character establishes the moral ideal: loving communion. Creation reveals its intended expression. The Fall explains its corruption and the true nature of sin: closed self-interest. Law governs humanity amid that corruption. Christ reveals and accomplishes its restoration.

## 1. Eternal triune character
John 17:24 places the Father's love for the Son before creation; 1 John 4:8 identifies love with God's character. Matthew 28:19 and 2 Corinthians 13:14 contribute to the biblical basis of triune revelation. The formulation of one divine essence and three distinct persons is a doctrinal synthesis. God's love requires no created object to complete it. The thesis that loving communion constitutes the moral archetype is a systematic inference from these texts, not a verbatim biblical formula.

## 2. Relational parsimony
**One is regressive. Two is obsessive. Three is expressive.** This heuristic distinguishes reflexive self-reference, reciprocal dyadic relation, and genuinely triadic shared communion. “Obsessive” applies to the *closed-loop model* rather than all two-person relationships. A triadic relation requires three distinct participants. That analytic fact does not independently prove that perfect divine love logically requires exactly three persons. God's self-revelation establishes the theological identity; the relational model evaluates explanatory coherence.

## 3. Creation and imago Dei
Genesis 1:26-27 establishes humanity's image-bearing dignity; Genesis 2:18 illustrates human relational vocation. Matthew 22:37-40 orders love toward God and neighbor. Self-love is understood as legitimate regard for one's God-given life and dignity, properly expressed in communion. This is an inference requiring argument; it does not entail that creatures share God's essence.

## 4. The Fall and closed self-interest
Genesis 3 depicts creaturely judgment and desire elevated above divine command. Romans 1:21-25 depicts disordered worship and consequent moral disorder. **Closed self-interest** denotes the creature treating self as the ultimate practical reference point, displacing God and neighbor. Pride, greed, hatred, idolatry, exploitation, and neglect are proposed manifestations.

The universal assertion that *every* sin stems from closed self-interest remains to be demonstrated. It must account for culpable ignorance, omissions (James 4:17), action against conscience (Romans 14:23), fear, and apparently altruistic wrongdoing. It would be circular to define closed self-interest simply as whatever makes an action sinful. Distinguish ultimate moral orientation, conscious motivation, culpability, and consequences.

## 5. Law and accommodation
Romans 7:12 describes the law as holy and good. Galatians 3:19 relates law to transgressions. Matthew 19:8 distinguishes permission amid hardness of heart from the created ideal. Thus law reveals righteousness and also governs fallen conduct. Permission is not by itself equivalent to moral endorsement, but neither does every permission automatically establish moral disapproval. Interpret each regulation in its own covenantal and literary context.

## 6. Christ and restored communion
John 13:34-35 makes love among disciples a public mark of allegiance to Christ. John 17:20-23 connects believers' unity to the communion of Father and Son. Matthew 18:20 illustrates Christ-centered gathering in the context of community discipline, not a proof of Trinitarian ontology. Colossians 1:19-22 grounds reconciliation in Christ. Revelation 21:1-4 describes consummated dwelling with God.

## 7. Eternal covenant
Hebrews 13:20 speaks of the eternal covenant; Jeremiah 31:31-34 portrays covenantal intimacy and internalized law. Ephesians 1:4-5 and Titus 1:2 locate redemptive purpose before the ages. The claim that covenantal redemption restores loving communion is a theological synthesis. A specific intratrinitarian covenant of redemption (*pactum salutis*) remains a systematic inference and must not be conflated with the eternal being of God.

## 8. Case study: slavery
The existing [slavery manuscript](../slavery-argument/biblical_slavery_final_integrated.md) appeals to neighbor-love as the moral criterion for interpreting Mosaic legislation. The new thesis provides a proposed ontological foundation for that criterion. Leviticus 25:44-46 remains a difficult text permitting inheritable foreign slavery. Philemon and Ephesians 6:5-9 also require direct examination. The abolitionist trajectory and the accommodation thesis must be argued rather than assumed.

## 9. Principal objections
A closed dyad may be a stipulative model; triadic sufficiency does not prove divine numerical necessity. Universal closed self-interest risks circularity and counterexamples. Classical divine simplicity constrains relational analogies. The eternal covenant must be distinguished from freely willed redemptive acts. Difficult slavery texts challenge simplistic appeals to trajectory.

## Conclusion
The framework offers a systematic connection between divine character, human vocation, sin, law, and redemption. The central biblical themes are well attested; the strongest universal and numerical claims remain open to formal testing. Further work requires close textual exegesis and engagement with opposing interpretations.
