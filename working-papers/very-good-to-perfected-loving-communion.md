# Working Paper: From Very Good to Perfected Loving Communion

**Status:** Working hypothesis, initial draft  
**Author:** James D. Longmire  
**Date:** 2026-11-01  
**Repository:** oddxian-apologetics  
**Discipline:** Biblical theology, theological anthropology, providence, theodicy

## Abstract

Genesis declares the completed creation *very good* (Genesis 1:31), while the biblical narrative culminates in an incorruptible new creation and perfected communion with God (Revelation 21–22). This working paper examines the proposal that these are distinct states in one eternal divine decree. Created loving communion was genuinely good, upright, and mutable. Perfected loving communion is secured in Christ, incorruptible and everlasting. The Fall was genuinely evil and regrettable in its moral character, yet ordained within God's purpose of glorifying the Son as Judge and Redeemer. Refinement and redemption carry a real cost, borne supremely by the incarnate Son. The thesis must distinguish what Scripture directly establishes from inferences about necessity.

## 1. Definitions and thesis

- **Good (Hebrew *ṭôḇ*):** God's favorable evaluation of created works in Genesis 1.
- **Very good (*ṭôḇ meʾōḏ*):** God's intensified evaluation of the completed creation (Genesis 1:31). The phrase does not itself specify metaphysical immutability or final consummation.
- **Perfected communion:** the consummated condition of the redeemed, permanently secured in righteousness and communion with God (Revelation 21–22).
- **Mutable agency:** original human moral agency with a genuine capacity for rebellion.
- **Refinement:** God's redemptive work bringing created humanity to glorified conformity to Christ; evil itself is not a morally constructive substance.
- **Decretal necessity:** necessity conditional on God's eternally chosen purpose; distinguish from absolute logical or metaphysical necessity.
- **Divine regret:** Scripture's genuine presentation of God's holy displeasure at wickedness, without presuming ignorance or failure of divine sovereignty.

**Proposed thesis:** God's eternal purpose was perfected loving communion through Christ. Original creation was wholly and very good for its intended initial state, though humanity was mutable. The Fall, judgment, sacrifice, and redemption were regrettable but necessary within God's chosen decree, culminating in the Son's manifestation as Judge and Redeemer and the confirmation of redeemed humanity in everlasting communion.

## 2. Exegetical foundations

### 2.1 Creation's evaluation

Genesis 1:4, 10, 12, 18, 21, and 25 repeatedly use *good*; Genesis 1:31 uses *very good*. Genesis 2:18 identifies the man's aloneness as *not good* before the Fall, illustrating a condition awaiting its intended resolution rather than necessarily moral evil. The lexical distinction between *ṭôḇ* and terms sometimes rendered complete, blameless, or perfect must be evaluated contextually. An argument from the absence of the word *perfect* alone is insufficient.

### 2.2 Original integrity and consummation

Ecclesiastes 7:29 describes humanity as made upright. Romans 8:29–30 locates the redeemed destiny in conformity to Christ and glorification. First Corinthians 15:42–49 contrasts corruptibility and incorruptibility and the first and last Adam. Revelation 21:4 and 21:27 describe the final exclusion of death and impurity. The original and consummated conditions should not be collapsed.

### 2.3 Sovereignty over evil

Genesis 50:20 attributes contrasting intentions to Joseph's brothers and God concerning the same events: human evil and divine good purpose. Acts 2:23 and 4:27–28 connect the crucifixion, human culpability, and divine predestination. Ephesians 1:11 affirms comprehensive providence. Romans 8:28–30 connects providential good to the destiny of those called according to God's purpose. These texts firmly ground divine sovereignty over morally evil acts; applying them to the specific necessity of the Fall requires theological synthesis.

### 2.4 Regret and the divine decree

Genesis 6:6 depicts divine regret and grief at human wickedness. Interpret alongside Genesis 50:20 and the providential texts without flattening regret into mere rhetoric or treating it as proof of divine surprise. The precise account of divine affections and anthropopathic language remains a doctrinal task.

### 2.5 The Son as Judge and Redeemer

John 17:5 affirms the Son's glory before creation. First Peter 1:19–20 places Christ's redemptive mission in the pre-creation divine purpose. Romans 9:22–23 concerns the revelation of wrath, mercy, and glory. Hebrews 2:10 and 5:8–9 address the completion of Christ's mediatorial mission through suffering. These texts motivate the proposed teleology but must not be made to imply that God's intrinsic glory depends on evil.

## 3. Proposed theological sequence

1. Eternal triune loving communion: the uncreated archetype and ground of the moral ideal.
2. Very good created loving communion: genuine, upright, mutable, and initially unconfirmed.
3. Fall and redemptive history: culpable rebellion, judgment, suffering, and the costly divine work of redemption.
4. Perfected loving communion: redeemed and glorified humanity, confirmed in righteousness through Christ.

**Key formulation:** The Fall was regrettable in its evil and suffering, yet necessary relative to the decree God freely chose. The Son bears the supreme redemptive cost of the ordained path to consummation.

## 4. Argument structure and limits

**P1 (textually grounded):** God declares the original creation very good (Genesis 1:31).

**P2 (textually grounded):** Scripture distinguishes original upright humanity from glorified, incorruptible humanity (Ecclesiastes 7:29; 1 Corinthians 15:42–49).

**P3 (textually grounded):** God intentionally governs events that remain evil in human intention (Genesis 50:20; Acts 2:23).

**P4 (textually grounded):** God's purpose for the redeemed includes conformity to Christ and glorification (Romans 8:28–30).

**P5 (theological proposal):** God's chosen eternal decree includes the Fall as part of the path by which the Son is manifested as Judge and Redeemer and created communion is perfected.

**Conclusion (conditional):** Given P5, the Fall is necessary relative to that decree, while remaining evil and regrettable. P1–P4 support the coherence of this conclusion but do not alone prove P5 or establish that all mutable free creatures must eventually sin.

## 5. Objections requiring treatment

- **Mutability versus inevitability:** Capacity to sin does not logically imply eventual sin. Develop an explicit argument if claiming ontological inevitability independent of decree.
- **Sinless freedom:** Christ's sinlessness and the confirmed righteousness of glorified humanity challenge simplistic claims that inability to sin makes persons robots. Specify the distinction between original and perfected agency.
- **Divine authorship of sin:** Explain how sovereign ordination and creaturely culpability coexist; engage Westminster Confession of Faith 3.1 and relevant primary theological texts.
- **Divine regret:** Distinguish moral displeasure from frustration of decree while taking the language of Genesis 6:6 seriously.
- **Necessity of evil:** Redemption from sin conceptually presupposes sin, but God's intrinsic glory does not. Avoid claiming that God lacked the power to create a sinless world.
- **The problem of suffering:** Clarify why this decree, its costs, and its final good are morally coherent; do not claim full resolution where Scripture does not supply it.
- **Pre-Fall physical conditions:** Genesis 1:31 alone does not settle animal death, predation, entropy, or geological processes.

## 6. A Unified Response to the Problem of Evil and the Euthyphro Dilemma

### 6.1 Shared foundation: the eternal moral archetype

The proposed moral ontology grounds objective goodness in the eternal, triune character of God, whose interpersonal loving communion precedes creation (John 17:5, 24; 1 John 4:8). Love is therefore not introduced by a contingent command or dependent upon creatures. This claim requires an account of the full range of divine goodness, including holiness, justice, truth, and righteousness, rather than reducing morality to an unspecified sentiment of love. Loving communion must be defined in a way that excludes coercion, exploitation, and closed self-interest.

### 6.2 The Euthyphro dilemma

The classic dilemma asks whether the good is good because God commands it or God commands it because it is independently good. On the divine-nature account, God's immutable character supplies the ontological ground of goodness, while divine commands authoritatively express that character in creaturely relations. Neither an external standard nor arbitrary voluntarism is required. This is a proposed third account, not a formal disproof of every Euthyphro objection.

**Remaining tests:** Does identifying goodness with God's nature explain why that nature is good without circularity? How do particular positive commands relate to universal moral obligations? Can justice and holy judgment be derived coherently from the same communion-centered ontology? Evaluate the distinction between God's essential nature and contingent decrees.

### 6.3 The logical Problem of Evil

The logical PoE alleges inconsistency among God's omnipotence, omniscience, perfect goodness, and the existence of evil. The proposed response adds a candidate morally sufficient purpose: the freely chosen decree to bring initially mutable, very good created communion to consummated, confirmed communion through the Son's redemptive and judicial work. Genesis 50:20 and Acts 2:23 support intentional divine governance of events that remain morally evil. Genesis 6:6 supplies the language of genuine divine grief. Romans 8:28–30 identifies conformity to Christ and glorification as the good of God's people.

**Logical scope:** If it is possible that God has a morally sufficient reason to ordain a history containing evil, the bare coexistence of God and evil does not entail contradiction. The present thesis proposes a particular biblical account of that reason. It does not follow that evil is itself good, that God requires evil to possess goodness, or that every evil act is individually necessary in isolation.

### 6.4 The evidential and existential Problems of Evil

The evidential PoE asks whether the actual scale, apparently gratuitous instances, unequal distribution, and duration of suffering make theism less plausible. The refinement thesis gives a global teleology but does not by itself demonstrate why a particular child's suffering, natural disaster, or atrocity was indispensable. Nor does a future consummation trivialize present injury. Any complete defense must address natural evil, nonhuman suffering, the uneven burdens of history, and whether equivalent goods were achievable by less costly means. Biblical lament, judgment, and the cross must remain substantive features of the account.

### 6.5 The necessity of refinement: modal distinctions

Let **D** denote God's actual freely chosen eternal decree; **F** the Fall; **R** redemption from sin; **C** consummated communion; and **J** the Son's manifestation as Judge and Redeemer in the specified redemptive history.

1. **Conditional/decretal claim:** If D includes F as an ordained means in the actual history, then given D, F occurs. This expresses faithfulness of providence to the decree, not an independent proof that F is logically unavoidable.
2. **Conceptual relation:** Redemption *from sin* presupposes sin; judicial condemnation of culpable rebellion presupposes rebellion. Neither proposition establishes that every possible expression of divine glory requires sin.
3. **Open stronger claim:** F is necessary in every metaphysically possible history leading to C. This does not follow from Genesis 1:31, Genesis 50:20, or Romans 8:28 alone and requires additional argument.
4. **Agency distinction:** Original mutability permits rebellion; glorified impeccability secures righteous freedom. Mutability alone does not entail inevitable rebellion.

The thesis should retain the phrase **“regrettable but necessary”** with its qualifier: *necessary relative to God's chosen decree*. The burden of showing that the actual decree is uniquely optimal or that no sinless path to C was possible remains open.

### 6.6 Integrated argument and objections

**M1:** Objective moral goodness is grounded in God's eternal character (theological and philosophical premise; compare 1 John 4:8; John 17:24).

**M2:** God's commands express that character and bind creatures in their relations (proposed divine-nature account).

**P1:** God sovereignly intends good through acts whose creaturely intentions are evil (Genesis 50:20; Acts 2:23).

**P2:** God directs His redeemed people toward conformity to Christ and glorification (Romans 8:28–30).

**P3:** God's chosen decree includes the Fall and the Son's redemptive and judicial mission (systematic theological synthesis; requires further demonstration).

**Conditional conclusion:** M1–M2 furnish an answer to Euthyphro; P1–P3 furnish a possible morally sufficient divine purpose addressing the logical PoE. Together they form a unified account of the standard by which evil is condemned and the good toward which providence moves. They do **not** deductively settle the evidential PoE or establish the unique necessity of this history.

**Priority objections:** the alleged circularity of divine-nature ethics; God's relationship to sin as first cause; whether suffering can be justified without instrumentalizing persons; the status of the unevangelized and the finally condemned; and the apparent availability of sinless perfected communion.

## 8. Provisional assessment

**HIGH (direct biblical text):** original creation declared very good; God purposefully governs human evil; redeemed humanity is destined for glorification.

**MEDIUM (theological synthesis):** original integrity and consummated perfection are distinct states within one divine purpose.

**UNCERTAIN (further argument needed):** unperfected free creatures must inevitably sin; the Fall was the only possible route to perfected loving communion.

**Working claim:** The Fall was *regrettable but necessary* within the particular divine decree that culminates in the Son's glory as Judge and Redeemer. This claim is advanced as a theological thesis for rigorous exegetical and philosophical testing, not as a proven lexical entailment of Genesis 1:31.
