# Working Paper: From Very Good to Perfected Loving Communion

**Status:** Working hypothesis, consistency-reviewed draft  
**Author:** James D. Longmire  
**Date:** 2026-11-01  
**Repository:** oddxian-apologetics  
**Discipline:** Biblical theology, theological anthropology, providence, theodicy

## Abstract

Genesis declares the completed creation *very good* (Genesis 1:31), while the biblical narrative culminates in an incorruptible new creation and perfected communion with God (Revelation 21–22). This working paper examines the proposal that these are distinct states in one eternal divine decree. Created loving communion was genuinely good, upright, and mutable. Perfected loving communion is secured in Christ, incorruptible and everlasting. The Fall was genuinely evil and regrettable in its moral character, yet ordained within God's purpose of glorifying the Son as Judge and Redeemer. Refinement and redemption carry a real cost, borne supremely by the incarnate Son. The thesis distinguishes what Scripture directly establishes from inferences about necessity, including the distinction between universal providential purpose and event-by-event instrumental indispensability.

## 1. Definitions and thesis

- **Good (Hebrew *ṭôḇ*):** God's favorable evaluation of created works in Genesis 1.
- **Very good (*ṭôḇ meʾōḏ*):** God's intensified evaluation of the completed creation (Genesis 1:31). The phrase does not itself specify metaphysical immutability or final consummation.
- **Perfected communion:** the consummated condition of the redeemed, permanently secured in righteousness and communion with God (Revelation 21–22).
- **Mutable agency:** original human moral agency with a genuine capacity for rebellion.
- **Refinement:** God's redemptive work bringing created humanity to glorified conformity to Christ; evil itself is not a morally constructive substance.
- **Decretal necessity:** the certainty of what God has freely decreed, conditional upon that decree; distinguish from absolute logical or metaphysical necessity and from the claim that each particular event is instrumentally indispensable.
- **Apparently gratuitous suffering:** suffering whose morally sufficient divine purpose is not discernible to a finite observer.
- **Actually gratuitous suffering:** suffering that lacks any morally sufficient purpose; the proposed framework excludes this given its premises of perfectly good, wise, comprehensive providence.
- **Divine regret:** Scripture's genuine presentation of God's holy displeasure at wickedness, without presuming ignorance or failure of divine sovereignty.

**Proposed thesis:** God's eternal purpose was perfected loving communion through Christ. Original creation was wholly and very good for its intended initial state, though humanity was mutable. The Fall, judgment, sacrifice, and redemption were regrettable but necessary within God's chosen decree, culminating in the Son's manifestation as Judge and Redeemer and the confirmation of redeemed humanity in everlasting communion.

## 2. Exegetical foundations

### 2.1 Creation's evaluation

Genesis 1:4, 10, 12, 18, 21, and 25 repeatedly use *good*; Genesis 1:31 uses *very good*. Genesis 2:18 identifies the man's aloneness as *not good* before the Fall, illustrating a condition awaiting its intended resolution rather than necessarily moral evil. The lexical distinction between *ṭôḇ* and terms sometimes rendered complete, blameless, or perfect must be evaluated contextually. An argument from the absence of the word *perfect* alone is insufficient.

### 2.2 Original integrity and consummation

Ecclesiastes 7:29 describes humanity as made upright. Romans 8:29–30 locates the redeemed destiny in conformity to Christ and glorification. First Corinthians 15:42–49 contrasts corruptibility and incorruptibility and the first and last Adam. Revelation 21:4 and 21:27 describe the final exclusion of death and impurity. The original and consummated conditions should not be collapsed.

### 2.3 Sovereignty over evil

Genesis 50:20 attributes contrasting intentions to Joseph's brothers and God concerning the same events: human evil and divine good purpose. Acts 2:23 and 4:27–28 connect the crucifixion, human culpability, and divine predestination. Ephesians 1:11 affirms comprehensive providence. Romans 8:28–30 connects providential good to the destiny of those called according to God's purpose. These texts firmly ground divine sovereignty over morally evil acts; applying them to the specific necessity of the Fall requires theological synthesis.

### 2.4 Regret and the divine decree

Genesis 6:6 depicts divine regret and grief at human wickedness. Interpret alongside Genesis 50:20 and the providential texts without flattening regret into mere rhetoric or treating it as proof of divine surprise. The precise account of divine affections and anthropopathic language remains a doctrinal task.

### 2.5 The Son as Judge and Redeemer

John 17:5 affirms the Son's glory before creation. First Peter 1:19–20 places Christ's redemptive mission in the pre-creation divine purpose. Romans 9:22–23 concerns the revelation of wrath, mercy, and glory. Hebrews 2:10 and 5:8–9 address the completion of Christ's mediatorial mission through suffering. These texts motivate the proposed teleology but must not be made to imply that God's intrinsic glory depends on evil.

## 3. Proposed theological sequence

1. Eternal triune loving communion: the uncreated archetype and ground of the moral ideal.
2. Very good created loving communion: genuine, upright, mutable, and initially unconfirmed.
3. Fall and redemptive history: culpable rebellion, judgment, suffering, and the costly divine work of redemption.
4. Perfected loving communion: redeemed and glorified humanity, confirmed in righteousness through Christ.

**Key formulation:** The Fall was regrettable in its evil and suffering, yet necessary relative to the decree God freely chose. The Son bears the supreme redemptive cost of the ordained path to consummation.

## 4. Argument structure and limits

**P1 (textually grounded):** God declares the original creation very good (Genesis 1:31).

**P2 (textually grounded):** Scripture distinguishes original upright humanity from glorified, incorruptible humanity (Ecclesiastes 7:29; 1 Corinthians 15:42–49).

**P3 (textually grounded):** God intentionally governs events that remain evil in human intention (Genesis 50:20; Acts 2:23).

**P4 (textually grounded):** God's purpose for the redeemed includes conformity to Christ and glorification (Romans 8:28–30).

**P5 (theological proposal):** God's chosen eternal decree includes the Fall as part of the path by which the Son is manifested as Judge and Redeemer and created communion is perfected.

**Conclusion (conditional):** Given P5, the Fall is necessary relative to that decree, while remaining evil and regrettable. P1–P4 support the coherence of this conclusion but do not alone prove P5 or establish that all mutable free creatures must eventually sin.

## 5. Objections requiring treatment

- **Mutability versus inevitability:** Capacity to sin does not logically imply eventual sin. Develop an explicit argument if claiming ontological inevitability independent of decree.
- **Sinless freedom:** Christ's sinlessness and the confirmed righteousness of glorified humanity challenge simplistic claims that inability to sin makes persons robots. Specify the distinction between original and perfected agency.
- **Divine authorship of sin:** Explain how sovereign ordination and creaturely culpability coexist; engage Westminster Confession of Faith 3.1 and relevant primary theological texts.
- **Divine regret:** Distinguish moral displeasure from frustration of decree while taking the language of Genesis 6:6 seriously.
- **Necessity of evil:** Redemption from sin conceptually presupposes sin, but God's intrinsic glory does not. Avoid claiming that God lacked the power to create a sinless world.
- **The problem of suffering:** Clarify why this decree, its costs, and its final good are morally coherent; do not claim full resolution where Scripture does not supply it.
- **Pre-Fall physical conditions:** Genesis 1:31 alone does not settle animal death, predation, entropy, or geological processes.

## 6. A Unified Response to the Problem of Evil and the Euthyphro Dilemma

### 6.1 Shared foundation: the eternal moral archetype

The proposed moral ontology grounds objective goodness in the eternal, triune character of God, whose interpersonal loving communion precedes creation (John 17:5, 24; 1 John 4:8). Love is therefore not introduced by a contingent command or dependent upon creatures. This claim requires an account of the full range of divine goodness, including holiness, justice, truth, and righteousness, rather than reducing morality to an unspecified sentiment of love. Loving communion must be defined in a way that excludes coercion, exploitation, and closed self-interest.

### 6.2 The Euthyphro dilemma

The classic dilemma asks whether the good is good because God commands it or God commands it because it is independently good. On the divine-nature account, God's immutable character supplies the ontological ground of goodness, while divine commands authoritatively express that character in creaturely relations. Neither an external standard nor arbitrary voluntarism is required. This is a proposed third account, not a formal disproof of every Euthyphro objection.

**Remaining tests:** Does identifying goodness with God's nature explain why that nature is good without circularity? How do particular positive commands relate to universal moral obligations? Can justice and holy judgment be derived coherently from the same communion-centered ontology? Evaluate the distinction between God's essential nature and contingent decrees.

### 6.3 The logical Problem of Evil

The logical PoE alleges inconsistency among God's omnipotence, omniscience, perfect goodness, and the existence of evil. The proposed response adds a candidate morally sufficient purpose: the freely chosen decree to bring initially mutable, very good created communion to consummated, confirmed communion through the Son's redemptive and judicial work. Genesis 50:20 and Acts 2:23 support intentional divine governance of events that remain morally evil. Genesis 6:6 supplies the language of genuine divine grief. Romans 8:28–30 identifies conformity to Christ and glorification as the good of God's people.

**Logical scope:** If it is possible that God has a morally sufficient reason to ordain a history containing evil, the bare coexistence of God and evil does not entail contradiction. The present thesis proposes a particular biblical account of that reason. It does not follow that evil is itself good, that God requires evil to possess goodness, or that every evil act is individually necessary in isolation.

### 6.4 The evidential and existential Problems of Evil

The evidential PoE asks whether the actual scale, apparently gratuitous instances, unequal distribution, and duration of suffering make theism less plausible. The refinement thesis, together with comprehensive wise and holy providence, denies *actual* gratuitousness within its theological premises (§6.12). It does not disclose why a particular child's suffering, natural disaster, or atrocity occurred, nor demonstrate that each event was instrumentally indispensable. Nor does a future consummation trivialize present injury. An external evidential defense must still engage natural evil, nonhuman suffering, the uneven burdens of history, and arguments that equivalent goods might have been achieved by less costly means. Deuteronomy 29:29 and warranted trust (§§6.9–6.10) delimit what is knowable without replacing that defense. Biblical lament, judgment, and the cross must remain substantive features of the account.

### 6.5 The necessity of refinement: modal distinctions

Let **D** denote God's actual freely chosen eternal decree; **F** the Fall; **R** redemption from sin; **C** consummated communion; and **J** the Son's manifestation as Judge and Redeemer in the specified redemptive history.

1. **Conditional/decretal claim:** If D includes F as an ordained means in the actual history, then given D, F occurs. This expresses faithfulness of providence to the decree, not an independent proof that F is logically unavoidable.
2. **Conceptual relation:** Redemption *from sin* presupposes sin; judicial condemnation of culpable rebellion presupposes rebellion. Neither proposition establishes that every possible expression of divine glory requires sin.
3. **Open stronger claim:** F is necessary in every metaphysically possible history leading to C. This does not follow from Genesis 1:31, Genesis 50:20, or Romans 8:28 alone and requires additional argument.
4. **Agency distinction:** Original mutability permits rebellion; glorified impeccability secures righteous freedom. Mutability alone does not entail inevitable rebellion.

The thesis should retain the phrase **“regrettable but necessary”** with its qualifier: *necessary relative to God's chosen decree*. The burden of showing that the actual decree is uniquely optimal or that no sinless path to C was possible remains open.

### 6.6 Integrated argument and objections

**M1:** Objective moral goodness is grounded in God's eternal character (theological and philosophical premise; compare 1 John 4:8; John 17:24).

**M2:** God's commands express that character and bind creatures in their relations (proposed divine-nature account).

**P1:** God sovereignly intends good through acts whose creaturely intentions are evil (Genesis 50:20; Acts 2:23).

**P2:** God directs His redeemed people toward conformity to Christ and glorification (Romans 8:28–30).

**P3:** God's chosen decree includes the Fall and the Son's redemptive and judicial mission (systematic theological synthesis; requires further demonstration).

**Conditional conclusion:** M1–M2 furnish an answer to Euthyphro; P1–P3 furnish a possible morally sufficient divine purpose addressing the logical PoE. Together they form a unified account of the standard by which evil is condemned and the good toward which providence moves. They do **not** deductively settle the evidential PoE or establish the unique necessity of this history.

**Priority objections:** the alleged circularity of divine-nature ethics; God's relationship to sin as first cause; whether suffering can be justified without instrumentalizing persons; the status of the unevangelized and the finally condemned; and the apparent availability of sinless perfected communion.

### 6.7 Election, redemptive necessity, and the ordained minimum

The central thesis is refined by distinguishing **election as God's choice of persons and destination** from **the content of the actual redemptive decree**. Ephesians 1:4–5 grounds election in Christ before the foundation of the world and specifies holiness and adoption; Romans 8:29–30 joins predestination, conformity to the Son, and glorification. Neither text, taken in isolation, proves that election in every conceivable divine decree entails a Fall.

The actual biblical decree, however, encompasses the Son's saving work in response to sin. Redemption *from sin* conceptually requires actual sin; righteous judgment *of culpable rebellion* requires actual rebellion; the ordained crucifixion involves suffering and culpable human action (Acts 2:23; 4:27–28). Hebrews 2:10 connects the Son's completed mediatorial work with suffering. These claims establish that **some evil and suffering belong necessarily to fulfillment of this particular decree**, without asserting that every evil event is individually indispensable or that God could not decree a different sinless history.

**Refined thesis:** God eternally elected a people in Christ for perfected loving communion. His freely chosen decree includes the Son's glorification as Redeemer and Judge through actual rebellion, righteous judgment, and redemptive suffering. Some evil and judgment therefore belong to the fulfillment of the actual redemptive decree; Romans 9:22–23 further connects the manifestation of justice and mercy to their actual objects. God sovereignly determines the precise historical extent and distribution within His decree (Ephesians 1:11). Those evils remain morally evil and genuinely regrettable. Their occurrence is certain relative to the decree; the claim does not prove that each event is uniquely indispensable to the redemptive end. The classical *pactum salutis* provides a theological synthesis of the eternal redemptive purpose (Ephesians 1:3–14; 1 Peter 1:19–20; Titus 1:2; John 17:1–5, 24), rather than a covenant explicitly named as such in these passages.

**Ontological qualification:** A creature cannot be an uncreated, self-existent God. Yet this does not by itself prove that God could not immediately establish a dependent creature in confirmed righteousness. The proposed necessity of redemptive history rests on the *content of God's decree*, not an alleged logical entailment from creatureliness to inevitable sin.

**Scope limitation:** The argument supports necessity of *some* evil and suffering in the ordained redemptive history. It does not justify each particular atrocity, prove that every instance of natural suffering is indispensable, or establish that the actual decree is the only logically possible decree. Those are separate evidential and modal obligations.

### 6.8 Objections and responses: initial dialectical register

**O1. Could God not have created everyone already perfected?**  
**Response:** A creature cannot possess God's underived aseity. Created beings can nevertheless be permanently secured in righteousness through divine sustaining grace. An immediately confirmed sinless humanity is not ruled out by creatureliness alone; it would not instantiate the specific redemptive history God decreed. **Residual:** Why this decree rather than another remains a question about divine wisdom, not a deduction from Genesis 1:31.

**O2. Does election itself necessitate evil?**  
**Response:** Election unto holiness and adoption (Ephesians 1:4–5) does not, abstractly considered, entail rebellion. Election *within the actual decree of redemption from sin and judgment of sin* entails that actual sin occurs. **Residual:** Establish the full content of the decree by contextual exegesis rather than assuming it from the word *election*.

**O3. Does this make God the author of sin?**  
**Response:** Genesis 50:20 distinguishes God's good intention from the brothers' evil intention concerning the same event; Acts 2:23 similarly joins divine determination and culpable human action. This demonstrates scriptural coexistence, but a full metaphysical account of secondary causes and culpability is still required. **Residual:** Engage Westminster Confession 3.1 and 5.4 from the primary confessional text and compare alternative theological accounts.

**O4. If God regrets evil, why decree it?**  
**Response:** Genesis 6:6 depicts genuine divine grief at wickedness; Genesis 50:20 depicts sovereign good intention through human evil. A coherent account distinguishes the moral evaluation of an act from God's comprehensive providential intention. **Residual:** Examine divine impassibility, anthropopathic language, and the scope of divine affections without dismissing either text.

**O5. Why not obtain the same good without suffering?**  
**Response:** Redemption from sin and the ordained suffering of Christ have determinate content in this decree (Acts 2:23; Hebrews 2:10). This provides a response concerning the *kind* of history decreed. **Residual:** It does not disclose the particular purpose of each suffering event or prove each event uniquely indispensable. Within the model, apparent gratuitousness does not entail actual gratuitousness (§6.12).

**O6. Does glorified freedom disprove the need for evil?**  
**Response:** Glorified human beings can be dependent creatures confirmed in righteousness (1 Corinthians 15:42–49; Revelation 21:27). Thus freedom does not require perpetual ability to sin. The distinction is between original mutable goodness and consummated confirmed goodness. **Residual:** No inference from mutability alone to inevitable sin is warranted.

**O7. Is divine-nature ethics circular?**  
**Response:** The proposed moral ontology identifies God's eternal triune character as the ground of goodness (1 John 4:8; John 17:24), with commands as its creature-facing expression. **Residual:** Demonstrate why this identification has explanatory content, and account for justice, holiness, and particular commands without reducing goodness to an undefined use of *love*.

**O8. Does a global redemptive purpose answer the evidential PoE?**  
**Response:** Comprehensive wise and holy providence excludes actually gratuitous suffering within the model's premises (§6.12), while Romans 8:28–30 gives positive assurance of providential good for the called. The particular reasons for the unequal distribution and scale of suffering may remain undisclosed (§§6.9–6.10). **Residual:** The evidential problem remains open and must be treated without instrumentalizing victims.

**O9. Is the Fall itself morally good because ordained?**  
**Response:** No. Genesis 50:20 preserves the evil moral intention of the perpetrators while affirming God's good intention. Ordination is not moral approbation. **Residual:** Clarify the relation between permissive and efficacious divine willing across Reformed primary sources.

**O10. Does this imply God needed creation or evil for His glory?**  
**Response:** God's intrinsic perfection and eternal triune communion are independent of creation (John 17:5, 24). The decree concerns the historical manifestation of glory through created relations, judgment, and redemption. **Residual:** Articulate the distinction between intrinsic glory and manifested glory without implying deficiency in God.

### 6.9 The epistemic boundary of divine counsel (Deuteronomy 29:29)

Deuteronomy 29:29 distinguishes the secret things belonging to the LORD from the revealed things entrusted to His people for faithful obedience. In context, the verse concerns covenant accountability and the limits of Israel's access to God's counsel. Its application to providence is a **theological extension** of that revealed/secret distinction, not a claim that the verse directly answers the philosophical Problem of Evil.

**Revealed premises:** God elects in Christ (Ephesians 1:4–5); God ordains the crucifixion while holding its human agents culpable (Acts 2:23); God intends good through human evil (Genesis 50:20); and the redemptive purpose encompasses the manifestation of justice and mercy (Romans 9:22–23). These claims are available for exegesis, logical scrutiny, and systematic synthesis.

**Reserved particulars:** Scripture does not disclose an exhaustive explanation for why God decreed this precise history, this distribution and magnitude of suffering, or each apparently gratuitous event. The specific providential rationale for an individual tragedy must not be invented or confidently assigned without revelation.

**Epistemic rule:** A missing revealed explanation for a particular evil does not logically establish the absence of a morally sufficient divine reason. Neither does the appeal to secret counsel *independently demonstrate* that such a reason exists for every particular evil or defeat all evidential objections. Within the model, the claim of sufficient purpose rests on the additional premises of perfectly good, wise, comprehensive providence (§6.12). The evidential PoE remains an open assessment of comparative explanatory adequacy. Deuteronomy 29:29 constrains claims about what we know; it does not exempt revealed theological propositions from testing for coherence.

**Pastoral and moral limit:** The appeal to secret counsel must never excuse injustice, silence lament, minimize suffering, or replace duties of protection and mercy. The verse itself directs attention to the obligations that have been revealed.

### 6.10 The sufficiency of revealed character: warranted trust without exhaustive accounting

**Thesis:** God does not owe His creatures an exhaustive accounting of His eternal counsel. Nevertheless, His revelation supplies substantial grounds for trusting His righteousness, wisdom, faithfulness, and love even where the specific reasons for particular providences remain undisclosed.

Deuteronomy 29:29 assigns secret things to the LORD and revealed things to His people. Genesis 18:25 presents Abraham's appeal to the justice of the Judge of all the earth. Romans 5:8 and 8:32 ground Christian confidence in God's demonstrated love through the giving of His Son. Job 38–42 portrays a sufferer confronted with the limits of creaturely perspective and the reality of divine wisdom without a detailed disclosure of the heavenly circumstances described earlier in the book.

**Epistemic distinction:** Warranted trust can rest upon sufficiently strong evidence of a person's character without access to every reason for every action. In this theological application, the revealed character and acts of God furnish positive grounds for trust; the undisclosed rationale of a particular suffering is not thereby known. This is a claim of rational warrant within a Christian revelatory framework, not a proof that every interlocutor must find the available evidence persuasive.

**Argument structure:**

- **R1 (revealed):** God presents Himself as just and trustworthy, and Scripture records concrete acts offered as evidence of His covenant faithfulness (Genesis 18:25; Romans 5:8; 8:32).
- **R2 (revealed):** God reserves some matters of His counsel from creatures (Deuteronomy 29:29; compare Job 38–42).
- **R3 (epistemological premise):** Knowledge of a trustworthy agent's character can warrant reliance amid incomplete knowledge of that agent's particular reasons.
- **Conclusion (conditional):** Given R1–R3 and the credibility of the relevant revelation, lack of an exhaustive explanation for an individual evil need not defeat warranted trust in divine providence.

**Limits and objections:** This principle must not become an immunity claim shielding all theological assertions from criticism. An objector may challenge the reliability of revelation, the adequacy of the positive evidence, or whether particular suffering is a defeater of trust. Those challenges require substantive engagement. Divine freedom from an obligation to provide an exhaustive accounting does not cancel His revealed commitment to justice, nor does it remove human obligations to lament, seek truth, oppose evil, and aid sufferers.

**Working formulation:** *God owes no exhaustive accounting of His sovereign counsel, yet He has revealed sufficient evidence of His character to ground rational trust. We may know enough to trust His goodness without claiming to know every reason for His providence.*

### 6.11 Romans 8:28–30 as the governing providential premise

**Textual claim:** Romans 8:28 states that *all things* (Greek **πάντα**, *panta*) work together for good for those who love God, who are called according to His purpose. The scope is comprehensive; the promised beneficiary is specifically defined. Verses 29–30 immediately identify the purpose's trajectory as conformity to the image of the Son and eventual glorification. The surrounding discussion includes suffering, groaning, weakness, and hope (Romans 8:17–27), so the assurance is not restricted to pleasant circumstances.

**Governing premise (RP):** God providentially orders all things toward the ultimate good of those called according to His purpose, a good whose consummation includes conformity to Christ and glorification (Romans 8:28–30). In the Reformed synthesis, this assurance coheres with God's comprehensive governance (Ephesians 1:11) and His good intention through culpable human evil (Genesis 50:20).

**Logical distinctions:**

1. **Universal scope of providential working:** No event lies beyond God's ability to govern it toward His promised end for His people.
2. **Specified covenantal beneficiary:** The promise is addressed to those who love God and are called according to His purpose; it should not be recast as an unconditional guarantee that every event produces the same redemptive outcome for every person.
3. **Teleological good:** The immediate context defines the promised good through conformity to Christ and glorification; it does not promise temporal comfort or the absence of loss.
4. **Moral nonidentity:** An event can be evil in creaturely intention and effect while being governed toward good by God; providential use does not make the evil act morally good.
5. **No event-by-event indispensability inference:** Comprehensive providential governance does not logically prove that each particular suffering was uniquely necessary as an instrument of the final end. Its specific role may remain within God's secret counsel (Deuteronomy 29:29).

**Implication for the evidential PoE:** Within the framework accepting apostolic revelation, Romans 8:28 supplies a *positive assurance* of comprehensive providential working rather than merely the abstract possibility of an unknown divine reason. This does not disclose the causal explanation for individual tragedies or compel assent from an interlocutor who disputes the credibility of Scripture. The comparative evidential argument must therefore defend the warrant for accepting the revealed premise while acknowledging unresolved questions of scale and distribution (§§6.4, 6.9–6.10).

**Exegetical obligation:** Examine the Greek syntax and textual variants of Romans 8:28, including the agency expressed by *synergei* and the referent of *panta*, before making stronger grammatical claims. The present theological conclusion relies on the clear contextual scope of the passage rather than a disputed syntactic reconstruction.

### 6.12 The epistemic asymmetry of divine wisdom and apparent gratuitousness

**Principle:** The limits of creaturely understanding do not set the limits of divine wisdom. First Corinthians 3:19 contrasts the wisdom of this world with God's judgment of it; Isaiah 55:8–9 contrasts God's thoughts and ways with human thoughts and ways. These texts do not license contradictions or invalidate sound reasoning. They caution against inferring that a reason is absent solely because a finite observer cannot identify it.

**Definitions:** *Apparently gratuitous suffering* is suffering for which an observer cannot discern a morally sufficient divine reason. *Actually gratuitous suffering* is suffering for which no morally sufficient reason exists. The former does not entail the latter.

**Framework-level conclusion:** Given comprehensive, wise, and holy providence (Ephesians 1:11), God's good intention through genuinely evil events (Genesis 50:20), and the promise that all things work together for the good of those called according to His purpose (Romans 8:28–30), the model denies that any suffering in the ordained history is ultimately without a morally sufficient purpose. This denial is **conditional on the truth of the theological premises** and the further moral premise that God's perfectly good governance of suffering includes sufficient justification for permitting or ordaining it. The assertion must not be presented as an independently demonstrated empirical fact.

**Epistemic argument:**

- **E1:** Human observers frequently lack access to the full causal and purposive context of an event; Deuteronomy 29:29 also distinguishes revealed matters from God's secret counsel.
- **E2:** Failure to identify a morally sufficient reason establishes actual gratuitousness only if the observer has adequate grounds to expect that such a reason would be discernible.
- **E3:** The scriptural account of divine wisdom (Isaiah 55:8–9; 1 Corinthians 3:19) challenges an unargued expectation of exhaustive human access to divine purposes.
- **Conditional conclusion:** The inference from *no reason is apparent to us* to *there is no sufficient reason* is unwarranted without a defended epistemic bridge.

**Remaining external challenge:** An objector can contest the truth or evidential warrant of the scriptural premises, argue for a reasonable expectation of discernible reasons in particular cases, or challenge whether the total evidence supports perfect goodness. The asymmetry principle does not automatically defeat such arguments, immunize the model from falsification, or imply that an event's divine purpose is available for confident human identification.

**Necessity qualification:** A morally sufficient purpose for every event does not entail that every event was uniquely indispensable to redemption. The actual decree includes the particulars of history, but instrumental indispensability is a stronger claim.

**Pastoral constraint:** No observer should use a presumed secret reason to minimize a victim's suffering, justify an aggressor, or excuse failures to oppose evil. The revealed duties of justice, mercy, and lament remain binding.

### 6.13 Formal consistency test and inferential boundaries

This section tests the argument's internal validity without claiming to have established every theological premise independently.

**Definitions:** Let **D** denote the actual eternal decree; **H** its realized history; **E(e)** that event *e* is an evil or suffering event within H; **G(e)** that God's governance of *e* has a morally sufficient purpose; **I(e)** that *e* is uniquely instrumentally indispensable to the final redemptive end; **K(h,e)** that human observer *h* knows the particular divine reason for *e*.

**Theological premises:**

- **T1 (revealed and synthesized):** God is perfectly good, holy, just, and wise in His governance.
- **T2 (revealed and synthesized):** Every event in H falls within God's comprehensive providential governance (Ephesians 1:11; compare Genesis 50:20).
- **T3 (explicit bridge premise):** Perfectly good and wise divine governance of an evil or suffering event entails a morally sufficient justification for its inclusion in H. T3 is a moral-theological commitment of the model; it is not simply the definition of sovereignty.
- **T4 (revealed, with specified beneficiary):** All things work together for the good of those called according to God's purpose, whose destiny is conformity to Christ and glorification (Romans 8:28–30).

**Derivation:** For any *e* in H, if E(e), then T1–T3 jointly entail G(e). Consequently, within the model, no E(e) is actually gratuitous. T4 positively specifies the promised redemptive good for the called; it must not be expanded into an identical promise of glorification to every human being. Genesis 50:20 provides an instance of contrasting divine and creaturely intentions; it is not, by itself, a universal quantification over all suffering.

**Non-entailments:** G(e) does not entail I(e); G(e) does not entail K(h,e). Likewise, the necessity of the actual history conditional upon D does not establish that D was metaphysically the only possible decree. The existence of actual suffering does not demonstrate that mutable creatures were bound to sin independently of D. The thesis therefore preserves both God's comprehensive purposiveness and the creature's limited access to the particular rationale.

**Consistency verdict:** The conjunction of original goodness, genuine creaturely evil, divine moral displeasure, comprehensive providence, and final consummation is logically satisfiable under the foregoing distinctions. This is an internal consistency finding, conditional on T1–T4. The argument's soundness additionally requires independent warrant for the premises and a defensible account of divine agency, moral justification, and secondary causes.

**Outstanding verification:** Check T3 against primary Reformed sources, examine Romans 8:28's Greek variants and immediate context, and develop an account of secondary causation that avoids transferring creaturely culpability to God. No claim of complete exegetical or historical validation is made here.

### 6.14 Primary confessional validation: decree, secondary causes, and the Fall

**Primary source:** *Westminster Confession of Faith* (1646/1647), chapters 3.1, 4.2, 5.1–5.4, 6.1, and 9.2, consulted in published confessional text at [Westminster Theological Seminary](https://www.wts.edu/wcf) and the [Reformed Presbyterian Church General Assembly transcription](https://www.rpcga.org/westminster-confession-of-1647/). References are to stable chapter and paragraph numbers, not unverified print pagination.

**Decree and creaturely responsibility (WCF 3.1):** The confession simultaneously affirms God's free and unchangeable ordination of all that occurs, denies that God is the author of sin, and maintains creaturely will and the contingency of second causes. The paper's distinction between divine ordination and moral approbation is thus explicitly confessional. The confession states the conjunction; a complete philosophical account of how it obtains remains a further task.

**First and second causes (WCF 5.1–5.3):** God governs every creature and action by wise and holy providence. Events are certain with respect to the divine decree while occurring through secondary causes according to their nature, whether necessary, free, or contingent. This supports the distinction between decretal certainty and coercion of the human will. The certainty of an event in the decree does not by itself make a creature's sinful intention God's sinful intention.

**The Fall and creaturely sinfulness (WCF 5.4; 6.1):** The confession expressly extends providence to the first Fall and subsequent sins. It describes divine bounding and ordering toward holy ends and assigns the sinfulness of the act to the creature. Its account exceeds a merely passive divine permission. This aligns with Genesis 50:20 and Acts 2:23, while requiring careful articulation of asymmetrical divine and creaturely intentions.

**Original mutability (WCF 4.2; 9.2):** The confession affirms original uprightness and the human capacity to transgress, while identifying original freedom as mutable. This corroborates the paper's differentiation between the very good initial state and confirmed consummation. Mutability alone does not logically entail an inevitable Fall.

**Validation judgment:** **HIGH** for agreement between the paper's core distinctions and these directly inspected confessional paragraphs. **MEDIUM** for the systematic explanation of their metaphysical compatibility. **UNCERTAIN** whether any one theory of secondary causation can be derived uniquely from the confession. The WCF supports the model's structural coherence; it does not expressly teach that this exact redemptive history was the uniquely possible route to perfected communion.

### 6.15 Romans 8:28 textual and contextual validation

**Primary text:** Romans 8:17–30, with particular attention to 8:28–30. The shorter Greek reading of 8:28 contains *panta synergei eis agathon* (πάντα συνεργεῖ εἰς ἀγαθόν); a documented textual variant adds *ho theos* (ὁ θεός) as explicit subject. Compare the Greek text reproduced in [OpenBible's Greek textual comparison](https://openbible.com/text/romans/8-28.htm). The [NET Bible textual note](https://classic.net.bible.org/passage.php/o/w/passage.php?passage=Rom+8%3A28%2C29) reports manuscript evidence and explains why both renderings, with God implied or explicitly supplied, are possible. The textual note is secondary editorial analysis; the Greek reading itself is the primary textual datum.

**Contextual findings:** Romans 8:17–27 discusses suffering, groaning, hope, and the Spirit's help in weakness. Verse 28 announces the comprehensive working of *all things* toward good **for those who love God, who are called according to His purpose**. Verses 29–30 immediately specify conformity to the Son and glorification. Accordingly, the paper is justified in treating the good as eschatological and Christological, without reducing it to temporal relief or claiming the same promise of glorification for all persons.

**Scope and inferential discipline:** The phrase *all things* has comprehensive force in the discourse; it does not by itself state that every event is individually indispensable to the outcome. God's providential agency is grounded in the passage's theological context and corroborating texts (Ephesians 1:11; Genesis 50:20), without making the disputed grammatical subject carry the entire argument.

**Validation judgment:** **HIGH** for the explicit beneficiaries, comprehensive expression, and conformity/glorification trajectory in Romans 8:28–30. **MEDIUM** for the model's synthesis of this promise with the stronger universal claim that no suffering anywhere in the ordained history is actually gratuitous; that inference additionally depends on T1–T3 (§6.13). The exact original Greek reading remains a textual-critical question and is not treated as resolved by this paper.

### 6.16 Augustine, Calvin, and the two states of created freedom

**Augustine, primary work in English translation:** *Enchiridion* (Handbook on Faith, Hope, and Love), §§10–14, 23, available in [Christian Classics Ethereal Library](https://ccel.org/ccel/augustine/enchiridion/enchiridion.chapter3.html) and [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1302.htm). In §10 Augustine distinguishes the Trinity's immutable goodness from created goods, which are genuinely good yet not supremely and unchangeably good. In §11 he argues that God can bring good from permitted evil; §§12–14 treat corruption as deprivation of created good. Section 23 identifies the falling away of a good but changeable creature as the cause of evil. **Finding:** Augustine strongly corroborates the conceptual distinction between originally good mutable creation and incorruptible goodness. He does not establish the stronger proposition that God was metaphysically unable to decree an immediately confirmed sinless creation.

**Calvin, primary work in English translation:** *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, I.15–18, particularly I.18.1–4, accessible via [CCEL's Institutes](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/institutes.toc.html). Calvin expressly discusses divine direction of wicked instruments while denying that their wickedness morally contaminates God. His treatment rejects an account in which providence is reduced to divine nonintervention. **Finding:** Calvin supports the model's asymmetry of divine purpose and culpable creaturely intention, but the exact metaphysics of concurrence requires further analysis; Calvin's position should not be conflated with every subsequent Reformed account.

**Westminster Confession, primary confessional text:** WCF 9.1–5, particularly 9.2 and 9.5 ([Westminster Theological Seminary](https://www.wts.edu/wcf)). Original human beings could will the good but could fall; in glory the will is perfectly and immutably free to good. **Finding:** Confirmed righteousness is compatible with genuine freedom in this confessional anthropology. The contrast is between different *states* of creaturely freedom, not between freedom and its absence.

**Christological boundary:** Christ's sinlessness (Hebrews 4:15) must not be modeled as mere human mutability that happened not to culminate in sin. His identity as the incarnate Son requires distinct Christological treatment. Neither original Adamic mutability nor glorified creaturely impeccability may be inferred uncritically from Christ's unique person.

**Assessment:** **HIGH** that these cited primary texts teach the stated contrasts; **MEDIUM** that the paper's full redemptive teleology is consonant with them; **UNCERTAIN** that any author independently establishes the unique necessity of this history. Historical consonance is evidence of continuity, not a deductive proof of the entire thesis.

### 6.17 Genesis 1:31 lexical validation and limits

**Primary Hebrew clause:** וְהִנֵּה־טוֹב מְאֹד (*we-hinnēh ṭôḇ meʾōḏ*), Genesis 1:31, in the Masoretic Hebrew text (see [Hebrew interlinear and morphology](https://biblevista.org/en/bible-study-tools/bible-verse-interlinear/genesis/1/)). *Ṭôḇ* is the adjective 'good'; *meʾōḏ* is an intensifier, 'very/exceedingly.' The evaluation encompasses everything God had made. The phrase asserts comprehensive divine approval of the created order in that context.

**Negative lexical inference:** The phrase does not itself encode *incorruptible*, *immutable*, or *eschatologically consummated*. Neither does the absence of a Hebrew adjective commonly translated 'perfect' demonstrate that the creation was defective. Distinctions involving *tāmîm* (whole/blameless) and *šālēm* (complete/whole) must be argued from their respective contexts rather than assumed as fixed metaphysical technical terms.

**Canonical control:** Genesis 2:18 identifies the man's aloneness as 'not good' before the Fall, in a narrative where God supplies its resolution. Ecclesiastes 7:29 speaks of humanity made upright; 1 Corinthians 15:42–49 distinguishes the first Adam and the glorified resurrection state. Thus the original-goodness/consummation distinction rests on broader canonical comparison, not on an etymological argument.

**Assessment:** **HIGH** for the Hebrew words and their basic grammatical relation in Genesis 1:31. **MEDIUM** for the canonical theological synthesis. **OPEN:** complete contextual lexical survey of *tāmîm* and *šālēm*, Genesis 6:6's verbal semantics, and Genesis 50:20's Hebrew construction. No comprehensive lexical study is claimed.

### 6.18 Genesis 6:6 and 50:20: Hebrew semantics and asymmetrical intention

**Genesis 6:6:** The Masoretic text uses וַיִּנָּחֶם (*wayyinnāḥem*, Niphal of נחם) concerning the LORD's response to making humanity, and וַיִּתְעַצֵּב (*wayyitʿaṣṣēḇ*, Hitpael of עצב) concerning grief in His heart. The ESV renders the first with *regretted* and the second with *grieved*. The first verb has a semantic range dependent on context, including regret, relenting, and consolation in different constructions; its meaning must not be mechanically equated with human ignorance of future events. The second conveys genuine grief in the narrative's presentation. Genesis 6:5–7 places both within God's holy response to pervasive human wickedness. **Finding:** The text supports genuine moral displeasure without explicitly deciding the metaphysical mechanics of divine affections or revising the comprehensive-decree passages.

**Genesis 50:20:** The Hebrew contrasts the brothers' evil design against Joseph with God's good design in the same matter. The verb חשב (*ḥāšaḇ*, to reckon/plan/intend) occurs with both human and divine subjects; the repeated verbal root supplies a particularly strong textual basis for *asymmetrical intention*. The clause ends with the preservation of many lives. **Finding:** This is a concrete case of a single historical complex being evil in creaturely intention and ordered to good by God. Its application to all history requires the wider providential premises of Ephesians 1:11 and Romans 8:28–30.

**Translation control:** ESV renderings *regretted/grieved* (Genesis 6:6) and *meant ... for evil / meant ... for good* (Genesis 50:20) are consistent with the respective Hebrew constructions. Other English translations may choose *was sorry* or *intended*; those choices do not independently resolve the theological question. A full comparative translation table and critical-apparatus inspection remain to be completed.

**Theological result:** God's regret at creaturely evil and His good intention in ordaining a history containing it are logically compatible if *regret* concerns the moral character of wickedness and *ordination* concerns God's comprehensive holy purpose. This is a systematic reconciliation of distinct biblical passages, rather than a lexical proof that Genesis 6:6 by itself teaches exhaustive predestination.

### 6.19 Created freedom, glorified impeccability, and Christ's unique person

**Adamic state:** Ecclesiastes 7:29 and WCF 9.2 support original uprightness with mutable created agency. The ability to rebel does not entail that rebellion is unavoidable independently of God's decree.

**Glorified state:** First Corinthians 15:42–49 and Revelation 21:27 support incorruptibility and exclusion of impurity. WCF 9.5 states that the glorified will is perfectly and immutably free to good. Therefore, the proposed perfected loving communion need not preserve the possibility of sin to preserve authentic creaturely freedom.

**Christological distinction:** Hebrews 4:15 presents the incarnate Son as tempted yet without sin; John 1:14 and Colossians 2:9 ground His full incarnation and divine identity. In classical Chalcedonian Christology, one divine person subsists in two natures without confusion or division. An argument for Christ's impeccability therefore turns on the identity and unity of the divine person and the proper account of His genuine human willing and temptation; it cannot simply be transferred to Adam or the redeemed. The claim that the incarnate Son *could not* sin is a further doctrinal inference, not a proposition explicitly phrased in Hebrews 4:15.

**Logical result:** Three claims are mutually consistent: Adam was upright and mutable; Christ is genuinely human and sinless, with a unique divine personal identity; glorified humans remain creatures yet are confirmed in righteousness. None entails that all originally mutable persons must sin, or that freedom requires an ongoing capacity for evil.

**Open source task:** Validate the precise Christological formulation against the Definition of Chalcedon and primary Reformed Christological texts before assigning HIGH confidence to the impeccability inference.

### 6.20 Modal analysis: certainty, necessity, and explanatory scope

Let **D** be the actual decree, **F** the Fall, **R** redemption *from sin*, and **C** perfected communion. Use □ for necessity and ◇ for possibility, without assuming that every conceivable world corresponds to a world God could decree.

**Decretal certainty:** If D includes F and D is infallibly fulfilled, then D → F. This conditional does not entail □F. The distinction is crucial: the certainty of what God has decreed does not establish that the Fall was logically or metaphysically necessary independently of that decree.

**Conceptual entailment:** R → (some actual sin). If the decree specifies the Son's saving work as redemption from actual sin, then that work presupposes sin within the history. This does not imply C → F in every possible history, nor does it imply that God required evil for His intrinsic perfection.

**Individual-event claim:** If e is included in D, then e occurs given D. This is distinct from claiming that removal of e while holding the final redemptive end fixed would be impossible. The latter is an indispensability counterfactual and requires additional justification.

**Moral-purpose claim:** T1–T3 (§6.13) entail a morally sufficient reason for the inclusion of each suffering event within the actual history. They do not identify that reason, prove unique optimality, or establish that all counterfactual histories are morally inferior.

**Verdict:** The phrase *regrettable but necessary* is logically defensible only with its explicit decretal qualifier. The stronger claims □F, □(F given C), and unique optimality remain unproved and are not required by the model.

### 6.22 Divine-nature ethics: communion, holiness, justice, and truth

**Ontological claim:** The moral archetype is the eternal character of the triune God. First John 4:8 identifies God as love; John 17:24 speaks of the Father's love for the Son before the foundation of the world. These texts support the pre-creational reality of divine love. They do not by themselves furnish an exhaustive philosophical definition of goodness.

**Holiness:** God's holiness entails His complete distinction from and opposition to moral evil (Isaiah 6:3; Habakkuk 1:13, read in its full context). In a communion-centered ontology, holiness guards the integrity of loving fellowship from corruption. It cannot be reduced to an emotional preference for harmonious relations.

**Justice:** The Judge of all the earth is expected to do what is just (Genesis 18:25); God's judgments are righteous (Psalm 89:14). Justice orders creaturely relationships according to truth and due moral responsibility. Love does not erase accountability, and judgment need not imply divine delight in suffering.

**Truth and faithfulness:** God is truthful and faithful (Numbers 23:19; Titus 1:2). Communion requires trustworthy self-disclosure and covenant faithfulness; deception destroys the conditions of rightly ordered relational trust.

**Righteous love:** First Corinthians 13:6 says love does not rejoice at wrongdoing but rejoices with the truth. This gives a particularly direct canonical connection among love, moral evaluation, and truth. God's love cannot be identified with indiscriminate approval of whatever creatures desire.

**Moral-ontological synthesis:** Divine loving communion is internally ordered by holiness, justice, truth, and faithfulness. The claim that these attributes are mutually coherent is biblically motivated; a complete metaphysical derivation of every moral obligation from triune relations remains an argumentative task. Commands express the character of the Lawgiver in the creaturely order, but the specific content of every positive command cannot be deduced solely from the abstract term *communion*.

**Euthyphro test:** The model rejects a moral standard independent of God and rejects arbitrary divine command. Its third alternative grounds goodness in God's essential nature. The residual challenge is explanatory: demonstrate why the essential character of God is appropriately called *good*, and distinguish essential moral truths from contingent covenantal instructions. Citing divine nature is a proposed ontological terminus, not a license to evade conceptual scrutiny.

**Assessment:** **HIGH** for the cited scriptural predications of love, holiness, justice, and truth; **MEDIUM** for their systematic integration into a communion-centered account; **OPEN** for a complete account of moral normativity and positive commands.

### 6.23 Chalcedonian boundary and the doctrine of impeccability

**Primary conciliar anchor:** The Definition of Chalcedon (451) confesses one and the same Christ, truly God and truly man, acknowledged in two natures without confusion, change, division, or separation. The council's doctrinal definition is the primary historical source; see the translated text in [Fordham's Internet Medieval Sourcebook](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/chalcedon.asp) as a mediated English rendering. Exact Greek/Latin critical-edition verification is still required before any claim about disputed wording.

**Scriptural controls:** John 1:14 teaches the Word's incarnation; Hebrews 4:15 states that Christ was tempted yet without sin; Philippians 2:6–8 affirms His humiliation and obedience; Colossians 2:9 speaks of the fullness of deity dwelling bodily. These establish real incarnation and sinlessness. The stronger claim of *impeccability* (that Christ could not sin) is a theological inference from the identity of the divine person and the unity of His person, not a direct lexical entailment of Hebrews 4:15.

**Agency distinction:** Christ's genuine human willing and temptation cannot be explained away to protect His deity. Neither may one divide Christ into independently acting personal subjects. A fully developed Reformed account must engage the doctrine of two wills and the relation between divine impeccability and genuinely human obedience.

**Implication for the paper:** Adam's mutable agency, Christ's unique incarnate obedience, and glorified human freedom occupy distinct theological categories. The compatibility of freedom with sinlessness is established at the level of the model's confessional commitments; it does not prove that Adam was originally impeccable or that the Fall was metaphysically inevitable.

**Assessment:** **HIGH** for the core conciliar distinction and direct scriptural claims; **MEDIUM** for the classical inference to impeccability; **OPEN** for the detailed Reformed treatment of two wills and temptation.

## 8. Provisional assessment

**HIGH (direct biblical text):** original creation declared very good; God purposefully governs human evil; redeemed humanity is destined for glorification.

**MEDIUM (theological synthesis):** original integrity and consummated perfection are distinct states within one divine purpose; some evil and judgment are necessary relative to the actual redemptive decree; the *pactum salutis* links pre-temporal election to the Son's mediatorial mission; and no suffering is actually gratuitous if the model's comprehensive wise and holy providence premises are true.

**UNCERTAIN (further argument needed):** unperfected free creatures must inevitably sin; the Fall was the only possible route to perfected loving communion; each particular evil was instrumentally indispensable; and the precise distribution of suffering can be explained from available revelation.

**Working claim:** The Fall was *regrettable but necessary* within the particular divine decree that culminates in the Son's glory as Judge and Redeemer. This claim is advanced as a theological thesis for rigorous exegetical and philosophical testing, not as a proven lexical entailment of Genesis 1:31.
