## Abstract and governing proposition

Humanity in God's Image (ST-2, Creation) teaches that human dignity is a received endowment, not an achievement and not a social grant. God made mankind in His own image and likeness (Genesis 1:26-27). The narrative substantiates the plate in five movements: the image as created (panels 1-4), its moral content (panel 5), its relational constitution (panel 6), its vocation (panel 7), its state after the Fall (panel 8), and its renewal and consummation in Christ (panel 9). Panel 10 summarizes. The volume stands between BWM-IS-05 (Creation: Very Good), whose panel 5 first states the image, and BWM-IS-07 (The Original Covenant and Human Calling), which takes up the commission covenantally.

## Biblical grounding

- Genesis 1:26-28 is the foundation text: the divine counsel ("Let Us make man in Our image, after Our likeness"), the creation of mankind male and female, and the blessing and commission that follow. The Hebrew pair is tselem (image) and demuth (likeness); the plate does not rest any claim on a distinction between the two words, and neither does this narrative.
- Genesis 2:7 gives the image its embodied form: formed of dust, animated by God's breath. Genesis 2:18 and 2:23-24 give its relational constitution: aloneness is the one pre-Fall "not good," supplied inside the creation order by the woman, received as bone and flesh, and sealed in one-flesh union.
- Psalm 8:5-6 states the image's royal dignity: made a little lower than the heavenly beings, crowned with glory and honor, set over the works of God's hands.
- Ecclesiastes 7:29 states the original moral posture directly: God made man upright (yashar). The moral content of the image, knowledge, righteousness, and holiness, is specified where the canon describes the image renewed: Ephesians 4:24 and Colossians 3:10. This is the traditional Reformed reading carried at WCF 4.2, and the treatise is explicit that those attributes rest on the wider canon, not on the word "good" in Genesis 1:31.
- Genesis 9:6 and James 3:9 are the retention texts: after the Fall, the image still grounds the protection of human life and the ethics of speech about other people. The image is therefore marred, not erased.
- Colossians 1:15 identifies Christ as the image of the invisible God; Colossians 3:10 and Ephesians 4:24 describe the renewal of the image in believers; 1 Corinthians 15:49 states the consummation: bearing the image of the man of heaven after bearing the image of the man of dust.

## Doctrinal derivation

Premises. (1) The image is conferred in creation, before any human act, obedience, or achievement (Genesis 1:27). (2) It belongs to humanity as male and female, not to one sex or to the individual in isolation (Genesis 1:27; 2:18). (3) Its moral content is read back from its renewal texts, the canon's own specification (Ephesians 4:24; Colossians 3:10). (4) It survives the Fall as the ground of dignity and accountability (Genesis 9:6; James 3:9). (5) It is perfectly borne by Christ and restored in Him (Colossians 1:15; 3:10).

Consequences. Human dignity does not scale with capacity, productivity, or recognition, because its ground is a divine conferral, not a performance. Dominion is representative rule under God, not ownership (Psalm 8:6 read with Genesis 1:28). Work belongs to the image's vocation from creation, so it cannot be a product of the curse (Genesis 2:15 precedes Genesis 3).

Synthesis, labeled as synthesis. That humanity images the triune archetype in its relational constitution is the treatise's framing synthesis (axiom I.2, Theological synthesis, MEDIUM), presupposed at that tag in treatise Part III sec. 5.3, not argued as a lexical entailment of Genesis 1:26. The plate's panel 6 carries it at that grade. The description of the image-bearer's dominion and garden work as priestly (panel 7 caption) rests on the work-and-keep verb pair and its sanctuary associations, and is likewise synthesis, not direct teaching.

Objections and limits. A purely functional reading (the image is only dominion) is too narrow for Ephesians 4:24 and Colossians 3:10, which specify moral and epistemic content. A purely structural reading (the image is a faculty list) underplays Genesis 2, where the image is expounded relationally. Bavinck's treatment of the image and Adam's state is registered in the treatise as NOT YET CONSULTED (Appendix C register entry 13); no originality claim is made for this volume's arrangement, and Bavinck is cited for nothing here.
